# Three Orders of Society

A scheme of three functions that hold up a Christian kingdom: *oratores* (those who pray), *laboratores* (those who work), and *bellatores* (those who fight). In [[concepts/institutes-of-polity]] it appears in *Be cynestole*.

## Key ideas

- Titterington prints Wulfstan’s Old English and Rabin’s English. A just throne stands on the three pillars. The clergy must serve God and pray for the whole people day and night. Workers must produce what the people live on. Fighters must defend the land with weapons.
- The Old English Boethius already has a brief form of the idea: three tools to rule with (*tol mid to ricsianne*), *gebedmen*, *fyrdmen*, and *weorcmen*. Crișan, now from the article and not only the abstract, says that version puts the king at the centre and leaves out the Christian frame of the Auxerre texts Powell compares. The king is the one who sets the three classes to work. Alfred’s own hand in the translation is still disputed. ^[ambiguous]
- Wulfstan’s paragraph is taken, nearly verbatim, from Ælfric’s letter to Sigeweard. He changes the order so that those who pray come first. That matches the weight *Polity* gives to bishops. Ælfric’s earlier list in the *Lives of Saints* is workers, then those who pray, then fighters, and it is aimed at priests who bear arms, not at the throne. The letter to Sigeweard is where the throne-legs image appears, with the fighters brought forward. Wulfstan takes over the warning that if one leg fails the other two fail with it. Unlike the Boethius translator, he does not make the king the assembler of the orders. The orders have to hold the throne up.
- Crișan places that revival in the Viking crisis of 991–1012, after Maldon and through the death of Ælfheah. Ælfric and Wulfstan use the scheme to say that all three orders have to support the king or the kingdom fails. The sequences differ. The claim of interdependence does not.
- Reinhard’s abstract ties the same scheme, as it sits in Wulfstan’s manuscripts, to the continental three orders and to the Peace of God. He calls the English result sub-Carolingian.

The three orders are functions, not the old kingdoms. A translator who meets *gebedmen*, *weorcmen*, and *wigmen* is in this paragraph, not in a list of tribes.
