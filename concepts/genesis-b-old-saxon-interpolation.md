# Genesis B and the Old Saxon interpolation

The headnote of the clip says *Genesis A* is an Old English verse adaptation of about the first half of biblical Genesis, "fused with a passage known today as Genesis B, translated and interpolated from the Old Saxon Genesis." That is the whole claim the source makes. The detail below is the standard account and is not in the clip. ^[inferred]

## What the passage is

- About lines 235-851 of the poem as printed, from the rebel angel's boast through the temptation of Adam and Eve and the Fall. The Old English of that stretch is a translation of part of a lost Old Saxon *Genesis*; a short piece of the Old Saxon original survives in a Vatican manuscript, which is how the relationship was recognised in 1875. ^[inferred]
- The scribe or compiler of the Junius manuscript set it into the Genesis A text where the narrative had reached the creation of Adam. The text before and after it does not know it: Genesis A has already told of the fall of the angels (*ærðon engla weard for oferhygde dwæl on gedwilde*), and Genesis B tells it again with a different Satan. ^[inferred]
- The difference is in Satan. In B he is an individual, a tragic lord who speaks at length (*Hwæt sceal ic winnan? ... Ic mæg wesan god swa he*), is bound in hell, and sends a messenger to deceive Eve. In A the fall is told briefly and from outside. ^[inferred]

## Language

The interpolated verse is Old English with Old Saxon forms and metre left in it; the match between the two Germanic languages is close enough that the translator could keep many words and phrases as they stood. See [[concepts/germanic-language-family]] for the relationship between Old English and Old Saxon, and [[concepts/old-english-dialects]] for the dialect colouring of the surrounding poem. ^[inferred]

## Interpretation

Because the passage was borrowed, readers who treat Satan's speech as an English poet's invention are wrong; readers who deny that the English translator shaped it are also doubtful. Its portrait of pride, *ofermod*, as a refusal to serve a lord is the same moral vocabulary as [[concepts/ofermod]] in *The Battle of Maldon*, though that word is more disputed there. ^[inferred]
