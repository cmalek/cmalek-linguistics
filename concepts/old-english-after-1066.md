# Old English after 1066

The chapter "Old English after 1066" in Godden and Lapidge, *The Cambridge Companion to Old English Literature* (2nd ed., 2013) argues that Old English kept a use for later writers, so "afterlife" is the wrong metaphor. James Fenton treats Anglo-Saxon poetry as somebody else's; Seamus Heaney calls *Beowulf* part of his voice-right. The chapter takes both as products of their moments, not as facts about the tradition. The alliterative line did die out, and the language did become unreadable without study. That does not settle whether later writers may still work in it.

The vault's period pages date the language to about 500–1100 and treat 1066 as the break into French elite culture. This chapter is the counter-narrative: continuity of copying, then deliberate reuse. ^[inferred]

## Later Middle Ages

A large share of surviving Old English, especially Benedictine-reform prose, is in manuscripts made after the Conquest. Accurate twelfth-century copies with little glossing imply that Old English was still read for about a century, and that scribes did not yet feel a hard break. Elaine Treharne reads this vernacular as Old English still current, on a line with the Exeter Book poems and Wulfstan's *Sermo Lupi*. One *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* manuscript at Peterborough continues to 1154. Ker lists twenty-seven manuscripts written after 1100. Toward the end of the twelfth century and the start of the thirteenth, copies start rewriting Old English into contemporary English: it was still used, but no longer transparent.

The Tremulous Hand of Worcester, an early thirteenth-century scribe, is the first reader we can watch learning the language. His left-sloping shake marks at least twenty Worcester manuscripts and worsens over his career. The one book entirely in his hand, Worcester Cathedral Library F. 174, is Ælfric's *Grammar and Glossary* with the English updated, plus four damaged verse folios (the Worcester Fragments: *The First Worcester Fragment*, also called the St Bede lament, and *The Soul's Address to the Body*). Most scholars think he copied the verse from a lost exemplar. Some have suggested he wrote the first fragment. ^[ambiguous] Christine Franzen shows his glosses grow more accurate: first Middle English, later Latin. Ælfric's grammar, meant to teach Latin through English, becomes his key to English.

The first fragment sets a time when Bede, Ælfric, and English-speaking bishops taught the people against a present when "another people" teaches them and the teachers perish with the folk. Its metre is not classical Old English (stress on weak words, alliteration on a fourth lift, few exact Sievers types). The chapter treats that as metrical change after inflectional levelling, not as failed imitation: long runs of one alliterating sound mark a rhetorical unit, and *lorþein* ("lore-thegn") looks like a new compound on *þegn*. Seth Lerer reads it as a political answer to English as a conquered language. The chapter calls the mixed style an early deliberate poetic Anglo-Saxonism.

*The Grave* and Lazamon's *Brut* are other medieval poems that draw on Old English tradition. The chapter then skips the rest of medieval Anglo-Saxonism and moves to the Renaissance.

## Renaissance and early modern

Anglo-Saxon studies is usually begun in the sixteenth century. After the Dissolution, reformers gathered cathedral manuscripts into private collections that later fed Oxford, Cambridge, and the British Library. Laurence Nowell signed the *Beowulf* manuscript. William Lambarde (the chapter spells him Lamberde) printed the first Old English texts. Matthew Parker built the largest post-Dissolution collection and backed John Foxe's 1571 Old English Gospels. The motive was not neutral: they wanted an early English church independent of Rome, and they valued the Alfredian and Benedictine translations because reformers wanted scripture in the vernacular. Allen Frantzen argues that editions were also "restored" toward that church history. Robert Cotton, Abraham Wheelock, and William Somner continue the work into the seventeenth century.

Very little Old English poetry was read as poetry. Prose, and poems embedded in prose (Cædmon's Hymn, the Chronicle poems), mattered more. Franciscus Junius edited the biblical poems of Junius 11 in 1655. Milton, in the *History of Britain*, retells *Brunanburh* and scolds the "Saxon Annalist" for extravagant metaphors. He hears the kennings and does not hear that the annal has switched into verse.

Wheelock's twenty-line poem in the 1641 Cambridge gift-book *Irenodia Cantabrigiensis* is composition in revived Old English, paired with a Hebrew version, for Charles I after a Scottish mission. He calls the language "Anglo- & Scoto-Saxonicé". The poem says Scotland conquered England without a fight, and James then Charles took its heel. It uses the dual *uncer* ("we two") for the two nations, then *we* where *þeod* means people rather than nation, so the Union is grammatically two and one. The verse is rhyming six-syllable couplets, not alliterative metre. William Retchford added an eight-syllable Old English poem to the same book. Christopher Hill documents more radical seventeenth-century uses of the Anglo-Saxon past; the chapter only points at them.

## Romantic, Victorian, modern

Humphry Wanley in 1705, in Hickes's *Thesaurus*, is the first published note of *Beowulf*, and he sees that it is verse though the manuscript writes it as prose. Percy (*Reliques*, 1765) and Warton (*History of English Poetry*, 1775) describe Old English poetry largely by analogy with Norse. Sharon Turner's *History of the Anglo-Saxons* (1799–1805) quotes poems, including *Beowulf*, widely enough that a novelist could fake the style and expect readers to recognize it.

Walter Scott's *Ivanhoe* (1819) gives Ulrica a "Saxon" death-song: strophic, abrupt, with the beasts of battle, kennings for eagle and fire, and Norse colouring ("maidens of Valhalla", "the race of Hengist") that the chapter treats as the antiquarian model, not as Old English. Ulrica is the pagan Saxon past that the novel kills so Saxon and Norman can become one England, an allegory of Britain after Jacobitism. The chapter notes the rhyme with Wheelock: Old English pressed into a union argument.

Nineteenth-century philology then made editions reliable enough for poets who were not trained in the language. Tennyson (1876) turns *Brunanburh* into short alliterative lines with a falling beat close to Sievers type A. Morris and A. J. Wyatt (1895) translate *Beowulf* in stress rhythm and almost only from native roots. William Barnes, the later Hopkins (self-taught Old English in 1882–3), and, by the chapter's account, Hardy's plain diction and even Larkin belong to that nativist line. The Larkin step is the chapter's extension, not a claim Larkin made. ^[inferred]

Ezra Pound's *Seafarer* (1911; reprinted in *Cathay*, 1915) is the direct modern route: he keeps falling rhythms, clashing stresses, and mixed patterns, and makes the speaker an artist against the "burgher". Fred Robinson showed that odd glosses such as *byrig* as "berries" follow the dictionaries Pound had (two words spelled that way). The first cantos reuse the mode. W. H. Auden, at Oxford 1926–8, is the first major poet to meet Old English on the syllabus; early poems allude to it, and *The Age of Anxiety* uses an alliterative line from Old and Middle English.

The chapter's further-reading list of later users includes J. R. R. Tolkien, David Jones, Borges, W. S. Graham, Edwin Morgan, Richard Wilbur, Geoffrey Hill, and others. Heaney's *Beowulf* (1999) widened access; he puts Irish words on some Old English ones (*burgum* as "bothies", *fengelad* as "keshes") and can be read against the Northern Ireland peace. *North* (1975) absorbs Old English technique. In *District and Circle* (2006), "Helmet" uses [[references/battle-of-maldon]] and Hrothgar's helmet-gift to answer 11 September 2001. Denise Levertov's "Caedmon" takes Bede's tongue-tied cowherd, not the Hymn, as a myth of a poet finding a voice. See [[entities/caedmon]].

## Conclusions

Jack Niles's question about *Beowulf* — what work a text did, not only what it meant — is the chapter's question for the tradition after 1066. Texts that find no reading or writing community can be called dead. This survey's claim is that Old English found work often enough, and more often in the last century, that "afterlife" undersells it.

## Open Questions

- When, exactly, the alliterative two-verse line dropped out of use. The chapter says the date is arguable.
- Whether the Tremulous Hand composed *The First Worcester Fragment* or only copied it.
- How the radical seventeenth-century uses Hill describes sit beside Wheelock's royal poem. The chapter flags the contrast and does not make it.
