# Early and Late Old English Writing

Early and late name stages of the written language, not two manuscripts. Working bounds, used only when a source does not give its own: early runs through the ninth century, including Alfredian West Saxon; late runs through the tenth and eleventh centuries, including the late West Saxon written standard and the late Northumbrian glosses. ^[inferred]

[[concepts/old-english-dialects]] stays the map: West Saxon, Kentish, and Anglian split into Mercian and Northumbrian. A late West Saxon form is a late West Saxon form until another dialect shows it.

## What the opened studies support

- **West Saxon spelling.** Early ⟨ie⟩ generally corresponds to late ⟨y⟩, and to ⟨i⟩ before palatals. [[sources/condorelli-2019-west-saxon-ie]] The two layers are not securely a parent and a child. ^[extracted]
- **Unstressed endings.** Ninth-century Kentish charters already weaken unstressed back vowels. By the eleventh century the suffix vowels largely fall together, and dative plural *-um* is no longer distinct from *-an*. [[sources/walters-2004-case-syncretism]] Wulfstan’s *Sermo Lupi* alternates *-um* and *-an*. [[sources/kranenburg-2019-dative]] ^[extracted]
- **Late Northumbrian verbs.** Final unstressed *-n* drops, so the subjunctive and the infinitive can look the same. [[sources/fernandez-cuesta-langmuir-2025-northumbrian-subjunctive]] Weak class II loses its *-i-* formative in the tenth-century glosses. [[sources/ramirez-perez-2023-weak-class-ii]] ^[extracted]
- ***sylf*.** It is optional beside a bare pronoun used reflexively. The dedicated pronoun + *self* form is dated to Middle English. [[sources/zimmermann-2012-old-english-self]] [[sources/vezzosi-2003-himself]] ^[extracted]

## What they do not support

A present plural subjunctive regularly spelled *-an*, an early West Saxon *-ie* replaced by late *-ige*, and a counted increase of *sylf* from Maldon to Wulfstan. Those leads are recorded in the [[synthesis/Research: Early and Late Old English Spelling and Inflection|review]]. Mercian cells for the same features were not filled from the texts opened there.

## Four strands

Mercian and Kentish belong in the history of the endings. A West Saxon study and a Northumbrian study cover the two strands with enough early and late writing for a full series. They leave out the other two dialects, and they leave out the ground under the Worcester books. [[references/den-hertog-2024-oe-dialects]] argues for two blocs, Anglian against a Saxo-Kentish group, and says up to ninety percent of what survives was written in West Saxon. That is a claim about subgrouping and about how much was copied. It is not a reason to drop Kentish, or to treat Mercian and Northumbrian as one morphology. ^[inferred]

The four records are not evenly deep.

- **West Saxon** has an early layer, in Alfred’s books, and a late layer, in Ælfric, Wulfstan, and the copies made to that standard. [[sources/lutz-1984-late-west-saxon-standard]] Late West Saxon is also what scribes wrote when the text in front of them was older or not West Saxon. ^[extracted]
- **Northumbrian** has an early layer in short poems and inscriptions, and a late layer in the tenth-century glosses: Lindisfarne, Owun’s part of Rushworth, and the Durham Ritual. Those glosses are where final *-n* drops and weak class II loses its *-i-*. [[sources/fernandez-cuesta-langmuir-2025-northumbrian-subjunctive]] [[sources/ramirez-perez-2023-weak-class-ii]] ^[extracted]
- **Mercian** is thinner, and it is not Northumbrian. The usual early witness is the Vespasian Psalter gloss. ^[inferred] The late one named in the class II study is Farman’s Rushworth gloss, set aside there because it is Mercian. ^[extracted] Back umlaut, as in *sceolan* beside *sculan*, is a Mercian habit. Worcester, where [[concepts/institutes-of-polity|Junius 121]] was copied, is Mercian country writing the late West Saxon standard.
- **Kentish** is thinner still, mostly charters and a few longer pieces. The ninth-century charters are the early evidence that unstressed back vowels were already weakening, before the eleventh-century West Saxon merger of *-um* and *-an*. [[sources/walters-2004-case-syncretism]] The *-an* spelling of the preterite plural subjunctive is the form Campbell treats as possibly Kentish. [[sources/armborst-1977-unstressed-endings]] ^[extracted]

Anglian remains the right cover term when a source, or a spelling cue, does not separate Mercian from Northumbrian. [[concepts/old-english-dialects]] uses it that way. For late verbs it hides the split. The two Anglian varieties share some older vowels against West Saxon. They do not share those late simplifications of the verb.

## Drivers

Some drivers act in every dialect. The dialects show them at different times. Initial stress weakens the endings. That shows up early in Kentish and generally by the eleventh century. [[sources/walters-2004-case-syncretism]] Analogy then reassigns endings that sound alike. That is the preterite plural subjunctive written *-on* or *-an*. [[sources/armborst-1977-unstressed-endings]] ^[extracted]

Some apparent endings are the dialect phonology of one grammatical cell. *sceal* against *scall*, and *sculan* against *sceolan*, are that kind of difference. The cell stays the same. The vowel is the dialect. ^[inferred]

Some of what a manuscript shows is the written standard laid over an older or local text. [[sources/lutz-1984-late-west-saxon-standard]] ^[extracted]

Some of the Northumbrian verbal evidence is a glossator choosing a form for a Latin text. [[sources/fernandez-cuesta-langmuir-2025-northumbrian-subjunctive]] That is a fact about the gloss. It is not automatically a fact about northern speech. ^[extracted]

A Norse contact driver for the northern loss of endings is a live hypothesis. The studies opened for the review did not establish it. [[sources/ramirez-perez-2023-weak-class-ii]] treats the class II loss as analogical, and as led by infrequent verbs. ^[extracted]

## How the work splits

The survey wants four strands. West Saxon and Northumbrian can each carry an early-to-late series. Mercian and Kentish stay in the design as shorter series, reported as thin where the manuscripts are thin, and not filled from the other two.

For *Polity*, the Mercian strand is the one that meets the late West Saxon standard inside Junius 121. The spelling mixture in that book is on [[concepts/institutes-of-polity]]. Northumbrian explains a different part of the language.
