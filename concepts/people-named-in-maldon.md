# People named in The Battle of Maldon

The names below are the ones the fragment actually speaks. Line numbers follow [Old English Aerobics](https://www.oldenglishaerobics.net/maldon.php). Griffith’s 2024 glossary of proper names summarizes each person and marks names that have historical evidence outside the poem ([[references/karasawa-2025-griffith-maldon]]). That marked list was not copied out in either review read here. The standard essay is M. A. L. Locherbie-Cameron, “The Men Named in the Poem,” in [[references/scragg-1991-maldon-essays|Scragg’s 1991 volume]], pp. 238–49. The chapter text is still unread. What follows under her name is what Shannon Miller’s open Trent thesis records, with her page numbers. See [[references/locherbie-cameron-1991-men-named]].

An unsigned essay on [WarHistory.org](https://warhistory.org/article/the-battle-of-maldon-i) (2018) makes the useful caution in plain terms: many of the names would fit Essex landholders of 991, and Old English names repeat so often that pinning a line to one charter-bearer is often misguided. The same essay treats Byrhtnoth, and Ælfwine as grandson of Ealdorman Ealhhelm, as the cases where the poem and the records meet.

## Named, and historical beyond the poem

- **Byrhtnoth, Byrhthelm’s son** (*Byrhtelmes bearn*, line 92). Ealdorman of Essex, killed at Maldon in August 991. The poem, the Chronicle, and the obits agree on the death. Full note: [[entities/byrhtnoth]].
- **Æthelred.** The king, alive and absent. Byrhtnoth calls the ground *Æþelredes eard, ealdres mines* (lines 53–54). See [[entities/aethelred-the-unready]].
- **Ealhhelm.** Ælfwine’s *ealda fæder*, a wise and prosperous ealdorman (lines 218–19). He is the historical ealdorman of Mercia, not a man on this field. Ælfwine says he himself is *on Myrcon miccles cynnes*.

## Her reported identifications

These are Miller’s footnotes to the unread chapter. Where she is reported as probable, or as offering two origins, the match stays open.

**Ælfwine, son of Ælfric** (lines 209–24). The poem gives the grandfather, Ealhhelm. At p. 241 she treats Ælfric Cild, Ealhhelm’s son-in-law, ealdorman from 983 and banished in 985 or 986, as a probable and uncertain father. ^[ambiguous]

**Offa**, *Gaddes mæg* (line 287; speeches at lines 230–94). At p. 246 he is likely the nephew of a Bishop Theodric. That episcopal match is her suggestion as Miller reports it. The poem’s own kinship is Gadd. ^[ambiguous]

**Byrhtwold** (lines 309–19). At p. 243 he is likely the *cniht* granted land at Donyland in Essex in the will of Æthelflæd, Byrhtnoth’s sister-in-law, between 975 and 991. ^[ambiguous]

**Maccus** (lines 74–83). At p. 239 the name is both Maccus/Maccos in Old Irish and Old Cornish, and a possible Norse *Magnus*. The Norse choice in Miller’s screenplay is his. ^[ambiguous]

**Æscferth, Ecglaf’s son** (lines 265–72). At pp. 239 and 242, Ecglaf could be an anglicized Old Norse Eilífr or Eglaf, and Æscferth the son of an Anglo-Danish family. Miller then assumes a hostage given to Byrhtnoth. That assumption is his. ^[ambiguous]

**Godric, Odda’s son**, with Godwine and Godwig (lines 185–97). At p. 245 they are hard to trace: Godric is a common name, and “their descent into obscurity [was] to be expected.” The other Godric, Æthelgar’s son, is a different man, as the fragment’s last line says.

**Ælfhelm Polga** is not named in the poem. At p. 241 she thinks he may be a relative of Æthelric, of the Godric who fights, and indirectly of Byrhtnoth. ^[ambiguous]

## Still open, beyond those footnotes

**Leofsunu** (lines 244–54) measures his shame by Sturmer, a real place in Essex. The place does not by itself identify the man.

**Wistan, Thurstan’s son** (lines 297–300) dies in the press, and the transcript’s patronymic at line 300 is disputed. [[references/insley-2026-wigelm|Insley]] emends *wigelines* to *Wīghelm* and reads Wistan as a Scandinavian who took that English name at baptism in Byrhtnoth’s household. Abstract only. ^[ambiguous]

**Edward the chamberlain** (*burþegn*, lines 117–21) and **Edward the tall** (*Eadweard se langa*, lines 273–79) are two appearances of the name Edward. [[references/griffith-2022-vengeance-of-offa|Griffith]] proceeds on the assumption that Edward the tall is not the chamberlain. The assumption is his. It matches the poem’s wording: one Edward is thanked as chamberlain early, and *se langa* speaks and dies later.

**Offa** speaks after Ælfwine, explains Godric’s flight, and dies thegnly beside his lord, having kept a *beot* that he and his ring-giver would ride home safe or die on the field (lines 230–94). The Bishop Theodric suggestion is above. Griffith’s chapter argues that the Viking he kills at line 286, *þone sælidan*, is the unnamed messenger of line 45, the man who drew Byrhtnoth into the promise of battle. The poem does not say that in so many words. The definite *þone*, and a metrical breach shared with line 45, are the evidence Griffith uses. See [[references/griffith-2022-vengeance-of-offa]].

**The two Godrics.** Godric, Odda’s son, flees with his brothers Godwine and Godwig on Byrhtnoth’s horse (lines 185–97). Godric, Æthelgar’s son, fights at the front until he falls. The fragment’s last line says he is a different man: *Næs þæt na se Godric þe ða guðe forbeah*. Her p. 245 remark on the fugitives is above.

## The rest of the English names, as the poem gives them

No charter or chronicle match was established for these in the sources read. They are listed so the fragment’s cast is in one place.

- An unnamed kinsman of Offa releases his hawk and steps to the fight (lines 5–10).
- **Eadric** means to serve his lord and keeps his *beot* while he can hold shield and sword (lines 11–16).
- **Wulfstan, Ceola’s son**, holds the ford with **Ælfere** and **Maccus** (lines 74–83). Her two origins for *Maccus* are above.
- **Wulfmær**, Byrhtnoth’s sister’s son, is cut down (lines 113–15).
- **Wulfmær the young, Wulfstan’s son**, draws the spear from Byrhtnoth and kills the man who threw it (lines 152–58).
- **Ælfnoth** and **Wulmær** die beside their lord (lines 182–84).
- **Dunnere**, a plain *ceorl*, shouts the vengeance maxim (lines 255–59).
- **Æscferth, Ecglaf’s son**, a Northumbrian hostage of a hardy kin, shoots while he can (lines 265–72).
- **Æthelric, Sibyrht’s brother** (lines 280–82).
- **Oswald** and **Eadwold**, brothers, encourage their kinsmen (lines 304–08).
- **Byrhtwold**, an old retainer, speaks the gnomic lines and vows to lie by his lord (lines 309–19). See [[concepts/heroic-speech-in-maldon]].

## People the records name, whom the poem does not

[[references/neubauer-2019-olaf-maldon|Neubauer]] warns against treating Óláfr Tryggvason as the poem’s Viking leader. Sveinn Forkbeard’s presence is widely doubted. Jóstein and Guðmund appear in later Latin accounts, including John of Worcester, and the WarHistory essay suggests those names may have been drawn from the 994 treaty rather than from this fight. The poem’s only Viking speaker is an unnamed messenger. Griffith’s argument that Offa later kills that same man is on [[references/griffith-2022-vengeance-of-offa]]. See [[entities/olaf-tryggvason]].

Byrhtnoth’s widow Ælfflæd, the Ely burial, and the embroidery sometimes compared with the Bayeux Tapestry belong to his life outside the fragment. They are noted on [[entities/byrhtnoth]], from the older vault page and from the contents of the 1991 essay volume.
