# St Brice's Day Massacre

On 13 November 1002, the feast of St Brice (Brictius), [[entities/aethelred-the-unready]] ordered the killing of Danish men in England. Contemporary evidence is two texts, and the scope, the death toll, and the effect on later events are all argued.

## What the sources say

- **ASC (C, D, E):** Æthelred was told the Danes meant to kill him and his councillors and take the kingdom, so he ordered all Danish men slain. [[references/postgrad-chronicles-st-brices-day]]
- **Frideswide charter (7 Dec 1004):** renews St Frideswide's, Oxford, after its church burned with Danes sheltering in it; calls the killing a "most just extermination" of an alien weed. No criticism of the king. One site misdates it to 1002; 1004 is the usual date.
- **Later accounts:** William of Jumièges (women and children), John of Worcester ("all Danish settlers"), Henry of Huntingdon (coordinated letters), and Malmesbury (Gunnhild, Swein's sister, killed with Pallig; Eadric Streona ordering it). Modern writers treat these as embellishment; Ann Williams calls Gunnhild a myth. [[references/wikipedia-st-brices-day-massacre]]

## Scope and motive

Most readers now take the target as mercenaries, raiding armies, and recent frontier settlers, not the settled Danelaw, where nothing records killing. Keynes and Howard read it as an answer to mercenary treachery; Yorke as a hard reply to Vikings; Lavelle as frontier-focused and fed by ethnic hatred and millenarian mood. Æthelred later gave land near Oxford to a Dane, Toti.

## Archaeology

- St John's College, Oxford (2008): 37 mostly young men, fleeing wounds, burning; radiocarbon 960–1020. [[references/archaeology-magazine-2013-vengeance-on-the-vikings]]
- Ridgeway Hill, Dorset (2009): 51–54 beheaded men, probably executed, dated 980–1030. Link to 1002 is possible, not proved.

## Wulfstan

He became archbishop of York in 1002, the year of the order. [[references/roach-2016-aethelred-massacre|Roach's 2016 essay]] accounts for the killing without naming him. [[references/savill-2021-st-brictius|Savill]] cites Wilcox and Roach's biography for a place among the prelates who planned it. The essay and those biography pages are still unread, and a later snippet has Wilcox unsure that Wulfstan took part in the execution. See [[synthesis/Research: St Brice's Day Massacre]]. ^[ambiguous]

## Consequences

Stenton read it as provoking Swein's 1003 return. The postgrad blog argues Swein wanted the kingdom anyway. Neither Scandinavian nor pre-Conquest English sources tie revenge to the invasions; that story comes from Norman and later writers. Roach and MacDonald, as reported on Wikipedia, see the suspicion it bred as feeding the divisions that let [[entities/cnut-the-great]] win. See [[concepts/danish-conquest-of-england]].
