# Old English

Old English (also called Anglo-Saxon, OE *Englisc*) is the West Germanic language spoken in England from roughly the mid-fifth century until the Norman Conquest and the twelfth-century shift into Middle English. It is the language of [[references/beowulf]], [[references/battle-of-maldon]], [[references/judith]], [[entities/caedmon|Cædmon's Hymn]], and the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*.

## Key Ideas

- After the Roman withdrawal, [[entities/anglo-saxons|Angles, Saxons, and Jutes]] (and Frisians) settled Britain speaking mutually intelligible West Germanic varieties. English as a distinct language is already taking shape in isolation by about 600.
- Four major dialects emerge: Northumbrian, Mercian, West Saxon, and Kentish. See [[concepts/old-english-dialects]] and [[concepts/anglo-saxon-kingdoms]]. UT *Old English Online* groups Mercian+Northumbrian as **Anglian** (three-way map). By the tenth century West Saxon is the dominant written standard, largely because [[entities/alfred-the-great]]'s Wessex survived Viking conquest. Family tree: [[concepts/germanic-language-family]]. Lesson path: [[skills/ut-eieol-old-english]].
- Literacy arrives with Augustine's mission (597) as part of [[concepts/christianity-in-anglo-saxon-england]]; it displaces [[concepts/anglo-saxon-paganism]] over the seventh century. Runes give way to the Roman alphabet plus *wynn*, thorn (þ), eth (ð), yogh (ȝ), and ash (æ). Latin loans at this stage are mostly ecclesiastical, with some domestic vocabulary.
- About 400 OE texts survive. The language is highly inflected (three genders, multiple cases, strong/weak verbs); word order is freer than in Modern English. See [[concepts/old-english-grammar]].
- Core Modern English function words and everyday vocabulary are overwhelmingly OE in origin, even though perhaps 85% of the attested OE lexicon later dropped out under Norse and Norman pressure. False friends include *wif* (any woman), *fugol* (any bird), *sona* (immediately). Slocum/Lehmann’s classroom stats (most-common-word overlap both ways) are the same point, not a second corpus.
- A sixth-century *sk* → *sh* change (*skield* → *shield*) means later *sk* words are typically Norse. Seventh-century [[concepts/germanic-sound-change|i-mutation]] yields pairs like *foot/feet* and *man/men*. ON **breaking** of *e* is why *gjalda* sits beside OE *geldan* ([[concepts/old-norse-grammar]]).
- Viking settlement and the [[concepts/danelaw]] add up to ~1,000 Norse loans (*they/them/their*, *take*, *sky*, *law*) and accelerate loss of inflections.
- Local tooling in this vault: [[projects/wyrdcraeft/wyrdcraeft]] generates inflected forms; [[projects/aenglisc-toolkit/aenglisc-toolkit]] is a Qt annotation editor. Those pages are session distillations, not linguistic sources. ^[inferred]
- The language did become hard to read without study, but a 2013 Companion chapter argues it did not stop being used: twelfth-century copies, the Peterborough Chronicle to 1154, and later poets from Wheelock to Heaney. See [[concepts/old-english-after-1066]]. The "about 1100" end-date above is the usual linguistic period, not that chapter's literary one. ^[ambiguous]

## Open Questions

- Exactly when English becomes a language rather than a German dialect is not recoverable from the sources.
- Composition date of *Beowulf* remains contested (eighth through early eleventh century).
