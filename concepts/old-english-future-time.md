# Future Time in Old English

Old English finite verbs inflect for two tenses, present and preterite. There is no future ending and no future auxiliary paradigm that grammars print beside those two. Future time is a use of the present, with a few verbs that can point the same way. The classroom form of that statement is already in the vault notes and in [[concepts/old-english-grammar]]: *hēo gāþ* can be “she goes,” “she is going,” or “she will go.”

## What the handbooks say

C. Alphonso Smith’s exercise grammar (1896) says Early West Saxon had no distinctive future, and that the present covered present and future, as Modern English “I go home tomorrow” still can ([[references/smith-1896-oe-grammar]]). Glasgow’s STELLA course says the same, and adds that *iċ wille gān* ordinarily means “I want to go.” Future *willan* “seems to have been used towards the end of the OE period.” If the verb is ‘be’, STELLA reaches for the *bēon* forms ([[references/stella-old-english-verb-phrase]]). Mitchell and Robinson’s “resolved tenses” (*habban*, *wesan/bēon*, *weorþan* plus a participle) are perfect and passive in that course, not a future tense.

[[references/ycoe-modal-and-tense-tags|YCOE]] records the same architecture in tags. A finite verb is present or past. *Willan*’s sense list includes “be about to, shall, will” inside the modal gloss. Nothing in the tagset is future.

## The verbs that get discussed

Four items recur once the two-tense fact is granted.

- **The present indicative**, sometimes with an adverb, is the ordinary translation of a Latin or Greek future. In the Anglo-Saxon Gospels, Cowper and colleagues find it in 84.8% of clauses they matched to a future in Greek, Latin, or the King James Version ([[references/cowper-2015-no-future-in-old-english]]).
- **The present subjunctive** also occurs with future time. In that gospel set it is 5.4% of the matched clauses, and they find no sign that subjunctive was the dedicated future mood.
- ***Willan* and *sculan* + bare infinitive** are the pre-modals that later become *will* and *shall*. YCOE glosses *sculan* as obligation and *willan* as willingness, habit, imminence, or future. STELLA and the Old English Online sheets tell beginners to translate *willan* as “want” or “intend” first.
- ***Bēon*** (the *b*-present of ‘be’) is preferred when a state is future, habitual, or generic. That split is [[concepts/beon-and-wesan]]. *Weorþan* plus a past participle is a passive that handbooks sometimes hear as future (Mitchell 1985 §755, as cited by Wischer). It did not become the English future auxiliary. German *werden* did ([[references/diewald-wischer-2013-futurity]]).

Wischer’s Helsinki sample shows why *willan* and *sculan* were the verbs that could grammaticalize. Most of their present-tense tokens already take a bare infinitive. *Bēon* and *weorþan* usually take a complement or a participle ([[references/wischer-2006-markers-of-futurity]]). Some Alfredian examples are hard to read as wish or obligation: plants that *wile weaxan*, doctrine that *wille fleon*. She treats those as prediction already in Old English, and quotes Denison (1993) that late Old English *will* and *shall* were “almost a future tense.”

## How far the grammaticalization had gone

The two best counts in this survey answer different questions.

Cowper’s denominator is every gospel clause that lines up with a Greek or Latin future or with King James *will/shall*. Modals are 2.1% of that set. The present does the work. By Purvey (ca. 1388) *shall* is 81.5%. By the King James Version a modal is 99.4%. On their analysis, Old English has no contrastive modality feature, so a present is simply non-past and can be predictive. Once modals spell out that feature, a bare present is non-modal and predictive futures need *shall* or *will*.

Wischer’s small Boethius count (25 expressions she had already judged “closest to a mere future”) is 11 *willan*, 5 present indicatives, 4 *bēon*, 3 *sculan*. That sample lives in a philosophical translation where she was looking for bleached prediction. Her own gospel slices are poor in *sculan*: one present-tense token in the West Saxon Gospels stretch, none in Lindisfarne. The gospel translators’ habit of rendering a Latin future with an Old English present is also what Brunner’s tradition reports, and what Cowper measured.

Both results can stand. There is no future inflection. The present is the usual future in a close biblical translation. In some prose, *willan* and *sculan* already lack a sensible volitional or obligational reading and behave syntactically like auxiliaries. They are not yet the obligatory future of Early Modern English. ^[inferred]

The 2013 comparison with Old High German, read from the authors’ manuscript, puts numbers on why the two languages split ([[references/diewald-wischer-2013-futurity]]). In matched samples of about 80,000 words, Old English *willan* is already an auxiliary 84% of the time and *sculan* 96% of the time. *Weorþan* (274 tokens) never takes an infinitive. Old High German *werdan* (682 tokens) does, twice, and 66% of its tokens are potential auxiliary uses, against 44% for Old English *weorþan* and 41% for *bēon*. They treat the become-verb as the cross-linguistically ordinary future source, and the English modal future as the path taken because *weorþan* was too infrequent and *bēon* already occupied the copula ground.

A further handbook tradition, from Streitberg (1891) onward, treated perfective *ge-* as a way of pushing an event forward in time. Wischer sets it aside: English lost the prefix system, and futures that grow out of perfective aspect are rare in Bybee’s survey.

## Open questions

- A prose count with Cowper’s method and Wischer’s verb inventory, outside the gospels and outside a pre-filtered “mere future” set, is not in these papers.
- Mitchell’s own section on tense, as opposed to the one paragraph on *weorþan* that later authors cite, was not read in this pass.
- The DOE entries for *willan* and *sculan* would show how the Toronto lexicographers divide wish, obligation, and prediction. They are not in the open web corpus used here.
