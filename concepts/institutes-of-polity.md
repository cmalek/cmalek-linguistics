# Institutes of Polity

A modern title for chapters in which [[entities/wulfstan-of-york]] sets out the duties of each rank in a Christian people. The manuscripts give rubrics (*Be cyninge*, *Be bisceopum*, and so on) and no title for the whole. It reads like a law code, it sounds like a sermon, and it is neither. Titterington’s label is estates literature. Passages are reused in the laws and in the *Sermo Lupi ad Anglos*.

## What a translator is looking at

Jost’s edition (1959) builds two stages. I Polity, placed in Æthelred’s reign, is Cambridge, Corpus Christi College 201, pp. 86–93. II Polity, placed under Cnut, is Oxford, Bodleian Library, Junius 121, which Jost thought was the fullest form. Cotton Nero A.i is the bridge, and the only copy Wulfstan annotated himself. Rabin (2020) still prints I and II. The Wikipedia life dates the first stage about 1008 and the revision about 1016. Those years are a compression of Jost.

Crișan (2024) drops I and II. He wants three manuscript versions: Corpus, Junius, and Nero. Titterington reports the same doubt. The chapter order in Junius 121, heavenly king first, is the order of that book, not a proven final intention of the author. Nero and Corpus start with the earthly king. Reinhard’s dissertation abstract says the work only makes sense inside the manuscripts, as one part of a reform collection, and that a new edition is still needed.

## Working manuscript

The translation uses Oxford, Bodleian Library, Junius 121, the long Worcester text that modern translations follow. Jost called this II Polity and treated it as Wulfstan’s final form. The Bodleian catalogue dates the book to about 1060–1072, during the episcopate of St Wulfstan of Worcester (1062–1095). That range includes the Norman Conquest. The sentences are the archbishop’s, from Æthelred’s reign and Cnut’s. The book is a generation later.

It interleaves *Polity* with *Episcopus*, the bishop’s daily work, the *Canons of Edgar*, and penitentials, under one run of chapter numbers (I–XLIII on fols. 9r–69v). Editors usually lift Jost’s chapters out and restore his order. The book’s own order starts with the heavenly king. Nero A.i and Corpus 201 start with the earthly king. Crișan treats that Junius order as the compiler’s arrangement, not as proof of a finished authorial edition.

No catalogue read for this page gives Junius 121 the siglum B. The letter B also marks Dunstan’s first biographer, and a recension of Wulfstan’s canon-law collection in Nero A.i. Neither is this book.

## What the chapters do

*Be cynestole* says a just throne stands on three pillars: those who pray, those who work, those who fight. The wording is Ælfric’s, reordered so that the prayers come first. The councillors’ chapter is really about bishops, who are to bring God’s law to the people and to restrain the lay magnates. A long Latin biblical close to the bishops’ section hands the interpretation back to the reader. The last chapter, on all Christian people, includes the author in “we,” and it shares its wording with the end of the *Sermo Lupi*.

The ranks are the offices of one kingdom: heavenly king, earthly king, throne, councillors, bishops, nobles, reeves, abbots, monks, nuns, priests, and all Christian people. They are not the seven kings of the [[concepts/anglo-saxon-kingdoms|Heptarchy]].

## Reuse and classification

Titterington's point, from the archived ROEP clip, is that *Polity* is hard to classify: the chapter divisions look like a law code, the style is Wulfstan's, but it is neither law nor homily, and none of the copies gives a purpose. Its best fit is estates literature. Unlike the *Sermo Lupi*, which harangues, it lays out an ideal Christian society rank by rank and names where the real one has slipped, so the better society is "a much-improved version" of Wulfstan's own rather than a distant dream. Wulfstan reuses his own work (and Ælfric's, including whole homilies) and keeps revising it: the closing exhortation to keep the baptismal promise is nearly the same as the end of the *Sermo*, with *hellewites* for *helle pites* (perhaps a p/w confusion in insular script) and no cry for God's help. The print history runs Wilkins 1721, Thorpe 1840, Jost's attribution to Wulfstan in 1918, Jost 1959, Rabin 2020. Nero A.i's twenty-seven bishops' chapters and Wulfstan's own annotations show where he cared most. See [[references/roep-titterington-institutes-of-polity]] and [[concepts/sermo-lupi-ad-anglos]].

## Open questions

- Which chapters inside Junius 121’s numbered series are Wulfstan’s *Polity*, and which were added by the Worcester compiler.
- Whether the heavenly-king opening is his arrangement or the book’s.
