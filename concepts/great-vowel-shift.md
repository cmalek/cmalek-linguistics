# Great Vowel Shift

A reorganization of the long stressed vowels, named by Otto Jespersen in 1909. On the usual dating it runs from late Middle English, around 1400, through Early Modern English, with some outcomes in the eighteenth century. People were speaking late Middle English when the conventionally dated shift began, and Early Modern English while it continued. Old English is the stage before 1150. Modern English is the stage that inherits the results.

The full argument, the contradictions, and the source limits are in [[synthesis/Research: The Great Vowel Shift]].

## What changed

The shift takes the long monophthongs. Short vowels, and unstressed vowels, are outside it. [[sources/harvard-chaucer-great-vowel-shift|Harvard’s Chaucer page]] makes the stress restriction explicit: Middle English *my* shifted, and the unstressed ending of *only* did not.

[[sources/schlueter-2012-emode-phonology|Schlüter]] states the traditional chain. Middle English /aː/, /ɛː/, /eː/, /ɔː/, and /oː/ each rose one step. /iː/ and /uː/ diphthongized. [[sources/oed-periods-and-great-vowel-shift|The OED]] illustrates the front series with *mite*, *meet*, and *mate*, and the back series with *mouth* and *moot*. Before the shift, those long letters had values near the continental ones.

| Middle English | Traditional movement | Later standard outcome | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| /iː/ | diphthong | /aɪ/ | *mite*, *bite* |
| /eː/ | raising | /iː/ | *meet*, *see* |
| /ɛː/ | raising, then a later merger in most words | /iː/, with a few /eɪ/ | *meat*, *sea*; *great*, *break*, *steak* |
| /aː/ | raising, then diphthongization | /eɪ/ | *mate*, *make*, *name* |
| /uː/ | diphthong | /aʊ/ in the south; a monophthong remains in the far north | *mouth*, *out* |
| /oː/ | raising | /uː/, later shortened in some words | *boot*; *good*, *blood* |
| /ɔː/ | raising, then diphthongization | /oʊ/, RP /əʊ/ | *boat* |

The intermediate quality of the new diphthongs is disputed. Schlüter reports both a centered /əɪ/, /əʊ/ reconstruction and Lass’s /ɛɪ/, /ɔʊ/. She dates modern standard British /aɪ/ and /aʊ/ to the eighteenth century, and /eɪ/ from Middle English /aː/ to around 1800. ^[ambiguous]

## When, and which English

OED3 sets Old English before 1150, Middle English at 1150–1500, and early modern English after 1500. [[sources/nevalainen-2017-early-modern-english|Nevalainen]] says Early Modern English is commonly 1500–1700, and that any such cut is conventional. She also records the *Cambridge History* window 1476–1776.

Against those period labels:

- Stenbrenden’s introduction says the Great Vowel Shift is generally held to have begun around 1400 and to have been completed about 1750. That starts in late Middle English and continues through Early Modern English into the eighteenth century.
- Schlüter puts the first signs at least in the fifteenth century, the first stage complete in the sixteenth, the *meet*/*meat* merger around 1700, and the wide modern diphthongs in the eighteenth.
- The Harvard teaching page starts the story in the twelfth century and puts the main effects in the fifteenth and early sixteenth.
- Stenbrenden’s book, as reviewed by Ikegashira, then denies that those later changes are a separate event from long-vowel raising and diphthongization going back about to the tenth or eleventh century.

So the classroom answer and the OED period labels agree on late Middle English into Early Modern English. The revisionist chronology pulls related long-vowel changes back to the Old English / Middle English boundary and refuses the single name. ^[ambiguous]

## Cause

There is no agreed cause. [[sources/prichard-2014-northern-gvs|Prichard]] says a century of work has not settled cause, chronology, or mechanism.

Three accounts of the order:

- **Drag chain.** Jespersen (1909): the high vowels diphthongized first and the lower long vowels rose into the gap. Krug (2003) defends this for the upper half and proposes a phonetic start in glide insertion between *I* and *am*, and *thou* and *art*. He says there is no direct evidence of that liaison.
- **Push chain.** Luick: the mid vowels rose first and forced the high vowels to diphthongize. Prichard (2014) reads northern Survey of English Dialects geography as support for that order. The famous northern fact is that Middle English *ū* stayed a monophthong where *ō* had already fronted, so nothing pushed *ū*.
- **No single chain.** Stockwell and Minkova (1988), as quoted by Prichard, call the shift “the linguist’s creation through hindsight.” Stenbrenden, as reported by Ikegashira, finds raising and diphthongization overlapping across centuries and says the phonetic cue has not been identified.

Push and drag are labels for an order of changes. Stenbrenden’s introduction says they do not explain why a vowel moved.

## Result

Standard English long vowels and diphthongs are the outcome of this reorganization plus a few later mergers. Schlüter is explicit that the chain itself kept the vowels distinct, and that *meet* and *meat* fell together only around 1700. Spelling still often shows the older distinction: *ee* in *meet*, *ea* in *meat*.

The OED’s spelling essay is the result most readers meet. Printers from the late fifteenth century were fixing London English. Between 1475 and about 1630, printed spelling regularized. Pronunciation kept moving, so the letters no longer match continental values for the long vowels. Some shifted vowels then shortened (*blood*, *good*). A few words never joined the *meat* merger (*great*, *break*, *steak*). Consonant losses such as the *k* of *knight* and the *gh* of *night* belong to the same early modern centuries. The OED lists them beside the vowel shift, as further reasons spelling and pronunciation diverged.

Northern and Scots-adjacent dialects do not show the southern path for every vowel. Retention of monophthongal Middle English *ū* in MOUTH words is the case Prichard builds on.

This is a different change from Old English i-mutation. *Foot*/*feet* and *man*/*men* come from a much earlier fronting before *i* or *j*. See [[concepts/germanic-sound-change]].

## Open Questions

- Whether the changes form one chain, two chains, or a long series of similar raisings and diphthongizations.
- Whether the mid vowels or the high vowels moved first, and what phonetic cue started either movement.
- How far north a unified shift was transmitted, as against diffused in pieces.
- The exact sixteenth-century quality of the new diphthongs.
