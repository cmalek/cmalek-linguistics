# Data at scale and visual interpretation

Chapter 10 considers scraped and second-source data, personal traces, crowdsourcing, sentiment analysis, social-network analysis, and combining sources. Collecting data is only part of the task: the designer must decide how to analyze it and what can responsibly be exposed.

## From representation to understanding

Data acquire meaning for a reader through perception and interpretation in context. Visual literacy affects how successfully someone reads a visualization. The data's origin, purpose, and representation therefore matter alongside the chart itself.

| Concern | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Collection | What trace is created, and is the person aware of it? |
| Combination | What becomes visible when datasets are joined? |
| Visualization | Can the intended audience understand the representation? |
| Disclosure | Can a supposedly aggregate display identify someone's activity? |

The chapter's building-energy example shows why displaying finer-grained occupancy or consumption information can make personal activity recognizable. Aggregation and anonymization labels should therefore not be treated as sufficient evidence that no individual can be recognized. ^[inferred]
