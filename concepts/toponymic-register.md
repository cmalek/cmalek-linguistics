# Toponymic Register and the [[concepts/old-english|Old English]] Lexicon

English place-names and charter bounds are the main evidence for Old English words and senses that continuous prose does not record. The concentration is practical: flora, fauna, landforms, and farming. [[entities/carole-hough|Hough]]’s 2010 lecture, [[sources/hough-2010-toponymicon-lexicon]], argues that this evidence is not a straightforward supplement to the dictionary.

## Two uses of a name-only form

The older account, which Hough says still explains a large share of the material, is that names were coined in speech. They therefore keep colloquial words for everyday surroundings that religious and literary texts ignore. Her examples of forms proposed from names include plant-names attested only there, and animal uses such as *wearg* “wolf,” *brūn* of a pig, *pohha*/*pocca* “fallow deer,” and *pur* “male lamb.” Gelling and Cole’s landscape study, as quoted by [[sources/banham-faith-2014-farms|Banham]] and by later tests of the hypothesis, treats the several Old English words for a hill or a valley as a precise system rather than a set of synonyms. Dictionary definitions that collapse them lose senses recoverable only by comparing the named places with the ground.

The revised account is that a naming system can maintain elements that were not ordinary lexemes. Hough’s example is *cumb* “short, broad valley.” It is common in names and bounds. The [[entities/dictionary-of-old-english]] does not show it in non-onomastic Old English. She suggests it was established in the toponymicon and entered the common lexicon only much later. On that analysis, putting every asterisked place-name element into the spoken lexicon overstates what Anglo-Saxon farmers said.

[[sources/roberts-kay-toe-distribution|Roberts and Kay]] already quarantine part of this material. In the [[entities/thesaurus-of-old-english]], Campbell’s place-name additions cluster under the physical world and the farm, and some are flagged **q**, dubious, because they are not found as independent words.

## What has been published

A 1995 database project planned a handlist of Old English words unattested outside place-names, expressly as a supplement to literary dictionaries. The working series that followed is *The Vocabulary of English Place-Names*. Checked in 2026, the published volumes run from *Á* through *Cock-pit*; later ranges were listed as in preparation. There is no finished handlist, in the sources read here, that one can set beside a 32,000-word dictionary and call the missing lifestyle vocabulary. ^[inferred]

Charter bounds are the dated core of the same material, from the seventh century to the eleventh, which is the period of this question. [[sources/banham-faith-2014-farms|Banham and Faith]] use them for crops and land use and still describe the farming lexicon as sparse. A bound can preserve a word without telling us whether a speaker used it outside the formula of a boundary.
