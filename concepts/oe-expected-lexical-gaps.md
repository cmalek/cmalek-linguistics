# Expected Gaps in the Old English Lexicon

A count near 32,000 Old English words is a count of attested dictionary forms. [[sources/kay-2012-oe-semantics-lexicon|Kay]] reports about 34,000 word forms in the [[entities/thesaurus-of-old-english]], and 50,700 meanings. Research that starts from Anglo-Saxon life between about 670 and 1100 has not published a list of the further words that life would lead one to expect. It has published something narrower: evidence that particular meaning classes are thinly or awkwardly recorded, and, in names and glosses, some forms the literary dictionaries miss. The master account is [[synthesis/Research: Expected Gaps in the Old English Lexicon]]. Counts and estimators already filed from a chat note are in [[concepts/oe-lexicon-size-and-gaps]].

## What absence can mean

Four situations are regularly distinguished in this literature.

1. **The writings that survive do not sample ordinary speech.** [[sources/mohlig-falke-2015-doec|Möhlig-Falke]] says the [[entities/dictionary-of-old-english-corpus]] contains the surviving texts and still lacks spontaneous, colloquial, everyday language, because those text types were not preserved. Most prose is translated from Latin.
2. **A thin thesaurus category records the corpus, not the society.** [[sources/kay-alexander-2015-thesauruses|Kay and Alexander]] use terms of endearment as the example. The same authors treat the richness of weapons and warriors as a fact about literature that has come down.
3. **A form is attested only as a name, a bound, or a gloss.** That can be a colloquial word the prose never uses, a learned equivalent coined for a Latin lemma, or an element that lived in the naming system. [[concepts/toponymic-register]] is the argument that these three are not the same.
4. **The referent was not part of the period.** [[sources/biggam-2013-plant-name-studies|Biggam]] notes that *tulip* and *potato* are absent because the plants were not in England. An expected lexicon has to be built from the 670–1100 repertoire.

## Meaning classes the scholarship treats as thin

- **Practical farming.** [[sources/banham-faith-2014-farms|Banham and Faith]]: writers show little interest in food production; agricultural vocabulary is sparsely attested and often obscure; *Gerefa* borrows otherwise unknown glossary words; Ine’s laws, the code most concerned with farming, still leave most agrarian work unlegislated.
- **Vernacular plant-names.** Biggam: manuscripts exclude the regional names of illiterate country-dwellers, and some English glosses are literal translations a villager may not have used. Her teaching unit says only a small proportion of Old English plant-names is likely to have been recorded. Herbal texts also omit parts of practice, such as setting a fracture, that archaeology shows were done.
- **Non-military dress, as description.** [[sources/owen-crocker-1979-wynflaed|Owen-Crocker]]: wills rarely itemize clothing; some documented garment-names occur only in glossaries or translations and may never have been in common use. The clothing vocabulary is compiled mainly from late texts, while earlier dress is reconstructed from graves and art.
- **Metalworking as workshop speech.** [[sources/coatsworth-pinder-2002-metalworking|Coatsworth and Pinder]]: terms for some materials, tools, and workers exist, mostly late, and often only in poetry or glosses. No Old English text is a process description. Verbs for heating and melting are attested for food and medicine.
- **Danelaw everyday loans.** Kay: Scandinavian words entered through spoken contact and often appear in writing only in early Middle English. See [[concepts/danelaw]].

## Meaning classes that are recorded, and filtered

[[sources/roberts-kay-toe-distribution|Roberts and Kay]] flag gloss-only, poetic, rare, and dubious forms so that a reader does not mistake a hapax or a glossary equivalent for general currency. Poetic vocabulary clusters in the sea, ships, courage, terror, rulers, weapons, and treasure. Law, possession, and much of the vocabulary of action are prosaic. [[sources/kay-alexander-2015-thesauruses|Kay and Alexander]] describe thematic glossaries, from the eighth century, whose favourite topics are the body, precious stones, medicinal herbs, animals, birds, fish, and plants. Those lists teach Latin. They are the nearest Anglo-Saxon inventory of meaning classes, and they are a schoolroom inventory.

Place-names and charter bounds are the other large supplement, especially for landscape, wild plants and animals, and farming. They are filed under [[concepts/toponymic-register]].
