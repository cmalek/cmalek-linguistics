# Old English g-Palatalization (ġ vs. g)

The dot in ġ is a modern editorial convention. Manuscripts write plain `g` for both sounds, so a reader has to infer the value from the word. History and mechanism are in [[synthesis/Research: Old English G-Palatalization]]; this page is the working reference.

## The sounds

| Letter | Sound | Where |
|---|---|---|
| ġ | [j] (as in *yes*) | most palatal contexts |
| ġ | [dʒ] (as in *judge*) | after *n* (*sengan*), or from old geminate *-gj-* (*bryċġ*); never word-initial |
| g | [ɡ] stop | word-initial before back vowels, after *n*, in geminate *gg* |
| g | [ɣ] voiced velar fricative | between vowels or after *l*/*r*, next to back vowels (*boga*, *dragan*) |

## Practical rules (community consensus)

1. **Palatal before or after a front vowel that was already front** (*i, ī, e, ē, æ, ǣ*, and diphthongs *ea, eo, ie*): *ġiefan*, *ġē*, *ġēar*, *ġeong*, *dæġ*, *weġ*, *mǣġ*, *þeġen*.
2. **Palatal next to *i* or *j* in the same syllable or the following one:** *byrġ*, *fīftiġ*, and the *-iġ* adjective suffix (*hāliġ*, read [ˈhɑːlij]).
3. **Velar before a back vowel**, even after a front vowel: *dagas*, *wegas*, *dagung*. The back vowel in the next syllable blocks the change.
4. **Velar next to a consonant** (other than a palatal-triggering *j*): *glæd*, *grēne*, *gnæt*, *lang*, *singan*. After *n* it is [ɡ], except where *-gj-* gave [dʒ] (*sengan*, *mengan*, *lenġ*).
5. **Spelling diacritics:** an inserted *e* or *i* can mark palatal value before a back vowel (*sēċean*, *strenġeo*, *drenċium*), and *ġeo/ġea/ġie* after an initial ġ signal palatal diphthongization, not a velar *g*.
6. **Initial *g* before a back vowel is velar, except where a palatal glide was inherited:** *ġeong* "young", *ġeoc* "yoke". The *eo* diphthong is the cue.
7. **Before a consonant a palatal reverts to velar after syncope:** *sēċiþ* → *sēcþ*, *senġiþ* → *sengþ*.

## Stop list: looks palatal, is velar

**Group A: front vowel created by i-mutation after palatalization had finished.** The velar was not next to a front vowel when the rule ran.

| Word | Gloss | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *gēs* | geese | pl. of *gōs*; *ē* is from umlauted *ō*. Contrast *ġē* "ye". |
| *gylden* | golden | from *gulþīn*; IPA /ˈɡyl.den/ |
| *gyldan* | to gild | *gold* + umlaut |
| *gyrdan* | to gird | from *\*gurdijaną*; velar despite the *j* |
| *gyrdel* | girdle | from *\*gurdilaz* |
| *gylt* | guilt | origin uncertain; /ɡylt/ in Wiktionary |
| *cyning*, *cyn(n)*, *cemban* | king, kin, to comb | the same pattern with *c* |

**Group B: a back vowel stood in the following syllable at the time of the change.**

*dagas*, *wegas*, *dagung*, *galan* "to sing" (a-restoration had restored the back vowel), *frogga* "frog" (geminate), *fealgian*, *swīgian* (probably velar), *singan*, *sangere*. The unstressed *-e-* words *(ġe)flogen*, *āgen*, *wōgere*, *folgere*, *bolgen*, *swolgen* used to sit in this group as a block. Hogg §7.16 n2 splits them: a fricative before that *e* can palatalize, a stop does not. See the caveats.

**Group C: later English velars beside a palatal Old English word.** Hogg's own Norse list, in [[references/hogg-1992-palatalization|§7.42]], is *birk*, *brig*, *rig*, *steek*, *weg*, *carve*, and *skin*. He does not name *give*, *get*, *gift*, or *guest* there. §7.15 n4 does name *gate* < *ġeat* as Scandinavian influence.

| OE | Modern English | What the sources checked actually say |
|---|---|---|
| *ġiefan* | give | Still the received Norse *gefa* account. Hogg's sections read here do not discuss it. |
| *ġietan* | get | Same. Not in the sections read. |
| *ġeat* | gate "door" | Hogg §7.15 n4: Scandinavian influence on palatal *ġeat*. Dialect *yate* / *yett* keeps the palatal. |
| *\*gatwōn* | gate "road" | A different word (Old Norse *gata*). Gersum and Versloot, citing Hogg and Fulk §3.116, say the oblique stem *\*gat-* would have stayed velar inside West Germanic, so a missing palatal is weak evidence of a loan. |
| *ġift* | gift "present" | Old English *gift* is recorded as bride-price. Etymonline takes the general sense, from the mid-13th century, from Old Norse *gift*, *gipt*. The ordinary Old English word for a present was *ġiefu*. |
| *ġiest* | guest | Gersum: Old Norse *gestr* supplies the unpalatalized /g/. West Saxon *giest* would have given a *y-* form. Middle English *gest* can be a Scandinavianized native word or a loan; in Anglian the outcome looks the same. |

These are not Old English exceptions. The Old English word, where it is *ġeat*, *ġiest*, or *ġiefu*, is palatal. The modern velar is a later history.

**Group D: analogy and levelling.** *seek* (from *sēcþ*, not *sēċan*), and Northumbrian forms, where palatalization is weaker (Scots *kirk* ~ *church*, *brig* ~ *bridge*).

## The reverse list: looks velar, is palatal

*ġeong*, *ġeoc* (inherited *j* before a back vowel, so palatal even though the following vowel is back); *ġeard*, *ġiefu*, *ġieldan*, *ġiellan* (front-vowel diphthongs); the prefix *ġe-*; *bryċġ*, *sengan*, *mengan* ([dʒ] from *-gj-*).

## Dialects

- West Saxon: palatal diphthongization is regular, so *ġe/ġæ* is rare in stressed syllables (*ġiefan*, *ġeaf*).
- Anglian and Mercian: more *ġæ/ġe* spellings (*ġæf*, *ġet*, *ġeldan*). Mercian *geatu* beside West Saxon *gatu*.
- Northumbrian: markedly less palatalization, probably Scandinavian influence.

## Caveats

- Hogg 1992 §§7.15–17 are now the working rule. See [[references/hogg-1992-palatalization]]. Before an unstressed *e*, he uses the same rule as before *æ*: fricatives palatalize, stops do not (§7.16 n2). *Hnīge* and *stīge* fit the fricative case. A stop in *folgere* stays velar. *Āgen* depends on whether its following vowel was already front when the rule ran. ^[ambiguous]
- Campbell 1959 §§426–430 were not opened. Hogg reports §§428–9 and 435 as the view that stops palatalized like fricatives and then reverted.
- Hogg 1979 is cited inside the grammar (pp. 93–96 and 103–4) and was not read as an article. Unpaywall has no open copy.
- Vowel + ġ + consonant is probably not depalatalized, but this is not settled.
