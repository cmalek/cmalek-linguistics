# Old Norse Grammar

Krause & Slocum lessons 1–2. Distill the rules, not the interlinear glossaries. Compare [[concepts/old-english-grammar]].

## Key Ideas

**Two pronunciations.** Literary ON from ~1100 uses a Latin alphabet via English. **Classical** (c. 1150–1350): length is quantity; þ vs ð as thin/this; f voiceless initially, voiced medially; *y* front rounded. **Classroom habit:** read texts with **Modern Icelandic** (á as *ow*, preaspirated *kk/pp/tt*, *ll* often voiceless *tl*, epenthetic *u* in *maður* < *maðr*). Keep the two systems apart.

**Alphabet extras:** ǫ (short *naught*); editors’ ǫ́ for a lost long; diphthongs *au, ei, ey*. *q/w* East Norse ≈ *k/v*.

**Sound rules (lesson 2):** syncope (*himin+i* → *himni*); contraction of adjacent vowels; **breaking** of PN *e* → *ja*, and *jǫ* before *u* (*gjalda* vs OE *geldan*; blocked after *v, l, r*); raising *e>i* in some class-5 *j*-presents (*biðja*); lengthening in stressed open finals; shortening before clusters (*góðr* / *gott*). Initial *j* dropped (ON *ungr* vs OE *geong*); initial *w* dropped before *l/r* (*líta* vs OE *wlitan*).

**Nouns:** masculine *i*-stems *staðr, gestr, bekkr*; feminine *i*-stems mixed with old *ō*-stems (*nauð, þǫkk, hǫll*). All *u*-stems masculine: *skjǫldr* shows breaking except D sg / N pl (*skildi, skildir*), which take *i*-umlaut.

**Pronouns:** 1/2 person have **dual** (*vit/it*, *okkr/ykkr*). 3rd person is suppletive: *hann/hon* sg vs *þeir/þær/þau* pl — the English *they* pattern. Reflexive *sik/sín/sér* for all genders/numbers; enclitic *-sk* becomes the **middle** marker.

**Weak verbs:** three classes (*krefja, kalla, vaka*); dental preterite *-ð/-d/-t*. Principal parts: infinitive, 3sg past, participle. Past subjunctive often *i*-umlauts a back root. *heyra* is class I or III depending on the book. ^[ambiguous]

**Particles:** *ok, en, eða, né*; *er* as all-purpose relative after demonstratives (*þá er* “when”). Verb + preposition often changes meaning without the preposition taking an object (*sœkja at* “attack”).
