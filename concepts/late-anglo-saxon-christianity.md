# Late Anglo-Saxon Christianity

The church [[entities/wulfstan-of-york]] was writing for, roughly 996–1023, and the church that copied his work at Worcester about 1060–1072. It is the Latin church in England, in communion with Rome, a century and a half after the conversion narrated in [[concepts/christianity-in-anglo-saxon-england]]. A twenty-first-century Catholic vocabulary is a poor glossary for it. So is a Protestant one. The useful comparison is doctrine by doctrine, with the century of the definition kept in view.

## What already matches later Catholic teaching

- His London sermons and the next block of homilies teach Antichrist, judgment, Christ’s life and passion, the *Pater Noster*, the Apostles’ Creed, baptism, and the date and manner of Easter Mass. The Mass is the centre of the public penance he orders in 1009.
- The eucharist is Christ’s body and blood, confected by a priest, and the usual Old English name for the consecrated element is *hūsel*. The Mass is *mæsse*. People are taught to attend it. Wulfstan’s 1009 edict, during Thorkell’s raids, orders three days of fasting, barefoot processions, a penny from each hide, and Mass each day.
- Baptism (*fulwiht*) is the entry into the people. Penance is *dædbōt*, imposed by a confessor (*scrift*). Payment for the soul at death is *sāwlsceat* (soul-scot). Tithe is *tēoðung*. A payment to Rome is *rōmfeoh* or *rōmpenig*, Peter’s pence.
- Mary is honored. A feast of her conception reached England in the eleventh century. That feast is not the dogma of the Immaculate Conception.
- Saints, relics, and translation of bodies are central to the tenth-century reform. Prayer and gifts for the dead are a normal part of lay religion: nobles endow monasteries so that monks will pray for them.
- The east–west breach dated to 1054 is later than Wulfstan’s death. England stays with the Latin rite.

## Definitions and institutions that are later

Set these beside the text before importing them.

| Later teaching or custom | When it is fixed in the sources read here | What to do with Wulfstan’s English |
|---|---|---|
| Bread and wine “changed in substance” into Christ’s body and blood | Fourth Lateran Council, canon 1, 1215 | He believes the *hūsel* is Christ’s body. He does not have this metaphysical sentence. “Spiritual” in Ælfric’s Easter homily, which Wulfstan knew, is not a denial of that body. |
| A closed list of seven sacraments | Lombard’s *Sentences*; listed at Florence (1439) and Trent | Peter Damian, a contemporary of the next generation, counted eleven, including the ordination of kings. Wulfstan’s pastoral list is creed, prayer, baptism, Mass, penance, tithe, feast, and rank. |
| Confession to one’s own priest at least yearly, and communion at least at Easter | Lateran IV, canon 21, 1215 | *Scrift* and *dædbōt* are real. The annual legal minimum is not yet this canon. |
| Purgatory as a defined condition the living can help | Second Council of Lyon, 1274. The councils refuse to call it a place of fire. | Soul-scot, Masses, and alms for the dead are practiced. His sermons push the Last Judgment and the pains of hell harder than a stay in purgatory. |
| Immaculate Conception as dogma | 8 December 1854, *Ineffabilis Deus* | Marian devotion, and perhaps a conception feast somewhere in eleventh-century England, is not this definition. |
| Papal infallibility | Called a Roman novelty by Patriarch Anthimos in 1895 | Wulfstan writes to popes and receives papal letters. The English reform of the tenth century ran on the king, with little papal direction. |
| Universal clerical celibacy as an invalidating law | Lateran II (1139) declares marriages after ordination non-existent, on the Vatican survey of that legislation. Lateran IV (1215) still knows regions where clerics have not renounced marriage. | Reformers condemn married priests. Wulfstan repeats Dunstan: a married cleric on trial should be tried as a layman. Many priests were married anyway. Do not translate every *prēost* as a celibate parish priest, and do not treat a wife as unremarkable. |
| Blessing of the ordeal forbidden to clergy | Lateran IV, canon 18, 1215 | Ordeal is still part of English law in his codes. |
| “Roman Catholic” as the name of one denomination among others | After the Reformation; the 1054 schism is the earlier split with the Greek church | His word *cristendōm* means Christianity, or the Christian people, not a modern denomination. *Riht gelēafa* is “right belief.” |

The survey article on the seven sacraments says Lateran IV “confirmed” the seven. The English canons of 1215 do not list them. Florence and Trent do. ^[ambiguous]

## How the church was organized

The tenth-century reform is monastic and royal: Dunstan, Æthelwold, Oswald, and the *Regularis Concordia* of about 970. It replaces married clerks with celibate Benedictines in a minority of houses, mostly in the south and the Midlands, and it makes monks into bishops. Worcester, Wulfstan’s southern see, is Oswald’s monastic cathedral, with clerks still present. York, his archbishopric, was not remade by that southern programme.

Wulfstan is an heir of the reform and a different kind of reformer. Ælfric puts his hope in monastic example and in preaching. Wulfstan puts his hope in the king’s law and in bishops. He holds York and Worcester together until 1016, which the reformers’ own rules frown on. His *Canons of Edgar* instruct the secular priests who actually serve the people.

There is no seminary, no curia of the modern kind, and no parish system of the thirteenth-century sort. Pastoral care comes from minsters, monastic cathedrals, and local priests. The liturgy of the Mass is Latin. The preaching and the law he writes for the people are Old English.

## Words that do the doctrinal work

- *Godes lage* and *woruldlage*: God’s law and the world’s law. Reinhard’s title for the whole project is “God’s lore and worldly law.” They are meant to agree.
- *Had*: ecclesiastical rank or orders, not yet “the sacrament of holy orders” as Trent defines it.
- *Clænnes*: chastity or purity, the reformers’ demand on clergy and the virtue urged on the laity.
- *Unrihthǣmed*, *ǣwbrice*: illicit sex and adultery. The moral list in his laws is public and concrete: murder, perjury, Sunday work, broken fasts, heathen practice, selling Christians out of the country.
- *Þēow* and *þrǣl*: unfree people. The 1008 code bans the export of Christian slaves. It does not abolish slavery.
- *Eorl*, *bonda*, *lagu*: Norse-coloured words for noble, householder, and law. In his usage *eorl* is the high noble, where older English said *ealdorman*.

## Open questions

- How explicitly Ælfric’s Easter homily, dependent on Ratramnus, teaches the presence Wulfstan assumes. The sixteenth-century claim that Ælfric denied the real presence, and the Catholic Encyclopedia’s reply, were not re-read for this page.
- Whether any passage in *Polity* itself names a conception feast of Mary.
- The exact date papal infallibility was defined. This page only has Anthimos in 1895 treating it as a novelty.
