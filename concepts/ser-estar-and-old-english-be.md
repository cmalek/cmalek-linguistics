# Ser, Estar, and the Old English Copula

Spanish has two copulas. *Ser* (from Latin *esse* “be,” with some forms of *sedēre* “sit,” and a past stem *fui* from the same “become” root as English *be*) marks identity, class, origin, material, and what a grammar calls essence. *Estar* (from Latin *stāre* “stand”) marks location, a condition the subject happens to be in, the progressive, and a resultant state (*está cerrado*). That is the account in the Romance-copula survey ([[references/romance-copula-ser-estar]]).

Old English also has two presents of “be,” [[concepts/beon-and-wesan]]. Luke Bradley’s thesis names Spanish *ser* and *estar* as the living parallel: two copulas with a real semantic split, of a kind continental Germanic did not keep ([[references/bradley-2011-two-copulas]]). He does not pair *bēon* with *ser* and *wesan* with *estar* sentence by sentence. The pairing below is a comparison of the published characterizations. ^[inferred]

## Where a pairing looks tempting

| Situation | Old English | Spanish |
|---|---|---|
| Generic or gnomic property | *bēon*: *wiga biþ strang* | *ser*: *un guerrero es fuerte* |
| Particular identity | *wesan*: *hē is wiga* | *ser*: *es un guerrero* |
| Future temporary state | *bēon*: *iċ bēo gearo sōna* | *estar*: *estaré listo pronto* |
| Resultant state after an event | *wesan* + participle: *wæron belocene* “were shut” | *estar* + participle: *están cerradas* |
| Transition into a state | [[concepts/weorthan\|weorþan]]: *milde wearð* | *ponerse*, *hacerse*, *volverse*, *llegar a ser*, not *ser* or *estar* |

The first row is the classroom analogy: *bēon* for the lasting truth, like *ser*. It fails on the second row. A particular person’s identity is *is* in Old English and *ser* in Spanish. *Estar* is the wrong Spanish verb for *hē is wiga*. The third row fails the other way: the Old English future of a temporary state is the *b*-form, and the Spanish future of that state is *estar*. One English verb is doing jobs that Spanish splits across *ser* and *estar*, and the other English verb takes the identity job that Spanish gives to *ser*.

## What the splits actually are

The Old English split, in Petré and Cuyckens following Kilpiö, is particular present (*is*) against generic, iterative, durative, or future (*bēon*). *Is* can also carry an imperfective “eternal present.” *Bēon* is not reserved for essence, and *is* is not reserved for accidents.

The Spanish split is essence, class, and identity (*ser*) against stage, place, and resultant condition (*estar*). Generics and identities both take *ser*. Location and “how one is right now” take *estar*.

*Weorþan* has no Spanish copula partner. It is the change. *Estar* plus a participle records the state after the change, which is the job of *wesan* plus a participle in the Jones and Macleod counts. German *werden* is the cognate that stayed.

## Etymology, which also crosses

Bradley, citing Ringe (2006), says Proto-Germanic *\*wesaną* meant “stay” and only secondarily “be,” while *\*beunan* still meant future being or becoming. *Estar* comes from “stand, stay.” That is an etymological echo between *wesan* and *estar*, and it is not how attested Old English uses *wesan*: by then *wesan* is the ordinary present of “be,” including identity. The “become” root is the other cross. Spanish *fui* and Old English *bēon* share it, and in Spanish that root sits inside *ser*, the identity verb, as a past stem. ^[inferred]

The contact claim that Bradley and Wischer actually argue is Celtic, not Romance. Old Irish has a copula for classification and a substantive verb (cognate with Latin *stāre*) for current existence and location. Bradley treats that twofold “be” as the model transferred into Old English. Spanish is his illustration that a living language can keep two copulas. It is not the historical source.
