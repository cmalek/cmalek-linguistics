# Ofermod

*Ofermod* occurs once in [[references/battle-of-maldon|The Battle of Maldon]], at the moment Byrhtnoth lets the Vikings cross:

> Ðā se eorl ongan for his ofermōde / ālȳfan landes tō fela lāþere ðēode.

The word is a hapax in this poem. With Anglian *oferhygd*, it is the usual Old English for the sin of pride in religious writing. That religious habit is why so many readers hear a rebuke. The fight over the word is a fight over the poem’s purpose.

## What each reading says

**Tolkien.** *The Homecoming of Beorhtnoth Beorhthelm’s Son*, with the essay “Ofermod,” takes the lines as overmastering pride: the earl yielded ground he should have held. [[references/halbrooks-2003-byrhtnoth-mirth|Halbrooks]] quotes that rendering. The teaching text on [Old English Aerobics](https://www.oldenglishaerobics.net/maldon.php) calls the same decision a tactical blunder. Later fiction of Tolkien’s revises the scene: Gandalf will not let the Balrog cross the bridge in Moria. That parallel is Alexander Bruce’s and Mary R. Bowman’s, summarized in the paper-search abstracts, and it is a reception of the poem in [[concepts/tolkien-old-english]].

**Halbrooks.** The ambiguity is the point. *Ofermod* signs both heroic elegy and Christian warning, praise and blame together. He glosses the phrase as great-hearted pride, and he sets *Hákonarmál* beside it. The article itself was not read past the abstract.

**Kirby.** “In Defence of Byrhtnoth” (*Florilegium* 11, 1992) sets out to show that censure of Byrhtnoth is mistaken. Abstract only.

**Griffith 2024, as reviewed.** Two reviews of the edition agree that he refuses “sinful pride,” and they phrase the positive sense differently.

- [[references/karasawa-2025-griffith-maldon|Karasawa]] reports a glossary sense of self-respect: the feeling of what is fitting, which stops a man doing what he thinks unworthy. Byrhtnoth has promised battle. Breaking the promise would class him with the cowards who made a *beot* in the meeting and then ran. Offa and the poet condemn those men. *Landes to fela* is an understatement that the Viking force was too large.
- [[references/thomas-2026-griffith-maldon|Thomas]] reports a change of strategy. The first speech has already committed Byrhtnoth to fight. The causeway defense follows once he sees the numbers. *Lytegian* is the Vikings’ guile in forcing the choice. *Landes to fela* is emotional, a wish that it had been otherwise. The glossary gloss is simply “pride,” and that pride is what makes him keep his word.

The edition was not read. The two paraphrases are both on the table. ^[ambiguous]

## What the lines do inside the poem

The crossing comes after a formal exchange. A Viking messenger asks for tribute. Byrhtnoth answers that the payment will be spears, and that Æthelred’s land will be held. Only then do the armies wait on the tide, and only then does he allow the passage. Readers who hear folly start from *ofermod* and *landes to fela*. Readers who hear honor start from the promise in the first speech and from the *beot* Offa remembers after the flight. The speeches are set out on [[concepts/heroic-speech-in-maldon]].

John D. Niles’s chapter abstract goes further than the gloss: glorifying this death is how the poem argues for paying tribute. That is a claim about policy, not only about a word. See [[references/niles-maldon-mythopoesis]].
