# Old English Grammar

[[concepts/old-english]] is weakly syntactic and strongly **synthetic**: role is carried by inflection more than by rigid SVO order. Noun ending-change is **declension** (the source notes sometimes say “conjugation”). ^[ambiguous] UT EIEOL: in **prose** actual order is often close to MnE (verbs may go clause-final; prepositions may be postposed); **verse** is much freer. They still call MnE analytic. Course path: [[skills/ut-eieol-old-english]].

## Nouns and articles

Nouns have declension class, grammatical gender (m/f/n), number, and case (nom, acc, gen, dat, plus a limited instrumental). Grimm's strong/weak split: strong nouns typically continue PIE stems; weak nouns are later Germanic *n*-stems.

**Definite article** (shortcut to case/gender/number):

| | Masc sg | Fem sg | Neut sg | Plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nom | sē | sēo | þæt | þā |
| Acc | þone | þā | þæt | þā |
| Gen | þæs | þǣre | þæs | þāra / þǣra |
| Dat | þǣm / þām | þǣre | þǣm / þām | þǣm / þām |
| Inst | þȳ / þon | — | þȳ / þon | — |

*þā* collides: fem acc/nom sg, acc pl, and the adverb “then/when.” Nominative singular articles can double as pronouns (*sē* he, *sēo* she, *þæt* it).

There is no consistent indefinite article; *ān* and *sum* (“a certain”) inflect as strong adjectives. Translators often supply Modern English *a/an*.

## Verbs

Grimm again: **strong** verbs mark past by internal vowel change (*sing, sang, sung*); **weak** verbs take a dental *-d/-t*. Rough inventory: ~74% weak, ~24% strong, ~2% irregular. EIEOL: **seven** strong classes and **three** weak; plus preterite-presents and anomalous *be/do/go*. Classroom sheets ([[skills/oe-paradigm-lookup]]) usually show two weak conjugation patterns (*fremman* / *lufian*) and treat *-ettan/-læcan* as subclasses. ^[ambiguous]

Useful endings: infinitive *-an* (class II weak *-ian*); 3sg present often *-eþ*; plural past *-on* (but preterite-presents use *-on* in the *present* plural); inflected infinitive *-anne/-enne* after *tō*.

**Principal parts (magic sheet — keep this as a pointer, not a dump):**

| Class | Inf. | 1/3 sg pret | pl pret | p.p. |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Strong 3 *helpan* | helpan | healp | hulpon | holpen |
| Weak 1 short | fremman | fremede | fremedon | fremed |
| Weak 1 long | hælan | hælde | hældon | hæld |
| Weak 2 | lufian | lufode | lufodon | lufod |
| Pret-pres | witan | wiste | wiston | witen |
| *wesan* | wesan | wæs | wæron | — |
| *beon* | beon | — | — | — |

Noun demo rows on the same sheet: *stan*, *scip*, *giefu*, *nama*, *sunu*, *mann*. Full person/number and i-mutation footprints: [[skills/oe-paradigm-lookup]]. Word-formation (compounds, *ge-*, *-had*): [[concepts/oe-word-formation]]. Mitchell §139–140: OE word-order is mostly MnE-like, but full inflection vs MnE prepositions is the real split; early Chronicle prose can still be breathless *ond*-chains.

Number includes **dual** (exactly two) as well as singular and plural. There is **no systematic future**; present forms cover habitual, progressive, and future readings (*hēo gāþ* she goes / is going / will go). Perfects use *beon/wesan* + past participle.

## Adjectives

The same adjective is **weak** after a definite article, demonstrative, or possessive (those words carry the grammatical load) and **strong** when it stands alone or after *beon* (*the gift is good*). Strong adjectives split short-stem vs long-stem (vowel length / coda complexity), e.g. *til* vs *gōd*. Diagnostic strong masc acc sg *-ne* (*tilne*, *gōdne*); gen/dat fem *-re*; dat pl *-um*; gen pl *-ra*. Adjectives in stem *-u/-o* switch to *-w-* before a vowel.

## Pronouns

Six classroom classes: personal, possessive/reflexive (the notes mis-label *-self* forms as “possessive”), relative, interrogative (*hwā, hwæs, hwǣm*), demonstrative, miscellaneous (*hwilċ, ælċ, æniġ*; *eall, sum, ān* as adjective or pronoun). ^[ambiguous]

Personal pronouns have **dual** (*wit, ġit*). Accusative often has a *-ċ/-iċ* form beside a dative-looking form. Neuter *hit* for sexless referents, but *wif* (neuter noun, female person) takes *hēo* (natural gender); grammatical gender can still force *hēo* for *bōc*. The definite article doubles as a personal pronoun.

Weak verbs form the past with a **dental preterite** (*t*/*d*: MnE *heard*, OE *hīerde*). Strong verbs use [[concepts/classroom-grammar-terms|gradation]] (ablaut) instead. Agreement in OE is gender and case as well as number — see that glossary for *concord*, impersonals, and analogical leveling of strong → weak.

| | 1sg | 1du | 1pl |
|---|---|---|---|
| N | iċ | wit | wē |
| A | mē, meċ | unc, uncit | ūs, ūsiċ |
| G | mīn | uncer | ūser, ūre |
| D | mē | unc | ūs |

Second person: *þū / ġit / ġe*. Third sg: *hē / hēo~hīo / hit*. Third pl (all genders): *hīe, hī, hȳ, hēo*.

## Prepositions, adverbs, conjunctions

Prepositions govern a **case**. Accusative often = motion toward / across a boundary / duration; dative = static place, time-point, means; genitive = partition, exclusion, reference, time-span as a whole. Instrumental sense usually rides dative (*mid*) rather than a distinct prepositional instrumental. In poetry, “prepositions” may be **postposed** (*Scedelandum in*).

Adverbs are often adjective + *-e* (*dēop → dēope*); some already end in *-e* (*clǣne*); some take *-līċe* (the ancestor of ModE *-ly*). An inflected adjective can look identical to an adverb: *swīþe rincas* vs *feohtaþ swīþe*.

Conjunctions mark clause boundaries for translators. Coordinators include *ac, ond, ne, oþþe*; correlatives *ġif … þonne, ne … ne, oþþe … oþþe, swā … swā*. *þæt* is overloaded (comparative, complement, result, purpose). A subordinator must start its clause; mid-clause *þā* is usually the adverb; *æfter* + dative NP is a preposition, *æfter* + finite clause is a conjunction.

## Translating sentences

Do **not** gloss left-to-right. Algorithm: (1) locate independent / dependent / relative / impersonal clauses; (2) find the **finite** verb (personal/tense endings; second position in many independent clauses); (3) find the subject; (4) fill remaining roles; (5) repeat. Sequence of tense/mood tends to stay past once past, subjunctive once subjunctive. Impersonal clauses have no expressed subject (*[it] befitted his tongue to sing* in the Cædmon prose).
