# Heroic speech in The Battle of Maldon

A large part of the fragment is direct speech, and the speeches are built to a pattern. A speaker is named, a verb of saying is paired with a gesture, and the words repeat a small set of promises: I will not flee, I remember what I said in hall, I will avenge my lord or lie beside him. The pattern is audible because the same half-line opens Byrhtnoth’s answer to the messenger and Byrhtwold’s last speech: *maþelode, bord hafenode*.

The line numbers follow the [Old English Aerobics](https://www.oldenglishaerobics.net/maldon.php) text of [[references/battle-of-maldon]].

## The opening exchange

A *wicinga ār* stands on the shore, calls out, and delivers his people’s *ærende* as a *beot* (lines 25–41). The demand is formal: send rings quickly for protection; it is better to buy off this spear-rush with *gafol* than to fight; they will fasten a truce for gold; if Byrhtnoth, the richest man there, will ransom his people, he may pay the seamen *on hyra sylfra dōm*, “at their own judgment,” and the fleet will sail.

Byrhtnoth answers angry and resolute, shield raised, ash-spear brandished (lines 42–61). He turns their noun back on them. The *gafol* will be spears, a poisoned point, and old swords, the heriot that will do them no good in battle. The herald is to report a hateful message: an undishonored earl stands here with his troop and will defend this homeland, Æthelred’s country. Heathens must fall in battle. Tribute would be too soft a way for them to take treasure. Point and edge, a grim battle-play, must reconcile the two sides before any *gofol* is paid.

After the tide drops and the ford is held, the Vikings *lytegian* and ask for passage (lines 84–88). Byrhtnoth calls back over the cold water (lines 93–95): the way is open; come quickly; God alone knows who will hold the slaughter-place. That short speech is the grant discussed on [[concepts/ofermod]].

His last speech is a prayer, once he can no longer stand (lines 173–80). He thanks the Ruler for the joys he has had and asks that his soul be allowed to travel into God’s keeping, and that hell-fiends not be allowed to harm it. The narrator then calls the killers *hæþene scealcas*. [[references/jones-1990-lexical-structure-maldon|Jones]] notes that the English otherwise claim to fight for king and land, so the prayer does not turn the poem into an exemplum.

## The chain after the flight

Godric, Odda’s son, rides off on Byrhtnoth’s horse. The hearth-companions see their lord dead and advance, willing to lose life or avenge the man (lines 205–08). From there the poet stages a series:

| Speaker | Gesture and verb | What the speech does |
|---|---|---|
| Ælfwine, Ælfric’s son | *wordum mælde*, *on ellen spræc* | Remembers *beot* spoken at mead; offers his Mercian lineage (grandfather Ealhhelm); will not leave now his kinsman and lord lies dead |
| Offa | *gemælde*, shakes the ash-spear | Tells Ælfwine he has urged them all; each must encourage the other while he can hold a sword; Godric’s flight broke the shield-wall because men thought the rider was their lord |
| Leofsunu | *gemælde*, raises the linden shield | Vows he will not flee a foot; the steadfast men of Sturmer must not reproach him; weapon, point and iron, shall take him |
| Dunnere, a plain *ceorl* | *cwæð*, shakes a dart, shouts over all | A maxim: he who means to avenge his lord in the host cannot hesitate or care for his life |
| Edward the tall | *gylpwordum spræc* | Will not flee a foot’s space or bend back while his better lies dead |
| Oswald and Eadwold | *wordon bædon* | Reported speech: they urge their kinsmen to hold and use weapons unweakly |
| Byrhtwold, an old *geneat* | *maþelode, bord hafenode*, shakes the ash | Gnomic comparatives, then a vow to lie by his lord |
| Godric, Æthelgar’s son | the narrator says he encouraged them in the same way | The last line of the fragment says this is a different Godric from the one who fled |

Byrhtwold’s lines are the ones anthologies quote:

> Hige sceal þe heardra, heorte þe cenre, / mod sceal þe mare, þe ure mægen lytlað.

Mind must be the harder, heart the keener, courage the greater, as our strength wanes. The old retainer will not go away. He means to lie beside his dear lord.

Ælfwine’s *beot* is a hall-boast remembered in the field. Edward’s *gylpwordum* names the same tradition from the other side: words of vaunt, spoken when the vaunt can only be a refusal to step back. Offa had already said, the day before at the meeting-place, that many would speak boldly there who would fail at need (lines 198–201). The later speeches are the kept vows. The flight is the broken one. [[references/karasawa-2025-griffith-maldon|Griffith, via Karasawa]], holds this verbal commitment until late, in Leofsunu, Offa, and Byrhtwold, and treats it as the standard the cowards fail.

## Why the speeches sound stylized

Three independent observations line up.

**Formulas and gesture.** *Maþelode*, *wordum mælde*, *gemælde*, and *cwæð* are ordinary Old English speech openings. Maldon binds them to a repeated prop: shield up, spear shaken. The Viking herald and Byrhtnoth share *wordum mælde*. Byrhtnoth and Byrhtwold share a whole half-line. The echo puts the last loyal retainer in the lord’s speaking posture. [[references/griffith-2022-vengeance-of-offa|Griffith]] treats that repeat as rare inside the poetic corpus, and he notes that these are the only two speakers who both teach the men and are old. He also follows Ball: the spears, the poisoned point, and the old sword promised to the messenger are the weapons Byrhtnoth then uses, in that order, on the three Vikings he fights. The reply to the herald is the speech whose wording the rest of the fragment keeps. The death-prayer is not.

**A local voice inside the formula.** [[references/karasawa-2025-griffith-maldon|Karasawa’s review of Griffith]] reports that unusual dialect forms sit disproportionately in the spoken lines: south-eastern *gofol* in the East Saxon ealdorman’s reply, Mercian *gemunu* in the Mercian noble’s speech, and possibly Essex *iren* in Leofsunu’s vow. The poet can write one heroic idiom and still color a speaker.

**Placement.** [[references/jones-1990-lexical-structure-maldon|Jones’s]] three information-dense dips are speech passages: the invitation to cross, the turn after the flight, and Byrhtwold. Speaking verbs dominate the latter part of the poem, where plurals have thinned to single men. He expected *beot*, then action, then victory or death. The boasts come when there is little left to boast about.

[Old English Aerobics](https://www.oldenglishaerobics.net/maldon.php) describes the same design from the other end: the poet is brief about the cowards and about the killing, and he sets each resolute speech next to a spare notice of death. *Forð* is the poem’s most frequent adverb for the English movement, and it is also a verb-piece for dying (*forðfēran*). That double sense is the Aerobics reading of the adverb, illustrated from *The Wanderer* and *The Dream of the Rood*.

Richard Marsden’s reader (DOI [10.1017/cbo9780511817069.037](https://doi.org/10.1017/cbo9780511817069.037)) calls these the defiant speeches of loyal, doomed heroes, and doubts they were meant as a national rallying cry. The stylization is real either way: the poem remembers the men as speakers of vows. An unsigned specialist essay on WarHistory.org adds the obvious historical limit: speeches and inner intentions in a battle poem are the poet’s, and they are weak evidence for words shouted on the shore. ^[inferred]
