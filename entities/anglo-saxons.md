# Anglo-Saxons

Cultural group who spoke [[concepts/old-english]] and inhabited much of what is now England and south-eastern Scotland in the Early Middle Ages. Conventional bounds: settlement by about 450, end with the Norman Conquest in 1066.

## Key Ideas

- Identity (*Englisc*) coalesces by the eighth century from Germanic settlers plus Romano-British culture. Late Anglo-Saxon politics and language are direct predecessors of high-medieval England and Middle English.
- [[entities/bede]] (d. 735) is the earliest detailed origin story: regional kingdoms tracing to Saxons (Old Saxony / northern Germany), Jutes (Jutland), and Angles (Angeln between them). He prefers collective “Angles/English”; “Saxon” for early pagans and for continental neighbors (“Old Saxons”).
- “Anglo-Saxon” as a compound is medieval (continental eighth century; Alfred as *Anglosaxonum Rex* in the 880s) but not the everyday self-name; “English” wins. Celtic neighbors keep “Saxon” (*Saeson*, *Sasanach*).
- Settlement was more gradual encroachment than a single invasion. Place-name fossils: *-ing*, *-ton*, *-ford*, *-ham*, *-stead*. Kingdom geography is on [[concepts/anglo-saxon-kingdoms]]; the Bede trio (Angles, Saxons, Jutes) is unpacked under [[entities/germanic-peoples]].
- Conversion from [[concepts/anglo-saxon-paganism]] to [[concepts/christianity-in-anglo-saxon-england]] is a seventh-century political process, not an overnight folk change.
- Literary invitation-story (Vortigern, Hengest and Horsa, c. 430) is late and dated inconsistently (Gildas, *Chronica Gallica*, Bede 450–455, *Historia Brittonum* 428, Chronicle 449). Archaeology (e.g. Spong Hill) pushes material change earlier than Bede. ^[ambiguous]
- A 2022 aDNA study reports substantial fifth-century immigration from a north-continental zone (Netherlands–N. Germany–Denmark), mixed burial, and modern English ancestry with large regional ranges (roughly 25–47% north-continental). ^[inferred]
