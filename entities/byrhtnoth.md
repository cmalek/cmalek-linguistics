# Byrhtnoth

Byrhtnoth, son of Byrhthelm in the poem, was ealdorman of Essex and a leading lay noble of [[entities/aethelred-the-unready|Æthelred]]’s early reign. He was killed fighting a Viking army at Maldon in August 991. The Chronicle annal, as Richard Marsden quotes it, says Ipswich was ravaged, Byrhtnoth was slain at Maldon, and tribute was first paid to the Danes because of the terror along the coast. The poem [[references/battle-of-maldon]] is the long vernacular account of the same death.

## The death-day

An unsigned specialist essay on [WarHistory.org](https://warhistory.org/article/the-battle-of-maldon-i) compares the obits collected in the 1991 Maldon volume. A Winchester calendar written for Ælfwine of the New Minster around 1030, within living memory, gives 11 August. A twelfth-century Ely calendar gives 10 August. A sixteenth-century copy of a lost Ramsey calendar gives 11 August. Jonathan Wilcox’s 1996 essay, known here from its abstract, says 10 or 11 August 991. The Winchester obit is the one the WarHistory essay treats as the firmer anniversary. ^[ambiguous]

## What writers near the time say

The same essay summarizes Byrhtferth of Ramsey’s *Vita Sancti Oswaldi*: Byrhtnoth’s own retinue fought with him, he encouraged them in formation, he is tall and white-haired, many died on both sides, the rest of the English fled, and the Danes were so wounded they could scarcely man their ships. The style is rhetorical. Height and white hair are the details the older vault note already flagged as later color; the *Vita* is in fact early, and the essay says other sources confirm them. The Ely story of a fourteen-day battle and of two separate fights is treated there as chronicle embroidery. The headless burial at Ely is the part that matches the body the monks had. *Liber Eliensis* is already marked unreliable for the fight on [[references/battle-of-maldon]].

He was a patron of Ely. The 1991 essay volume includes chapters on his family, his tomb, his remains and stature, and an embroidery associated with his widow. Those chapters were not read. See [[references/scragg-1991-maldon-essays]].

## In the poem

He draws up the shield-wall, refuses tribute in a formal speech, then allows the Vikings over the ford *for his ofermode*. The argument over that word is [[concepts/ofermod]]. He kills at least one Viking after he is wounded, laughs, thanks God for the day’s work, and dies asking that his soul be received and that hell-fiends not harm it. His horse is then taken by Godric, Odda’s son, and the men who stay speak the vows collected on [[concepts/heroic-speech-in-maldon]].

The poem calls him *eorl* at the moment of the grant. [[references/karasawa-2025-griffith-maldon|Karasawa, reporting Griffith]], says the poetic title is there so the audience judges him as a hero of the verse tradition, alongside the rank *ealdormann*.

## Dying prayer (Glenn translation)

[[references/glenn-1982-maldon-translation|Glenn's translation]] has him wounded by a "southern spear," shove with his shield so the shaft bursts, kill two Vikings, laugh and thank *Metod* "for the day-work God gave him," then lose his sword-arm to a Viking while drawing his bill. He prays to the "Wielder of peoples" that his soul may go to God and that "no hell-scathers harm it," and the heathens hew him down; Ælfnoth and Wulfmær die beside him. A half-line is missing in the original at the moment he looks to heaven.
