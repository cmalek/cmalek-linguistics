# Germanic Sound Change × Old English Grammar

## The Connection

[[concepts/old-english-grammar]] tells a reader to look endings up: strong verbs change a root vowel, weak verbs add a dental, and the magic sheet holds the principal parts. [[concepts/germanic-sound-change]] says those vowel patterns are layers — inherited ablaut, then later i-mutation and breaking — and that analogy afterward hides older phonology. The glossary on [[concepts/classroom-grammar-terms]] is where the two vocabularies meet: gradation is ablaut, and analogy is an irregular reshaped toward a dominant pattern.

## Where They Co-occur

- [[concepts/classroom-grammar-terms]] defines gradation with *sing/sang*, defines analogy with the leveling of many Old English strong verbs toward the Modern English weak past, and sends breaking and i-mutation to the sound-change page.
- [[concepts/old-english]] uses i-mutation for *foot/feet* and *man/men*, and points at the grammar page for the inflected system those alternations sit inside.
- [[concepts/old-norse-grammar]] shows the sister language doing the same stack inside a paradigm: *skjǫldr* with breaking, beside *skildi* and *skildir* with i-umlaut. The sound-change page already flags Old Norse breaking (*gjalda* beside Old English *geldan*) as a debated mechanism.

## Cross-cutting Insight

A magic-sheet row such as *helpan / healp / hulpon / holpen* is the grammar page's strong class 3, and the vowel pattern is the ablaut the glossary calls gradation. ^[inferred] That is a different layer from i-mutation, which the sound-change page illustrates with *fōt* ~ *fēt* and *mann* ~ *menn* and which can coexist with ablaut in one paradigm. The grammar page's rough inventory — about 74% weak, 24% strong — is the outcome the glossary names as analogy: a dental past became the dominant pattern, and many strong verbs joined it on the way into Modern English. The sheet remains a lookup because that leveling is unfinished. Cells still show the older layers, and a single ending rule will not generate them.

## Tensions and Trade-offs

- The grammar page marks two classroom uncertainties that this synthesis inherits: whether noun ending-change should be called declension or conjugation, and whether weak verbs are two patterns or three classes. ^[ambiguous]
- Old Norse breaking's mechanism is flagged as debated on both the sound-change page and the Norse grammar page. A parallel paradigm is an illustration, not a shared explanation.
- The analogy definition gives *mouse*/*mouses* and "many" strong verbs becoming weak. It does not list which Old English classes leveled. The 74% figure and the analogy label are from different notes.

## Strongest Objection

Ending-class lookup would still be required if every stem vowel were phonologically transparent. Seven strong classes, strong and weak nouns, and strong versus weak adjectives are morphological inventory. Sound laws explain some stem alternations. They do not explain *þæs* versus *þǣre*.

> test: which magic-sheet vowel alternations does a named layer (ablaut, i-mutation, breaking, Verner) predict, and which cells are only an ending class with a stable stem?

## Open Questions

- The wiki has no inventory of magic-sheet principal parts tagged by layer. Until that exists, "unfinished analogy" is a reading of the 74% weak majority, not a cell-by-cell account.
