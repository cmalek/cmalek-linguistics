# Research: Future Tense in Old English

## Overview

Old English documents future time without a future tense. Finite verbs have a present and a preterite. The present is non-past, so it covers “she goes,” “she is going,” and “she will go.” From Smith’s 1896 exercise book through the York corpus manual, that two-tense description is stable. What the literature argues about is the extra machinery: *willan* and *sculan* plus a bare infinitive, the *bēon* present of ‘be’, a present-tense *weorþan* passive, and, in older work, perfective *ge-*. English eventually grammaticalized *will* and *shall*. German grammaticalized *werden*. Neither outcome is an Old English paradigm.

## Key Findings

- **There is no inflectional future.** Smith (1896) says Early West Saxon used the present for present and future ([[references/smith-1896-oe-grammar]]). STELLA §§37 and 44 teach the same rule and tell the reader that *iċ wille gān* means “I want to go” ([[references/stella-old-english-verb-phrase]]). The vault’s own verb notes already say this, with *hēo gāþ* as the three-way gloss ([[concepts/old-english-grammar]]).
- **Close biblical translation uses the present for Latin and Greek futures.** In 1,118 matched futurate clauses, the Anglo-Saxon Gospels use a present form 84.8% of the time and a modal 2.1% ([[references/cowper-2015-no-future-in-old-english]]). Cowper and colleagues conclude that modality was not yet a contrastive inflectional feature, so the present was free to be predictive. By Purvey, *shall* is about 81%. By the King James Version, a modal is effectively required.
- ***Willan* and *sculan* are already auxiliary-like, and sometimes predictive, in Alfredian prose.** In Wischer’s Helsinki selection, 84% of *willan* and 96% of *sculan* take a bare infinitive, and some of those tokens cannot mean ‘want’ or ‘must’ ([[references/wischer-2006-markers-of-futurity]]). She quotes Denison (1993: 304) that by late Old English they were “almost a future tense.” Modal colouring is still the usual sense. YCOE records that range inside the gloss of *willan* (“wish … be about to, shall, will”) and does not give the corpus a future tag ([[references/ycoe-modal-and-tense-tags]]).
- ***Bēon* is the future-leaning present of ‘be’, not a general future auxiliary.** *Eom/is* marks a current state. *Bēo/biþ* is preferred for future, habitual, and generic states ([[concepts/beon-and-wesan]]). It rarely takes an infinitive, so it could not become “will” plus a lexical verb. The Dictionary of Old English published the verb as Kilpiö’s *BEON* fascicle (1992). Laing (2010) finds some early Middle English systems in which singular *b*-forms are future-only ([[references/laing-2010-beon-futurity]]).
- ***Weorþan* was the cognate of German *werden* and was not chosen.** Handbooks say a present *weorþan* passive can sound future (Mitchell 1985 §755; Visser §1918; Kilpiö 1989). In Old English it is less frequent than in Old High German, it mostly builds passives and copular clauses, and it then disappears. Diewald and Wischer treat the English modal future as the less common path in Bybee’s typology ([[references/diewald-wischer-2013-futurity]]).
- **The two headline percentages measure different sets.** Wischer’s 11 *willan* out of 25 “mere future” expressions in Boethius is a hand filter for bleached prediction. Cowper’s 2.1% is every gospel clause aligned with an earlier or later future. Wischer’s own gospel stretches barely use *sculan*. The present remains the default future in that genre. Bleached *willan/sculan* are real in other prose and are not yet obligatory.

## Contradictions and Open Questions

- **How grammaticalized was the Old English future?** Teaching grammars and Cowper’s gospel count say: barely; use the present; do not translate *willan* as “will” by default. Wischer says the auxiliary syntax is already in place and bare prediction occurs in Alfred. The difference is mostly the denominator. A residual disagreement remains about dating: STELLA puts future *willan* “towards the end of the OE period,” and Wischer finds it in texts from about 880.
- **What do non-future *bēon* forms mean?** Future preference is agreed. Campbell, Kilpiö, and Traugott, as lined up in a 2009 abstract that could not be re-fetched, describe the other uses with incompatible aspect labels (invariable fact, generic, iterative, timeless, eternal). Wischer’s 2010 chapter, unread here beyond its abstract, collapses the marked member to habitual or future and invokes Celtic contact.
- **Mitchell’s syntax and the DOE entries for *willan* and *sculan*** are the two standard references this pass cites at second hand. §755 is the only Mitchell paragraph the articles keep quoting.
- **Perfective *ge-* as a future** is an older analysis (Streitberg 1891 and after). Wischer excludes it. This survey did not re-test it on texts.
- **The dual count outside the gospels was not found, and was not run.** Cowper’s alignment of an earlier or later future exists here only for the gospels. Wischer’s bleached-prediction filter on Boethius is 25 expressions. Her other figures are lexeme counts of *willan*, *sculan*, *bēon*, and *weorþan* in a mixed sample, not a census of future-referring clauses. Chronicle annals have no Latin future to align. Pastoral Care and the Boethius could be aligned to Latin. That count is still undone.
