# Old Norse Grammar × Snorri Sturluson

## The Connection

[[concepts/old-norse-grammar]] provides access to the wording of literary texts; [[entities/snorri-sturluson]] locates a possible author and compiler. New evidence makes the relationship more productive than a general warning about late sources: the same linguistic material can support a grammatical reading, an attribution argument, or a historical claim, with different controls required for each. ^[inferred]

## Where They Co-occur

The original scan found three shared backlinks: [[references/prose-edda]], [[references/egils-saga-eirik-court-passage]], and [[skills/ut-eieol-old-norse]]. The classroom material combines normalized readings, grammatical explanations, and historical introductions. Their presentation together does not give each the same evidential status. ^[inferred]

## External Evidence

Utrecht’s manuscript account identifies shared errors linking R and T, while U has a shorter Edda text and an explicit Snorri attribution. Its late paper T can preserve a lost medieval exemplar. Therefore manuscript date alone cannot determine the age or independence of a reading. See [[sources/utrecht-codex-trajectinus-transmission]].

Þorgeirsson’s 2018 study finds *Egils saga* stylistically closest to both portions of *Heimskringla*, even against further contemporary comparisons. It normalizes spelling and variant forms and treats the result as support for common authorship. This is evidence beyond the first draft’s statement that direct attribution is absent; it is not direct testimony. See [[sources/thorgeirsson-2018-egils-saga-stylometry]].

## Cross-cutting Insight

Normalization preserves some questions while reducing access to others. It can make a controlled vocabulary-frequency comparison possible, yet remove variation needed to study a particular manuscript’s spellings. The appropriate textual representation therefore depends on the question; no single normalized reading is an all-purpose historical witness. ^[inferred]

In the Egill court passage, *þit* can identify two addressees in the narrated exchange. That analysis does not date the dialogue. A statistical resemblance can support a common prose author without authenticating the represented tenth-century conversation. Metrical evidence for an embedded poem would require its own analysis. ^[inferred]

Utrecht’s shared errors also connect this problem to [[synthesis/bosworth-toller-x-dictionary-build]]: repeated readings can reveal dependence, whereas treating them as independent confirmations hides it. ^[inferred]

## Tensions and Trade-offs

The original draft was right to distinguish interpretation from direct testimony but too thin about positive attribution evidence. Snorri’s authorship of *Egils saga* remains debated; absence of a signature is not absence of a scholarly case. ^[ambiguous]

Neither the manuscript account nor the stylometric paper dates individual verses of *Hǫfuðlausn*. This pass therefore does not turn the saga’s narrative setting into a composition date or claim that later copying makes all earlier linguistic evidence unusable.

## Strongest Objection

The separation of questions can be too neat: linguistic dating, manuscript affiliation, and attribution can inform one another. Their interaction is evidence when made explicit, rather than automatically circular.

> test: does the common-authorship result persist across manuscript-based transcriptions and alternative normalization policies, with date, genre, and quoted material controlled?

## Open Questions
