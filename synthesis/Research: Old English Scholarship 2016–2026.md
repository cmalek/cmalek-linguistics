# Old English scholarship, 2016–2026: a scoping-review draft

**Living review, after batch 3.** **140 articles and scholarly chapters** are mapped, with **13 books/editions**, **two adjacent experimental studies**, and **six supplementary items** kept separate. The publication window is **January 1, 2016–October 28, 2026**. Batch 1 was a pilot of 49 articles and chapters. Batch 2 added 59, mostly from *Anglo-Saxon England* 45–50 and *Early Medieval England and its Neighbours* 51. Batch 3 adds 32 articles and chapters from publisher pages that arrived after that chart was filed: place-name articles, runic studies, homilies, and 2025–2026 journal articles, including *Early Medieval England* 52. Records already in the corpus were not added again. The set is still a retrieved corpus, not a census of the field. The review remains in progress.

Start with the [[references/old-english-2016-2026-literature-map|paper-by-paper literature map]]. It gives each title, authors, venue, topic, methodology, source link, and reading scope. The [[concepts/old-english-research-topics|topic guide]] and [[concepts/old-english-research-methodologies|methodology guide]] provide two independent ways into the material. [Structured corpus and queries](../references/old-english-2016-2026-corpus.json) are available for reuse.

## Incremental updates

This is the master page for a **cumulative, living scoping review**. New batches update this page in place. They also update the paper-by-paper map, structured corpus, topic and methodology guides, and batch log. Existing source IDs stay stable; duplicates, online/issue-date variants, and published/preprint versions are reconciled before counts change.

Each batch should aim for **50–100 additional articles/chapters with informative abstracts**, adjusted to access and source quality. This is a working batch size, not a cap or a completion rule. The aim is to build coverage into the hundreds while checking whether the search still omits entire areas or source families. Abstracts are sufficient when they supply the topic and methodology; otherwise the missing field remains unspecified or provisional.

Batch 2 did that venue-led pass for *Anglo-Saxon England* and its successor journal. Batch 3 then filed the later returns that were not already charted, including several place-name articles whose texts had been closed. The following pass should open homily-chapter abstracts and the place-name articles that are still titles only. See [[references/old-english-review-batch-log|batch log and coverage]] and [[skills/incremental-old-english-literature-review|update procedure]]. Later synthesis must state what new evidence adds, qualifies, or overturns; it must not merely append summaries to the end of this page.

## What the map shows

**Interpretation, historical reconstruction, and empirical analysis coexist.** Formal semantic work on modality and pronouns sits beside corpus studies of word order and phraseology, physical study of manuscripts, literary criticism, modern reception, and classroom enquiry. The differences are visible at publication level; “qualitative versus quantitative” would flatten too much of the research. For contrasting examples, see [Yanovich on modality](https://doi.org/10.1353/lan.2016.0045), [Beck on pronouns](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10828-020-09116-y), [Cichosz and colleagues on formulaicity](https://doi.org/10.1075/jhp.21008.cic), and [Rowley on scribal practice](https://doi.org/10.1017/ean.2025.10005).

## Connections that cut across the categories

**Literary scholarship can use experimental or computational procedures without changing its subject.** Minkova and Zhou compare compounds in verse and the lexicon through simulation and statistical analysis. Neidorf and colleagues profile poetic authorship through sound, metre, and diction. The latter conclusions were challenged in a [reply preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.12927); this review records the dispute without resolving it. See [[sources/oe-neidorf-2019-stylometry]] and [Minkova and Zhou](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1470542722000083).

**Manuscript and corpus work intersect.** A copied spelling is simultaneously linguistic data and evidence of scribal intervention. Rowley examines book production and collaboration, while Stratton raises questions about how editing and sampling affect historical corpora. Treating digitized text as transparent evidence would miss the distinction. See [[sources/oe-rowley-2026-palaeography]], [Stratton](https://doi.org/10.1177/00754242261426400), and [[sources/oe-taylor-2026-genitive]].

**Critical frameworks change the question asked of familiar material.** Deaf studies, material feminism, queer interpretation, and ecocriticism appear in this set. They are not interchangeable analytical procedures: they guide different kinds of interpretation and evidence selection. For examples see [[sources/oe-garner-2017-deaf-studies]], [Heckman on Juliana](https://doi.org/10.17077/1536-8742.2095), [Buchanan on queer reception](https://doi.org/10.7765/9781526136442.00023), and [[sources/oe-paz-2025-ecopoetry]]. The corpus establishes their presence during the window, not their first emergence or rate of adoption.

**Teaching is both an object of historical interpretation and a setting for present-day enquiry.** Weber surveys medieval education; Brookman and Robinson investigate creative translation with learners; Ogliari proposes a teaching unit. A proposal is distinct from an evaluated intervention. See [Weber](https://doi.org/10.1111/hic3.12518), [[sources/oe-brookman-robinson-2016-teaching]], and [Ogliari](https://doi.org/10.54103/2035-7680/30977).

**Medicine reaches across several research traditions.** Scott studies cognition and recipe language; Sweany questions cross-cultural comparison; laboratory reconstruction and a human-volunteer safety study form a separate adjacent group. These should not be collapsed into one method or treated as proof of historical clinical efficacy. See [Scott](https://doi.org/10.1111/tops.70015), [[sources/oe-sweany-2020-comparison]], and corpus records M14–M15.

Batch 3 adds the Rudolf and Irvine essay collection on anonymous homilies (2020) from the publisher’s volume description. Its chapters stay uncharted. ## Coverage and unresolved questions

The main answer is the **range of topics and approaches documented above**, rather than a claim that one theoretical school or text dominates the field. The [[sources/oe-ywes-2024-overview|2024 YWES overview]] supplies a useful independent coverage check across manuscripts, prose, poetic genres, and reception. [[entities/years-work-in-english-studies|YWES]] remains a route to extend the bibliography, although its complete decade of references was not extracted.

Batch 2 changes the shape of the retrieved set. Law, charters, homilies, names, and inscriptions are no longer thin corners of a poetry-and-corpus pilot. Scragg’s Northumbrian homily note (M17), Cardwell on *Be wifmannes beweddunge* (M29), and Porck’s Alkmaar psalter gloss (M34) put prose and liturgical Old English beside the poetic cluster. Baker on *sǣte* (M42) and Briggs on collective plant-names (M43) open a names family that batch 1 did not have. Okasha’s inscription supplement (M23), Sobol on the Franks Casket (M44), and Findell and Kopár on commemorative runes (M45) do the same for inscribed objects. Perillo on the *Letter of Alexander* (P26), Davis on Gothic *Beowulf* (P27), Callander on *Bryten* (P28), and Rambaran-Olm on the field’s racial historiography (P29) make race and global imagination visible; Wade’s 2020 essay on Bede’s Latin Song commentary stays supplementary because the text studied is not Old English.

Year counts for 2016–2021 rose because *Anglo-Saxon England* volumes 45–50 were opened. The 2026 count rose again because *Early Medieval England* 52 and *English Language and Linguistics* 30 were opened, and because issue year is kept when the online date falls in 2025. Issue years and online years still diverge. Three JEGP articles (O’Dell, Fulk, Leake) stay method-unspecified.

Batch 3 qualifies several gaps left after batch 2. Place-name work now includes Coates on *Wippedesfleot* (M46), Bailey on Putney (M47), Freeman on *\*peon* (M48), Wager on *fildena* (M49), and Pears and colleagues on *\*wæsse* (M50). Runic studies now include Waxenberger (L32), Hines on new finds and on the Eyke formula (L33, M64), Majewski on *strēlum* (P42), Fitzgerald on the Franks Casket *wylif* (M60), and Ooi, Majewski, and Kopár on dry-point runes (M65). Findell and Kopár (M45) are corrected to *Fragments* 6; an earlier volume 7 URL was a different page. Homiletic prose includes Upchurch’s edition of Leofric’s sermons (M56), missed in the volume 48 chart, plus Kehoe on Eustace (M54), Rudolf on the ECHOE editorial method (M53), Di Giuseppe on Wulfstan (M55), Thyr on a Temptation homily (M62), and Buniyatova’s English-language comparison of Ælfric and Wulfstan (L34). Computus is less thin: Perry Clarke reads the Old English tabernacle passage in Byrhtferth (M51), and Teresi ties a prayerbook diagram to Ælfric’s *De temporibus anni* (M52). Race and disciplinary criticism add Rambaran-Olm and Wade on the field’s name (P37), Sebo on enmity in *Beowulf* (P38), and Schilling on faith and othering in *Judith* and *Elene* (P40). Lehr on the Donestre (P39) sits with bodies and sex. Versloot’s phonology article (L29) had been set aside for lack of an abstract; the Cambridge page now supplies one. Leneghan’s preface article (M63) is inside the window: it appeared online on 26 October 2026.

What remains a limit of this search: chapter arguments in the Brill 2020 and Brepols homily volumes, which were seen as titles; JEPNS articles still known only from contents lists; Pelle’s 2024 note, whose opened paragraph discusses the Latin *Visio Pauli* and does not state the Old English analysis; Fontaine on slave resistance and the isotopic feorm companion, which stay out; and non-English scholarship beyond Buniyatova’s English summary. One *Neophilologus* article, Kesling on macaronic verse (P41), does not cover that journal. Uneven years still cannot establish a trend.

Dates require care: issue years, first-online publication, reprints, and digitization may differ. Original 2016 and 2019 Language papers must not be counted as 2026 research because of platform migration. The chart preserves known discrepancies.

## How the review was conducted

The user selected the whole field, articles and chapters with books separate, a scoping review, and abstract-based classification where topic and method are described. Public web discovery, targeted gap searching, and synthesis/contradiction checks produced the retained set. Batch 1 used three separate branch charts, integrated by the parent; those workers were not two independent screeners of every record. Batch 2 worked from journal contents and gap searches, then merged new records into the same corpus without renumbering IDs. Batch 3 checked the same workers’ later returns against the filed corpus and added only records that were not already present and whose pages stated an Old English argument. The [[references/old-english-scoping-review-protocol|protocol and decision record]] explains eligibility, dates, charting, and access limits. Its protocol was written after preliminary discovery and is not preregistered.

No authenticated MLA, ABELL, International Medieval Bibliography, Scopus, or Web of Science search/export was performed. Full discovery and exclusion counts were not retained, so a complete selection flow cannot be reconstructed. Batch 2 kept a decision list for what was opened and then left out: digitizations of pre-2016 articles, Latin liturgy and hagiography without an Old English text in the abstract, Old Norse court poetry, a dissertation, conference records, and the isotopic companion to the feorm article. Birkett’s vine-workers article was already in the corpus. Critical appraisal was outside this mapping task; abstracts were not used to assign methodological weaknesses.
