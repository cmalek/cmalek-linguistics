# Alfred the Great × Germanic Sound Change

## The Connection

[[entities/alfred-the-great]] made Winchester West Saxon the written koiné. [[concepts/germanic-sound-change]] is a stack with a different clock: Grimm and Verner are early and shared across Germanic; breaking, palatalization, and i-mutation are later and can diverge by dialect. The pages meet because the dialect a ninth-century court chose to copy is the layer in which those later changes get taught as "Old English."

## Where They Co-occur

- [[concepts/old-english-dialects]] treats West Saxon as the baseline because so much prose survives, and names Alfred's Wessex as the reason classrooms still learn it while Modern English continues Anglian.
- [[concepts/history-of-english]] puts the same ninth-century fact beside the sound-change stack: West Saxon survives under Alfred, so teaching texts are West Saxon.
- [[concepts/old-english]] dates the written standard to the tenth century and separately dates i-mutation (*foot/feet*, *man/men*) to the seventh.
- [[references/beowulf]] brings the two together thinly: Skeat's *biewolf* etymology is criticised there as violating Grimm's Law, and Alfred sits in the related list because the manuscript tradition is West Saxon.

## Cross-cutting Insight

The vowel spelling a beginner meets first is a late West Saxon photograph, not the Grimm/Verner layer. ^[inferred] Wright's high-value cues on the dialect page — West Saxon *ea* beside Anglian *æ*/*e* (*ceaster*/*cæster*), West Saxon *ie*/*y* beside simpler *e* (*dierne*/*derne*) — are the later processes the sound-change page calls breaking and i-mutation, caught in spelling. Alfred's vernacular program is why that photograph became the textbook default. Grimm's Law still matters in the wiki, but in *Beowulf* it shows up as a test of a name etymology, which is an earlier layer than the diphthongs the paradigm sheets drill.

## Tensions and Trade-offs

- Archive and descendant pull apart. Surviving prose is West Saxon; the dialect page says Modern English comes mostly from Anglian. A grammar of "the" language is a grammar of the copies.
- Early shared laws and late dialect spelling answer different questions. A West Saxon and an Anglian form can both obey Grimm and Verner and still disagree in breaking diphthongs.
- The *Beowulf* co-occurrence is an etymology dispute plus a manuscript-history link. It does not show that the poem's phonology was Alfred's policy.

## Strongest Objection

Wessex's political survival explains which manuscripts were copied. The sound laws were earlier, and their causes are already marked unsettled on the sound-change page. Calling the phonology Alfredian confuses copyists' dialect with the scope of Grimm and Verner.

> test: do Anglian and West Saxon witnesses differ in Grimm or Verner outcomes, or only in later processes (breaking diphthongs, i-mutation spellings, palatal *c*/*g*)?

## Open Questions

- A token count of textbook *ea* against Anglian *æ* or *a* across every copy was not found. What is published is a share of the written record. [[references/den-hertog-2024-oe-dialects|den Hertog]] says up to ninety percent of surviving Old English was written, not necessarily composed, in West Saxon. [[references/computational-study-of-old-english-2025|Martín Arista]] says about 60 percent. ^[ambiguous] Either way the classroom vowel is the spelling of the bulk of the copies. Anglian spellings survive in a named minority of early witnesses, not as the default page a learner opens. Grimm and Verner are not what those copies disagree about.
