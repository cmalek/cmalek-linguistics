# Research: Early and Late Old English Spelling and Inflection

Systematic review, protocol confirmed 30 October 2026. Early and late are period markers. West Saxon, Kentish, Mercian, and Northumbrian are kept apart. Cædmon’s Hymn and Junius 121 are examples of early and late books, not the comparison. This page was drafted with AI assistance. Claims below come from texts that were opened. Campbell 1959, Hogg 1992, and Hogg and Fulk 2011 were not opened. Where an included study cites them, the citation is second-hand.

Registration on OSF was recommended and was not completed before the search.

## Overview

Written Old English does not move from early to late as one system. Inside West Saxon, the spelling ⟨ie⟩ in early writing generally corresponds to ⟨y⟩ in late writing. Unstressed endings, including the dative plural *-um*, become less distinct in the eleventh century, and Wulfstan’s *Sermo Lupi* already alternates *-um* and *-an*. Late Northumbrian simplifies parts of the verb earlier than the southern written standard, including final *-n* and the class II *-i-* formative.

The four named leads do not all resolve the same way. *-an* for a present plural subjunctive, *-ige* as a late replacement of *-ie*, and a rise of *sylf* from Maldon to Wulfstan are not what the opened studies report. Mercian is almost empty in this pass.

## Question and methods

The question: in written Old English, how did spelling and inflection change from early to late, once the four dialects are kept apart, and which changes do the sources report as shared?

Eligibility followed the confirmed protocol. Abstracts were allowed. A form, dialect, or period missing from the available text is “not reported.” Silence is not evidence that the dialect lacked the form.

## Key findings

### West Saxon spelling: ⟨ie⟩ and ⟨y⟩

[[sources/condorelli-2019-west-saxon-ie]] compares Chronicle manuscripts and states the handbook correspondence: early West Saxon ⟨ie⟩ generally matches late West Saxon ⟨y⟩, and before palatals the late spelling is ⟨i⟩. He cites Campbell 1959 §§302 and Hogg 1992 §5.167 for that rule. His own counts show the rise of ⟨y⟩ and the fall of ⟨ie⟩ moving together only loosely. He also reports that early and late West Saxon are probably not a straight line of descent, citing Hoad. ^[extracted]

[[sources/lutz-1984-late-west-saxon-standard]], from the publisher abstract, says late West Saxon was written by scribes outside Wessex and was used when earlier texts were copied, so it behaves as a written standard. ^[extracted]

This is a period change inside West Saxon writing. The opened texts do not show the same ⟨ie⟩ to ⟨y⟩ replacement in Kentish, Mercian, or Northumbrian.

The local cue sheet on [[concepts/old-english-dialects]] already listed “y for earlier ie” as a late West Saxon diagnostic that still needed a grammar check. Condorelli is that check for the Chronicle pronouns and the handbook rule, with the descent caveat attached.

### Unstressed vowels and dative plural *-um*

[[sources/walters-2004-case-syncretism]] reports Campbell 1959: the back vowels of final unstressed syllables are already weakening in ninth-century Kentish charters; by late Old English the suffix vowels are mostly a front-versus-back distinction; in the eleventh century those vowels largely coalesce (Campbell 1959: 369, 379, as she cites him). She also reports that *-um* is occasionally *-un* in early West Saxon by the beginning of the eleventh century, and that replacement of final /m/ by /n/ is complete by the end of that century (Campbell 1959: 378; Moore 1928, as she cites them). Once the vowel has weakened, *-um* is no longer distinct from the common *-an* ending. ^[extracted]

[[sources/kranenburg-2019-dative]] states the path as *-um* then *-an* then *-e*. He says Wulfstan’s *Sermo Lupi ad Anglos*, about 1014, alternates dative plurals in *-um* and *-an*. He quotes a 1001 Chronicle passage with *scipan* where a dative plural is expected, and later Peterborough annals with *-e*. ^[extracted]

So *-an* beside *-um* is reported for late West Saxon prose, including Wulfstan, and the vowel weakening that makes the endings confusable is reported earlier in Kentish. A counted dative-plural sample for Mercian or Northumbrian was not in the opened texts. “Shared across dialects” is not established for *-an* as the dative plural. Kentish participates in the vowel story. It was not shown, in these texts, to use *-an* for the dative plural as a regular late ending.

### Verbal *-an*, the subjunctive, and the infinitive

The present plural subjunctive is historically *-en*. The infinitive is *-an*. The question was whether late writing uses *-an* for that subjunctive.

[[sources/armborst-1977-unstressed-endings]] works from Malone, Moore, Marckwardt, and Campbell on Beowulf and on eleventh-century prose. The ending he can document as *-on* or *-an* in place of *-en* is the **preterite** plural subjunctive. He treats that as early analogical leveling of indicative *-on* into the subjunctive, and he reports Campbell §377 and §735(e) for *-an* as a possible Kentish spelling of that leveled ending, not as a reverse spelling caused only by identical vowels. ^[extracted]

On the infinitive, the same article reports Marckwardt’s counts: 35 infinitives in *-en* against 1,458 in *-an* across fifteen texts, with a calculable proportion only in the latest text, about 10 percent. The infinitive stays *-an* until very late. ^[extracted]

On the present plural subjunctive, Armborst reports Reed’s non-northern counts for retention of final *-n*: about 93 percent in early eleventh-century texts, about 85 percent in late eleventh-century texts. The present plural is losing *-n*. It is not, in these counts, being rewritten as *-an*. ^[extracted]

[[sources/fernandez-cuesta-langmuir-2025-northumbrian-subjunctive]] says late Northumbrian lost unstressed *-n* early enough that the subjunctive and the infinitive fall together in form. Their West Saxon table, cited from Hogg and Fulk 2011: 214, still separates *drifen* (present plural subjunctive) from the infinitive. The Northumbrian table is a spread of vowels (*drifa*, *drife*, and others), not a single *-an* rule. ^[extracted]

What the opened studies support is confusion among *-en*, *-on*, and *-an* in the preterite plural, with a Kentish association for *-an*, plus late loss of *-n*, sharpest in Northumbrian, so that subjunctive and infinitive can look alike because the consonant is gone. A general late rule “present plural subjunctive is spelled *-an*” is not reported.

### Weak class II *-ige* and *-ie*

[[sources/fernandez-cuesta-langmuir-2025-northumbrian-subjunctive]] prints the West Saxon present subjunctive of class II as *lufige*, *lufigen*, again from Hogg and Fulk 2011: 214. Their Northumbrian cell includes *lufiga*, *lufage*, *lufie*, *lufaga*, *lufaige*, *lufega*, and *lufege*. ^[extracted]

[[sources/ramirez-perez-2023-weak-class-ii]] studies a different piece of the same class: loss of the *-i-* formative in tenth-century late Northumbrian glosses (Lindisfarne, Owun’s Rushworth glosses, and the Durham Ritual). He argues that this loss is already visible there, against accounts that place it in Middle English, and that Farman’s Rushworth glosses are Mercian and were left out. ^[extracted]

No opened study says early West Saxon wrote *-ie* and late West Saxon replaced it with *-ige*. The handbook form cited for West Saxon is already *-ige*. Northumbrian writes both *-ie* and spellings with *-ig-*, among others. The early-versus-late contrast asked about remains not reported. The dialect contrast, West Saxon handbook *-ige* beside a Northumbrian scatter that includes *lufie*, is what the table states.

### *sylf*

[[sources/zimmermann-2012-old-english-self]] separates reflexive, intensifying, and reinforced *self*. Reflexive *self* occurs with a pronoun that is an object coreferential with the subject, and it alternates with the bare pronoun, so *self* is optional. His pair is Ælfric: *hine sylfne* beside *hine* with no *sylf*. The Wulfstan sentence he quotes, *he sceal losian sylf*, is the intensifier, “he shall be lost himself,” not an object reflexive. ^[extracted]

[[sources/vezzosi-2003-himself]] says Old English has no dedicated reflexive. The personal pronoun marks both coreference and disjoint reference. *self* is an intensifier. She included Wulfstan’s homilies in a corpus whose point is that the dedicated pronoun + *self* reflexive develops in Middle English, when written English is under pressure, and is established by Early Modern English. ^[extracted]

[[sources/goralczyk-2013-chronicle-self]] reads Chronicle manuscript A the same way: plain pronouns carry reflexive readings, including with verbs of motion and fear, and *self* is an inflected adjective used for emphasis. ^[extracted]

None of these studies counts *sylf* in Wulfstan against *The Battle of Maldon*, or reports a rise inside Old English. The explicit form is available early. The bare pronoun still does the reflexive work. The change they date as grammaticalization sits in Middle English.

### What is shared, and what is northern

Two late Northumbrian changes are each argued from primary material, not from a West Saxon handbook alone: loss of final unstressed *-n*, which collapses subjunctive and infinitive ([[sources/fernandez-cuesta-langmuir-2025-northumbrian-subjunctive]]), and loss of the class II *-i-* formative ([[sources/ramirez-perez-2023-weak-class-ii]]). Ramírez Pérez says this dialect is closer to Middle English grammar than other Old English dialects of the same century. That is a period change inside Northumbrian, not a change shared by the late West Saxon standard.

Unstressed-vowel weakening is the change with the widest report: Kentish charters in the ninth century, West Saxon *-um* in the eleventh, Wulfstan’s *-um*/*-an* alternation, and the verbal endings Armborst reviews. It is one phonological story with several morphological effects. It is not one new ending that every dialect adopts on the same date.

## Contradictions and open questions

Armborst warns that *-an* for *-en* in the preterite subjunctive should not be read as proof that the vowels had already merged. Campbell, as he cites him, allows an early analogical and possibly Kentish explanation. Walters’s eleventh-century coalescence of unstressed vowels is a later stage of a longer weakening. Both can be true if analogy and Kentish spelling come first and a general merger later. This review does not collapse them into one event.

Early and late West Saxon are treated as successive layers in the handbook rule Condorelli quotes, and as not lineally descended in the caveat he quotes from Hoad. The spelling correspondence can hold either way. A claim that late West Saxon “developed out of” Alfredian spelling is stronger than these texts.

Open:

- Mercian early and late cells for every primary feature.
- A counted dative plural for Kentish and Northumbrian.
- Whether any early West Saxon witness prefers *-ie* and any late one prefers *-ige* in class II.
- A count of *sylf* in Wulfstan against Maldon or against earlier prose.
- The 64 relevant OpenAlex titles that were not opened, including Adamczyk 2008 on Anglian *i*-stems, Wood 2019 on the Lindisfarne subjunctive, and Rodríguez Ledesma 2022 on late Northumbrian *-s*. [[concepts/old-english-orthographic-variation]] already uses Adamczyk for the Anglian *i*-stem plural. That page was not re-checked against the article in this pass.

## Review notes

The strongest objection is that a capped export and a handful of opened texts cannot represent the grammars. That objection holds for coverage. It does not license filling the empty cells. Certainty is low for any “shared across dialects” claim, and moderate only for the West Saxon ⟨ie⟩/⟨y⟩ correspondence, the late West Saxon *-um*/*-an* alternation in the witnesses named, and the late Northumbrian verbal simplifications those two studies describe.
