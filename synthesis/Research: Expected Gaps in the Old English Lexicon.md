# Research: Expected Gaps in the Old English Lexicon

Research checked on 23 October 2026. The question was what academic work, using the life of people in England from about 670 to 1100, has asked which words or meaning classes ought to appear in Old English and do not, given a surviving lexicon of about 32,000 words.

No such inventory was found. The figure of about 32,000 matches published counts of **attested forms**, not a reconstruction of the spoken language. What the scholarship does provide is a set of domain studies, a thesaurus of what was written, and a method for recovering some missing forms from names. Those studies agree that large areas of ordinary work are thinly recorded. They do not agree that every form found only in a place-name can be put back into the common lexicon.

## The 32,000 words

[[sources/kay-2012-oe-semantics-lexicon|Kay (2012)]] reports about 34,000 separate word forms in [[entities/thesaurus-of-old-english]], and 50,700 meanings once polysemy is counted. That is the closest published figure to “about 32,000 extant words.” The [[entities/nerthus-project]] headword files, already filed at about 30,000–33,000, sit in the same band. [[sources/mohlig-falke-2015-doec|Möhlig-Falke]]’s count for the 2009 [[entities/dictionary-of-old-english-corpus]] is a different quantity: 3,033,142 running words of Old English, plus about 750,000 foreign words, mostly Latin. Earlier chat-note comparisons of these quantities remain in [[concepts/oe-lexicon-size-and-gaps]].

Kay presents 34,000 as well below a modern desk dictionary. She does not present it as the vocabulary an educated person knew, or as the vocabulary the society possessed. [[concepts/oe-expected-lexical-gaps]] keeps those quantities apart.

## What the written record is made of

The 2009 corpus, by Cameron category:

- Prose: 2,128,781 Old English words, about seven tenths of the total. The extracted table split this figure after the millions digit; this is the reading that matches the printed total of 3,033,142. ^[inferred]
- Interlinear glosses: 699,606
- Poetry: 177,480
- Glossaries: 26,598 Old English words, beside 70,511 foreign words
- Inscriptions, runic and roman: under 700 Old English words together

Möhlig-Falke’s conclusion is that this is every surviving text, and that spontaneous colloquial language is missing because those text types were not preserved. Most of the prose was translated from Latin, and literacy was for a long time primarily religious. Dialect material outside West Saxon is largely glosses to Latin.

[[sources/roberts-kay-toe-distribution|Roberts and Kay]] encode the same skew as flags on forms: rare, poetic only, glossary only, or dubious. Dubious entries include place-name reconstructions and words projected back from later English. Poetic compounds cluster in the sea, ships, battle, courage, rulers, and treasure. Law and possession are almost without poetic vocabulary. A lifestyle question that looks only at poetry will miss the prosaic lexicon, and a question that looks only at the dictionaries will still miss speech.

[[sources/kay-alexander-2015-thesauruses|Kay and Alexander]] state the interpretive rule. A large category of weapons shows what the surviving literature contains. A small category of terms of endearment “is not a reflection on the Anglo-Saxon peoples, but rather one on what writings we have of theirs that survive.” Thematic glossaries, from the eighth century, do record meaning classes — body, stones, herbs, animals, birds, fish, plants — because those were the lists used to teach Latin.

## Lifestyle domains that have been studied

### Farming

[[sources/banham-faith-2014-farms|Banham and Faith]] start from the practice and then say why the words are hard. Anglo-Saxon writers took little interest in how food was produced. The Old English management texts are *Rectitudines*, *Gerefa*, and the Ely Farming Memoranda. *Gerefa* is partly a glossary import, and much of its vocabulary is otherwise unknown. Ine’s code is the law most concerned with farming and still leaves most agrarian work unlegislated. Narratives are not about farming. Glossaries and Ælfric’s *Colloquy* are used because the agricultural vocabulary is sparsely attested and often obscure. Charter bounds and place-names supply crops and land use. Archaeology carries the description where the lexicon does not. The book mentions words unique to place-names. It does not list the unattested ones.

### Plants

[[sources/biggam-2013-plant-name-studies|Biggam]] is the clearest statement that a meaning class in daily use is under-recorded. From the seventh century to about 1100, plant-names reach us through manuscripts written by ecclesiastics. The agricultural population’s regional names are lost. Some recorded names translate Latin and may never have been village words: *hundes heafod* for *canis caput* is her example. She judges that only a small proportion of Old English plant-names has been recorded, by comparison with later folk-names (93 recorded English names for the foxglove). One species could have many names, and one descriptive name could mean different plants in different districts. The attested collection is Bierbaumer’s three volumes, not a reconstruction of the unnamed residue.

She also marks a false gap. *Tulip* and *potato* have no Old English entries because the plants were not there. And she marks a gap in practice rather than in plant-names: herbals describe the plant in a remedy, while healed skeletons show that fractures were set and splinted, a procedure the herbal wording does not record.

### Landscape, animals, and the naming system

[[sources/hough-2010-toponymicon-lexicon|Hough]] and the Gelling–Cole landscape work are the studies that recover **forms**, not just thin classes. Names and bounds preserve words for landforms, plants, animals, and farming that prose does not. Gelling and Cole, as quoted in later work, argue that the several words for hill or valley were a precise system. Hough’s own proposals include animal meanings found in names (*wearg*, *brūn* as a pig, *pohha*/*pocca*, *pur*) and a catalogue of plant-names attested only in Anglo-Saxon place-names.

Her 2010 lecture then refuses a simple restoration. Some elements may have belonged to a toponymic register. *Cumb* is frequent in names and bounds and, on the *Dictionary of Old English* evidence she cites, absent from other Old English. She treats it as a name-word that became an ordinary noun centuries later. [[concepts/toponymic-register]] files both halves of this argument. The planned handlist of Old English words unattested outside place-names was not found as a finished publication. *The Vocabulary of English Place-Names* had reached *Cock-pit* when this review checked the project page.

### Dress and metalwork

[[sources/owen-crocker-1979-wynflaed|Owen-Crocker]] finds many garment-names and very little description of ordinary clothing. Some names occur only in glossaries or translations and may never have been in common use. The vocabulary is drawn mainly from the last two centuries of the period; earlier dress is rebuilt from graves and pictures. [[sources/coatsworth-pinder-2002-metalworking|Coatsworth and Pinder]] find terms for some materials, tools, techniques, and workers, flagged when they are poetic only or gloss only, and no text whose job is to describe a process. Verbs of heating and melting, needed for metal, are generally attested for food and medicine. Archaeology shows the workshops. Neither study prints the words that must have existed and left no trace. They print, or describe, the words that survive in the wrong kinds of text.

### Speech after contact

Inside the same centuries, Kay notes that Norse loans transmitted in daily speech in the north and east are only thinly present in written Old English and turn up in early Middle English. Absence from the Old English dictionaries is, for that layer, a delay in writing, documented by the later record. See [[concepts/danelaw]].

## What was not found

- A monograph or article that reconstructs household, farm, craft, and church life from 670 to 1100 and then lists expected lexemes absent from Clark Hall, Bosworth–Toller, the Thesaurus, or the *Dictionary of Old English*.
- A completed handlist of name-only Old English words covering the whole alphabet.
- A statistical estimate, in the academic sources read here, of how many lifestyle words are unseen. Hapax estimators were already noted as a method in [[concepts/oe-lexicon-size-and-gaps]]. They were not found applied to this lifestyle question.
- An Old English counterpart to the *Bilingual Thesaurus of Everyday Life in Medieval England* (building, farming, food preparation, manufacture, trade, travel by water). That thesaurus classifies Middle English and Anglo-French occupational vocabulary. Its existence shows how such a meaning-class inventory can be built. It does not, in the pages read here, explain an Old English gap list. ^[inferred]

The *Bilingual Thesaurus* project and Sylvester, Marcus, and Ingham’s 2016 methods paper were checked far enough to fix the period and the domains, and no further.

## Contradictions and open questions

**Name-only words.** Hough’s earlier work, and the 1995 database plan, treat words attested only in place-names as supplements to the literary lexicon, especially for everyday surroundings. Her 2010 lecture treats some of those elements as possibly never lexical. Both can be true of different words. A single “missing words” file that promotes every asterisked element would ignore the later argument.

**Glossary words.** Roberts and Kay keep glossary hapaxes because they matter for the history of the vocabulary. Biggam, Banham, Owen-Crocker, and Coatsworth and Pinder all warn that a glossary equivalent can be a schoolroom translation, unknown outside that list. Presence in the 34,000 forms is not proof of community use. Absence is not proof that the thing had no name.

**Rich category, important thing.** Kay and Alexander separate cultural importance from lexical richness in the surviving corpus. Warfare looks over-lexicalized because of poetry. Endearment looks under-lexicalized because of what was copied. Farming looks under-lexicalized for a stronger reason: the historians of the practice say the writers were not interested, and the texts that do list tools borrow from glossaries.

**Volume number.** Möhlig-Falke’s article is *Neuphilologische Mitteilungen* (2015), pages 395–420, as printed on the PDF. A citing paper’s volume “15” was not adopted.

**Unread primary books.** Gelling and Cole’s *Landscape of Place-Names*, Bierbaumer’s plant-name volumes, the Coatsworth and Pinder word-lists, Owen-Crocker’s thesis inventory, and Banham and Faith beyond the introduction were not read. Claims about them are limited to what later authors quote or what publisher abstracts state.
