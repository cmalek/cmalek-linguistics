# Research: The Battle of Maldon

## Overview

*The Battle of Maldon* is a late Old English alliterative fragment on the death of Ealdorman Byrhtnoth at Maldon in August 991, during the renewed Viking attacks of Æthelred’s reign. The unique manuscript, Cotton Otho A. xii, burned in 1731. What we read is David Casley’s transcript, printed by Thomas Hearne in 1726. The poem’s distinctive art is a run of formal speeches: a tribute challenge and Byrhtnoth’s refusal, then, after the flight, a chain of vows that end in Byrhtwold’s “mind must be the harder.” The fragment is heroic verse about a real defeat. Readings of *ofermod*, and of the poem’s purpose, diverge.

## Key Findings

- The fight is the 991 Maldon campaign. Chronicle versions and Richard Marsden’s reader place Byrhtnoth’s death and the first large tribute in that year. Obits split between 10 and 11 August; a Winchester calendar of about 1030 gives 11 August ([WarHistory.org](https://warhistory.org/article/the-battle-of-maldon-i)). Northey Island in the Blackwater is the usual site in teaching accounts and in Wilcox’s 1996 abstract. Glenn Foard’s 2003 battlefield paper, already on [[references/battle-of-maldon]], still says the site is a landscape argument and not a confirmed findspot. ^[ambiguous]
- Simon Keynes, in the opening of “A Lost Cartulary of St Albans Abbey,” *Anglo-Saxon England* 22 (1993), DOI [10.1017/s0263675100004403](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0263675100004403), names the poem with Asser’s *Life of Alfred* as a text lost in the Cotton fire of 23 October 1731, both from Cotton Otho A. xii, known now from early modern transcripts. [[references/karasawa-2025-griffith-maldon|Karasawa]] specifies the witness: Casley’s transcript, Bodleian Rawlinson B. 203, fols. 7–12, and Hearne’s 1726 print, pp. 570–77. Griffith, as reviewed, thinks Casley more reliable than his reputation, and he also uses quotations from a lost Price transcript.
- The poem already lacked its beginning and its end before the fire. [[references/jones-1990-lexical-structure-maldon|Jones]] reconstructs one lost opening leaf and a short lost ending from his lexical pattern. That leaf-count is his proposal.
- [[concepts/people-named-in-maldon|The named Englishmen]] are a hearth-troop plus men of the host. Byrhtnoth and Ælfwine’s grandfather Ealhhelm are the secure historical matches. Most other names are known from the poem alone. Óláfr Tryggvason is a chronicle candidate, and [[references/neubauer-2019-olaf-maldon|Neubauer]] argues he should not be read into the unnamed Viking messenger.
- Purpose is an open critical question, set out under Contradictions. The speeches themselves are the poem’s instrument: formulas, gestures, hall-*beot* remembered on the field, and dialect coloring inside the spoken lines. See [[concepts/heroic-speech-in-maldon]].
- Griffith 2024, reviewed by Karasawa and by [[references/thomas-2026-griffith-maldon|Daniel Thomas]], is now the large critical edition, aimed at the poetry. Scragg 1981 remains the previous standard. The historical dossier is still [[references/scragg-1991-maldon-essays|the 1991 essay volume]].

## Core Concepts

- [[concepts/ofermod]] — the one-word crux at the causeway, from Tolkien’s rebuke to Griffith’s “pride that keeps a promise.”
- [[concepts/heroic-speech-in-maldon]] — the herald’s demand, Byrhtnoth’s answer, and the vow-chain after the flight.
- [[concepts/people-named-in-maldon]] — who the fragment names, and how far the records identify them.
- [[concepts/viking-age-expansion]] — the wider raids of which 991 is one campaign.
- [[concepts/tolkien-old-english]] — Tolkien’s *Homecoming* is the modern reading that fixed *ofermod* as excessive pride for a generation of critics.

## Entities

- [[entities/byrhtnoth]] — ealdorman of Essex, the dead commander.
- [[entities/aethelred-the-unready]] — the king whose land Byrhtnoth says he holds; absent from the field.
- [[entities/olaf-tryggvason]] — later king of Norway; a disputed name in the 991 annals, absent from the poem.
- [[references/locherbie-cameron-1991-men-named|Locherbie-Cameron]] is the scholar of the named men and of Byrhtnoth’s family. The chapters are still unread. Miller’s 2013 thesis supplies her page-cited judgments.

## Contradictions and Open Questions

- ***Ofermod*.** Tolkien and the Aerobics introduction hear a blunder and overmastering pride. Halbrooks hears deliberate praise and blame. Kirby’s abstract rejects censure. Griffith’s edition, in two reviews, refuses sinful pride. Karasawa reports “self-respect,” and *landes to fela* as “too many Vikings.” Thomas reports a change of strategy, Viking guile, and an emotional rather than moral *landes to fela*. The edition itself was not in hand, so the two paraphrases stay unresolved. ^[ambiguous]
- **Purpose.** Niles’s abstract: the poem glorifies the death in order to argue for paying tribute. Marsden’s reader: the speeches are a poor national rally, and the aim may be local memory of Byrhtnoth. Jones: there is no encomium, the heroism is ironical, and the poem is the elegy of a country whose traditions gave no answer. Locherbie-Cameron’s 1995 *Parergon* opening, seen only in a search snippet, pairs nationalistic propaganda with a eulogy for the magnate, on the model of the tapestry his widow commissioned. These can overlap. They are not one finding. ^[ambiguous]
- **Date of the poem.** Thomas, reporting Griffith: no conclusive linguistic date; probably an Essex or south-eastern audience soon, or soon-ish, after the battle. The WarHistory essay argues from the spelling *Byrhtnoth* (a late tenth-century window between *Beorhtnoth* and *Brihtnoth*) and from armour described in a way that fits before 1008. Both can be true. Griffith’s “no conclusive date” is the more cautious linguistic claim. ^[ambiguous]
- **Viking command.** Chronicle E, via Foard, names Olaf and 93 ships. Neubauer says that does not make Óláfr the poem’s antagonist. Sveinn is widely doubted. Justin and Guthmund in John of Worcester may echo 994.
- **Wistan’s second name.** Insley 2026 emends the transcript’s *wigelines* and identifies a baptized Scandinavian. The article was not read, and teaching texts already print *Wigelm*. ^[ambiguous]
- **Locherbie-Cameron 1991.** Google Scholar, OpenAlex, and Crossref still do not yield the chapter. Miller’s open Trent thesis now supplies page-cited judgments, filed on [[concepts/people-named-in-maldon]] and [[references/locherbie-cameron-1991-men-named]]: Ælfric Cild as a probable father of Ælfwine (p. 241), Offa as likely nephew of Bishop Theodric (p. 246), Byrhtwold as the Donyland *cniht* (p. 243), two origins for Maccus (p. 239), an Anglo-Danish Ecglaf for Æscferth (pp. 239, 242), and the fugitive Godrics expected to vanish (p. 245). Each of those is her “probable,” or one of two options, as Miller reports it. Scragg’s 1981 edition, Griffith’s 2024 edition, and Tolkien’s essay in full remain unread.
