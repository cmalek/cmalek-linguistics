# Research: Old English G-Palatalization

## Overview

Old English *g* (the Germanic voiced velar fricative [ɣ], and the stop [ɡ] after *n* or in geminates) shifted toward the palate when a front vowel or *j* stood next to it in the same syllable. The result was [j] for the fricative and, later, an affricate for the stop. [[references/hogg-1992-palatalization|Hogg 1992]] §§7.15–17 is now the rule used here: fricatives palatalize before any front vowel; stops, except at the start of the word, only before /i/ or /j/. The change ran before i-umlaut, so front vowels created by i-mutation did not trigger it. The working stop list is in [[concepts/old-english-g-palatalization]].

## Key Findings

### Mechanism

- Assimilation to a neighbouring front vowel or glide. The triggers are reducible to "close proximity of a front vowel and/or the glide /j/", and the process was blocked next to a back vowel or another consonant ([[references/kijak-2022-velar-palatalization]]).
- Intermediate stages are reconstructed, not attested. The usual path is [ɣ] → [ʝ] → [j], and [ɡ(ɡ)] → [ɡj] → [ɟ(ɟ)] → [dʒ] (Campbell 1959: 176, as cited by Kijak). Kijak declines to rule on the exact phonetics.
- Three sources of the geminate or post-nasal palatal: West Germanic gemination before *j* (*\*agja* → *ecg*), *-nj-* (*\*sangjan* → *sengan*), and contact with a front vowel ([[references/kijak-2022-velar-palatalization]]).
- Wikipedia's phonological history gives the same triggers: /i, iː, j/ before; after front vowels *e, ē, æ, ǣ* unless a back vowel follows ([[references/wikipedia-old-english-phonology]]).

### Chronology

- After a-restoration, before i-mutation. *galan* stays velar because a-restoration had already restored the back vowel; *gēs*, *gylden*, *cyning* stay velar because the front vowel appeared later ([[references/wikipedia-old-english-phonology]]).
- Breaking vs. palatalization ordering is unsettled (Howell 1991, as cited by Wikipedia).
- Traditional dating puts it in the continental or very early insular period. Kluge argued it needed an inflectional *i* that was later lost, so it predates the Angles' separation from other West Germanic dialects. Siebs, Luick and Campbell agreed ([[references/van-der-hoek-2010-palatalization-west-germanic]]). Hogg (1979), Stiles and Nielsen disagree; Fulk (1998) and Laker (2008) reopen a "soft" Anglo-Frisian view.
- Phonemic split: traditionally early (Penzl 1947: i-mutation plus loss of final vowels turned allophones into phonemes). Minkova (2003) puts the split of [ɣ] into /j/ and /ɡ/ in the mid tenth century, based on alliteration (*gēar* with *gold*) ([[references/kijak-2022-velar-palatalization]]).
- Affrication to [dʒ] is later than palatalization. Stenbrenden (2019) argues a stem-initial voiced affricate did not appear until after the Conquest.

### Why some words escaped

1. Back vowel in the next syllable (*dagas*, *wegas*).
2. i-mutation came later (*gēs*, *gylden*).
3. Analogy and levelling, and depalatalization before a consonant after syncope (*sēċiþ* → *sēcþ*).
4. Scandinavian replacement (*give*, *get*). Matsuse (2016) argues this is better explained by social selection than by sound change; Wikipedia and Etymologist.ai give the traditional Norse account ([[references/matsuse-2016-palatal-softening]]).
5. Northumbrian: much less palatalization.

### Phonemic status in practice

By Late West Saxon /ɡ/ and /j/ were almost certainly separate phonemes. Near-minimal pairs are few (*gēs* ~ *ġē*, *ġēotan* ~ *guton*) ([[references/wikipedia-old-english-phonology]]).

## Contradictions & Open Questions

- **Early vs. late phonemicization.** Penzl/Hogg (early) against Minkova (tenth century). Unresolved; Kijak's own account sides with the late dating.
- **Was palatalization before umlaut a phonetic fact?** Kijak: velars before umlauted vowels were never palatalized, because the velar and the rounded vowel share a labial-velar component. The traditional account needs "palatalize, affricate, then umlaut". van der Hoek rejects Penzl's allophone model outright.
- **Unstressed *e*.** Hogg §7.16 n2 treats a following unstressed /e/ like /æ/: fricatives palatalize, stops do not. *Hnīge* and *stīge* match the fricative case. A stop before that *e* (*folgere*) stays velar. *Āgen* is unsettled, because the spelling *e* may be later than the rule. ^[ambiguous]
- **Vowel + ġ + consonant.** Probably not depalatalized (cf. *līst*); consonant + ġ + consonant (*myrgþ*, *bierġst*) is unresolved.
- **Scandinavian cause.** Hogg §7.42 names dialect *birk*, *brig*, *rig*, *steek*, *weg*, plus *carve* and *skin*, and cites Campbell §438. §7.15 n4 names *gate* < *ġeat*. *Guest* is Gersum's Old Norse *gestr* (or a Scandinavianized *giest*). *Gift* in the sense "present" is Etymonline's Old Norse *gift*; Old English *gift* is the bride-price, and the ordinary word for a present is *ġiefu*. *Give* and *get* are still the received Norse account, and they are not in the Hogg sections read. *Gate* "road" is a separate *\*gatwōn* word; Gersum and Versloot, citing Hogg and Fulk §3.116, treat a missing palatal as weak evidence of a loan.
- **Source gaps.** Hogg 1979 (not open on Unpaywall) and Campbell 1959 §§426–430 were not read. Hogg reports Campbell §§428–9 and 435 as the stops-palatalize-then-revert view. Hogg and Fulk 2011 was used only where Gersum and Versloot quote §3.116. Minkova 2003 and Laker 2008 remain unread. Follow-up in [[_meta/todos]].
