# Old English after 1066 × Alfred the Great

## The Connection

[[concepts/old-english-after-1066]] follows later uses of the language; [[entities/alfred-the-great]] follows a king whose reputation became useful to later communities. External research shows that these histories intersect without moving together. A celebrated king can circulate through Latin and Icelandic narratives even where his Old English texts are not being read. ^[inferred]

## Where They Co-occur

The original scan found three shared backlinks: [[concepts/history-of-english]], [[concepts/old-english]], and [[references/battle-of-maldon]]. The last records a manuscript containing both Maldon and Asser’s Alfred biography. Such association identifies a transmission context, not common authorship or purpose.

## External Evidence

Firth’s 2024 study locates medieval uses of “the Great” in English and Icelandic historical writing, including Roger of Wendover, Matthew Paris, *Egils saga*, and *Landnámabók*. Elizabethan scholars therefore inherited an existing tradition. Indexed passages also connect Parker’s 1574 Asser edition with that later reception. See [[sources/firth-2024-alfred-the-great]].

Library evidence supplies a specific doctrinal witness distinct from Alfred: *A Testimonie of Antiquitie* prints Ælfric’s sacramental homily with translation. The British Library records episcopal authentication; Kansas dates its copy [1566?], while the British Library gives [1567]. See [[sources/testimonie-antiquitie-library-evidence]].

## Cross-cutting Insight

There are at least three routes through this material: preservation and use of Old English texts; transmission of a story about a learned king; and selection of an old church text as evidence in a later controversy. Their convergence creates the appearance of one continuous national programme. Tracing them separately shows which continuity is actually evidenced. ^[inferred]

Ælfric’s homily makes the distinction concrete. Its usefulness as doctrinal precedent does not require Alfred to have written or sponsored it. Conversely, medieval Icelandic recognition of Alfred does not demonstrate reading of his vernacular translations. A claim about reputation cannot silently become a claim about textual access. ^[inferred]

## Tensions and Trade-offs

This corrects the first draft’s disproportionate emphasis on Reformation selection. Later reuse did not create Alfred’s reputation from nothing. Yet the existence of an earlier byname does not prove an unchanged interpretation of his rule. ^[inferred]

Firth’s abstract and indexed passages were available; the full PDF was not retrieved. The library descriptions establish bibliographical witnesses, not a complete account of editorial intervention. No count of how often reformers invoked Alfred rather than Ælfric has been established.

## Strongest Objection

The three routes are analytical distinctions imposed on communities that may themselves have joined king, language, and church history. Separating them is useful only if it reveals an actual difference in their sources or reception.

> test: in a defined set of early modern prefaces, do references to Alfred, quotations of Alfredian prose, and quotations of Ælfric occur together or in different argumentative contexts?

## Open Questions

- Across specified editions and prefaces from 1560–1625, how do royal reputation, access to Old English texts, and doctrinal citation distribute?
