# Research: The Great Vowel Shift

Research checked on 30 October 2026, in deep-research quick mode, and filed with the wiki research loop. Three rounds. The question was what the shift was, when it happened, which stage of English was being spoken, what caused it, and what it did to the language. This page was produced with AI-assisted research. Claims below are tied to the sources that were actually opened. Jespersen 1909, Luick’s grammar, Stockwell and Minkova 1988, Lass’s Cambridge History chapter, and Krug’s later handbook chapters were not read.

## Overview

The Great Vowel Shift is the reorganization of the long stressed vowels that turned late medieval values into the long vowels and diphthongs of standard English. Otto Jespersen gave it that name in 1909. On the dating still used in handbooks, it begins around 1400, in late Middle English, and continues through Early Modern English, with the last standard outcomes in the eighteenth century. Old English is earlier. Modern English is the language that shows the result.

The cause is not known. Drag-chain, push-chain, and “not one event” are all live positions. What is secure is the mismatch everyone notices: printing fixed a late Middle English spelling while the long vowels kept moving.

## Key Findings

- The shift raises the long monophthongs that had room to rise, and diphthongizes /iː/ and /uː/. [[sources/schlueter-2012-emode-phonology]] states that chain and says its mechanics are still debated. [[sources/oed-periods-and-great-vowel-shift]] illustrates it with *mite*, *meet*, *mate*, *mouth*, and *moot*.
- OED3 dates Old English before 1150, Middle English 1150–1500, and early modern English after 1500. [[sources/nevalainen-2017-early-modern-english]] uses 1500–1700 for Early Modern English and calls the cut conventional. The *Cambridge History* window she cites is 1476–1776.
- The handbook dating of the named shift is about 1400 to about 1750 ([[sources/stenbrenden-2016-long-vowel-shifts]]). Schlüter puts the first stage in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, the *meet*/*meat* merger around 1700, and standard British /aɪ/ and /aʊ/ in the eighteenth century. The language of that span is late Middle English, then Early Modern English.
- [[sources/ikegashira-2020-stenbrenden-review]] reports Stenbrenden’s conclusion that there was no single Great Vowel Shift, only overlapping raising and diphthongization from about the tenth century into the sixteenth or seventeenth. Her book’s own introduction frames the traditional shift as the later bundle inside a study that starts about 1050.
- [[sources/prichard-2014-northern-gvs]] finds no consensus on cause. She argues from northern dialect geography for Luick’s push-chain. Jespersen’s drag-chain remains the better-known order. [[sources/krug-2003-gvs-inception]] offers one drag-chain trigger, glide insertion in *I am* and *thou art*, and says the liaison itself is not directly attested.
- Stockwell and Minkova (1988) call the unified shift “the linguist’s creation through hindsight,” as quoted by Prichard. The 1988 article was not opened. The title and pages are in [[entities/donka-minkova]]’s own bibliography.
- The audible and visible result is a new long-vowel system plus a spelling that still looks late medieval. The OED dates the fixing of printed spelling to about 1475–1630. The chain itself did not merge *meet* and *meat*; that merger is later. *Great*, *break*, and *steak* stayed with the lower vowel. Far northern English kept a monophthong for Middle English *ū*.
- Silent consonants such as the *k* of *knight* are early modern losses listed by the OED next to the vowel shift. They are not the definition of the shift.
- *Foot*/*feet* is Old English i-mutation, already filed under [[concepts/germanic-sound-change]]. It is a different change, centuries earlier.

## Contradictions & Open Questions

These are disagreements in the scholarship, left standing after a third-round check. Another search would not pick a winner.

- **One shift or many.** Handbooks still draw one chain (Schlüter). Stenbrenden, via Ikegashira, denies a unique process. Stockwell and Minkova, via Prichard, say the unity is hindsight. Prichard keeps a chain and explains ragged northern outcomes as diffusion.
- **Start date.** Conventional start around 1400 (Stenbrenden’s report of the usual view; Krug). Harvard’s teaching page says the twelfth century, with the main effects in the fifteenth and early sixteenth. Stenbrenden’s project starts about 1050 and her reviewer summarizes a tenth-century beginning. The tenth- versus eleventh-century wording is the review against the book’s title and introduction. ^[ambiguous]
- **Order and cause.** Drag chain (Jespersen; Krug, with an explicit evidence gap), push chain (Luick; Prichard’s dialect maps), or simultaneous change (Jordan and Crook, as Ikegashira reports them). No source opened here identifies a phonetic cue that the others accept.
- **Diphthong quality on the way to /aɪ/ and /aʊ/.** Schlüter reports the centered reconstruction and Lass’s lower first element as both current. The eighteenth-century standard endpoints are less controversial than the sixteenth-century steps.
- **Period boundary.** A merger dated “around 1700” falls at the end of Nevalainen’s Early Modern English and at the start of what her bibliography tradition calls Late Modern English. Lass’s 1776 end, as she cites it, would still call that merger early modern. The labels are conventional.

Unread, and still needed before a cause can be treated as settled: Jespersen 1909, Luick 1896 and 1914–40, Stockwell and Minkova 1988, Stenbrenden’s spelling chapters, and Krug’s handbook chapter.
