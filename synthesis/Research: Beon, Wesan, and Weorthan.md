# Research: Beon, Wesan, and Weorthan

## Overview

Old English has three verbs in the “be” area, and they are not three tenses of one verb. *Wesan* supplies the ordinary present *eom, eart, is, sind* and the only past, *wæs, wǣron*. *Bēon* supplies a second present, *bēo, bist, biþ, bēoþ*, for situations that are generic, repeated, durative, or still to come. *Weorþan* means “become” or “happen.” It marks the entry into a new state, including many passives, and it is gone by late Middle English. Spanish *ser* and *estar* are a genuine two-copula parallel. They do not line up with *bēon* and *wesan* context by context.

## Key Findings

- ***Is* is the particular present.** It identifies a specific person or thing and describes a situation viewed without its boundaries. *Hē is wiga* is the classroom case. Petré and Cuyckens, following Kilpiö (1993), say identifying clauses are rare with *bēon* ([[references/petre-cuyckens-2009-copulas]], [[concepts/beon-and-wesan]]).
- ***Bēon* is the marked present: generic, iterative, durative, or future.** *Til biþ se þe his trēowe gehealdeþ*, *wē bēoð oft costode*, *þā hwīle þe hē hēr on līfe biþ*, and *on þǣm æfteran dæge biþ gehyred*. Gnomic *wyrd bið ful āræd* belongs here. Calling *bið* only a future, as Mitchell and Robinson do before they mention the gnomic use, leaves the eternal truths out ([[references/bradley-2011-two-copulas]]). The future-tense survey is [[synthesis/Research: Future Tense in Old English]].
- ***Weorþan* is the transition.** Become merciful, become abbess, an earthquake occurs, the gates become shut. It is not generic. Present *weorþan* is almost always future, because a perfective present does not describe an ongoing now. In a York corpus sample, stative passives are entirely *wesan/bēon* (zero *weorþan* across several hundred states). Telic eventive passives are both, and *wesan/bēon* is still about 73 percent of them ([[references/jones-macleod-2017-oe-passive]], [[concepts/weorthan]]).
- **The stative-versus-dynamic textbook rule is too clean.** *Weorþan* really does avoid states. It does not own events. Mitchell’s free-variation claim and Kilpiö’s actional-only claim are both partly kept by the 2017 counts.
- ***Ser* and *estar* share the fact of two copulas and not the division of labour.** *Ser* takes both particular identity (*es un guerrero*) and generic essence (*un guerrero es fuerte*). Old English puts identity on *is* and the generic on *biþ*. A future temporary state (*iċ bēo gearo sōna*) is *bēon* in Old English and *estar* in Spanish (*estaré listo*). A resultant “they are shut” is *wesan* plus participle, and Spanish *estar* plus participle. The change itself is *weorþan*, which Spanish assigns to *ponerse, hacerse, volverse*, or *llegar a ser* ([[concepts/ser-estar-and-old-english-be]]). ^[inferred]
- **The historical comparison scholars actually argue is Celtic.** Wischer’s 2010 abstract and Bradley’s thesis derive the double present from British Celtic, where a copula and a substantive verb (related to Latin *stāre*) divide classification from current existence. Bradley mentions *ser* and *estar* only as a living two-copula system. *Estar*’s origin in “stand, stay” echoes the older meaning of *\*wesaną* “stay,” which is not how attested *wesan* behaves.

## Contradictions and Open Questions

- **Future only, or generic too?** Smith (1896) says the *b*-forms are chiefly future. Kilpiö, Petré, and the classroom gnomic examples require generic, iterative, and durative uses as well. Both are in the texts. A future-only rule undergenerates.
- **Are the passives in free variation?** Mitchell said yes for events and claimed some stative *weorþan*. Jones and Macleod find no stative *weorþan* and a large eventive overlap, with *wesan* unmarked. The disagreement is about Mitchell’s examples, which they re-read as events.
- **Did Celtic cause the split?** Bradley and Wischer argue yes. The thesis assumes the population history and then looks for stricter contrasts in the north and west. This pass did not re-count his dialect tables.
- **No published sentence-by-sentence *ser/estar* gloss of Old English** turned up in paper search or web search. The comparison on the concept page is built from the two descriptions. The identity row and the future-temporary row are the places it breaks.
