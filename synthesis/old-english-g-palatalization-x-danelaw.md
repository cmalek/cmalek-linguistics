# Old English g-Palatalization × Danelaw

## The Connection

[[concepts/old-english-g-palatalization]] lists, as Group C of its stop list, words where Old English had a palatal *ġ* and Modern English has a velar from Old Norse: *give*, *get*, *gift*, *guest*, and *gate* (a received view). [[concepts/danelaw]] lists contact loans and the region where they concentrate. The g-page treats the Norse items as not OE exceptions; the Danelaw page treats them as lexical borrowing. Together they offer a diagnostic. ^[inferred]

## Where They Co-occur

- The g-page's dialect section says Northumbrian palatalization is weaker, "probably Scandinavian influence", and Scots *kirk* ~ *church*, *brig* ~ *bridge* show the pattern for *c* and *cg*.
- [[synthesis/danelaw-x-old-english-dialects]] already uses one such test: a sixth-century *sk* → *sh* change means later *sk* words are typically Norse.
- [[concepts/old-english-dialects]] gives the Anglian/Mercian *ġæ*, *ġe* spellings against West Saxon *ġiefan*, *ġeaf*.

## Cross-cutting Insight

A Modern English velar *g* or *k* in a word whose Old English cognate was palatal works as a loan or contact detector, parallel to the *sk* test. The g-page's warning ("do not read *give* as evidence of an OE velar") is the same fact from the opposite direction: the diagnostic and the pitfall are one observation. A reader who knows the stop-list groups can sort velar *g* in English into four sources: back-vowel environment (Group B), late i-mutation (Group A), Norse replacement (Group C), and analogy (Group D). Only Group C is a Danelaw effect. ^[inferred]

## Tensions and Trade-offs

- Circularity: Northumbrian velars are attributed to Norse influence, and Norse loans are recognised partly by velar *g* where palatal is expected. Neither supports the other independently. ^[ambiguous]
- *kirk* is both a Norse form and a northern dialect form; the vault pages do not separate loan from dialect development.
- *gate*, *guest*, and the *-iest* items rest on standard etymology "not checked in sources here" per the g-page.

## Strongest Objection

Weaker Northumbrian palatalization may predate Norse contact (an Anglian trait), in which case it is not evidence of contact at all and the diagnostic only works for loans with known Norse etymons.

> test: in Northumbrian texts written before the Great Heathen Army (865), is palatalization of g and c already weaker than in contemporary West Saxon?
