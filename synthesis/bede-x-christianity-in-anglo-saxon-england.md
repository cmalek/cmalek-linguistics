# Bede × Christianity in Anglo-Saxon England

## The Connection

[[concepts/christianity-in-anglo-saxon-england]] is a seventh-century sequence: Augustine in Kent, Aidan at Lindisfarne, then papal custom after the Synod of Whitby (664). [[entities/bede]] is a Northumbrian Benedictine (d. 735) whose *Historia ecclesiastica* is the foundation narrative for English origins and the sole narrative source for [[entities/caedmon]]. He also worked on Easter *computus*. The historian and the conversion chronology sit inside one church.

## Where They Co-occur

- [[entities/caedmon]] takes its life from Bede's *Historia*, places the hymn at Hilda's Whitby, and points at the Christianity page for that house. The hymn text in the wiki is the West Saxon Alfredian prose of Book IV.25.
- [[entities/anglo-saxons]] tells Bede's origin story (Angles, Saxons, Jutes) and calls the move from paganism to Christianity a seventh-century political process.
- [[concepts/anglo-saxon-paganism]] lists Bede among the Latin writers who were uninterested in a full portrait of the cult, and notes that he writes as if pagan practice had faded.
- [[references/passion-peter-and-paul]] still uses Bede as the glossator of a gospel place-name, in a later Old English homily.

## Cross-cutting Insight

The conversion sequence and the earliest named English poem travel as episodes of one Latin ecclesiastical history, written by a computist of the Roman-aligned Northumbrian church. ^[inferred] Whitby's Easter decision and Bede's habit of dating English events are the same institutional setting: the page on Bede records that he popularized *Anno Domini* and worked on Easter reckoning; the Christianity page records that Whitby chose Rome's Easter over Iona's. Cædmon's hymn then reaches Old English classrooms as a later West Saxon rendering of that Latin report, so the vernacular canon and the conversion plot share a pipeline even though the spelling is Alfredian.

## Tensions and Trade-offs

- Bede's Angles-Saxons-Jutes invitation, dated 450–455 on his own page, is an eighth-century synthesis read against archaeology. The conversion page's seventh-century mission narrative inherits that narrator.
- The paganism page has Bede treating offerings to demons as a fading problem a generation after Wihtred's laws (695). Weekday names and place-names on that page are a different kind of survival from cult.
- The Peter and Paul homily shows Bede's authority as a commentator long after the conversion generation. Longevity of the book is separate from accuracy of the seventh-century plot.

## Strongest Objection

Gregory's correspondence and non-Bedan lives, including the *Life of St Wilfrid* already named on the paganism page, date parts of the mission on their own. Folding Augustine, Aidan, and Whitby into "Bede's plot" over-reads a set of pages that all cite the same famous book.

> test: which of Augustine's landing (597), Aidan's Lindisfarne (634), and Whitby (664) are fixed in a contemporary letter or a non-Bedan life, and which dates appear first in the Historia?
