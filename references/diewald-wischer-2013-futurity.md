# Markers of Futurity in Old High German and Old English (Diewald and Wischer 2013)

Gabriele Diewald and Ilse Wischer, “Markers of futurity in Old High German and Old English: a comparative corpus-based study,” in *Comparative Studies in Early Germanic Languages* (Amsterdam: John Benjamins, 2013), 195–216. The file read here is the authors’ manuscript: [Diewald and Wischer 2013](https://www.gabrielediewald.de/fileadmin/_gd/downloads/Diewald_Wischer_2013_Final_Manuscript_Version.pdf).

The question is why two West Germanic languages, with cognate *werdan*, *sculan*, and *wellan*, grammaticalized different futures: German *werden* + infinitive, English *will/shall* + infinitive. Their corpus is about 80,000 words on each side, dated 790–1155, matched as far as possible for date, text type, and content. This is the same project as [[references/wischer-2006-markers-of-futurity]], now set beside Old High German.

## Key claims

- A simple future, again from Bybee, Perkins, and Pagliuca (1994: 244), is a prediction, not an obligation or a wish. In Present-Day German that marking is optional. In Present-Day English it is largely obligatory.
- Cross-linguistically, movement verbs and become-verbs are common future sources. A future built from a modal is less common. On that typology German followed a frequent path and English a rarer one.
- Their path for modal futures runs obligation, desire, or root possibility, then intention, then future.
- Old English *weorþan* is discussed in the handbooks mainly as a passive auxiliary. A present *weorþan* passive is often said to carry a future tone: Visser (1963–73) §1918, Mitchell (1985) §755, Kilpiö (1989). *Bēon* for future states and general truths is a separate literature: Kilpiö (1992, 1993, 1997), Lutz (2009), and Wischer (2010).
- Kilpiö (1992) is the Dictionary of Old English entry *BEON* (Toronto: Pontifical Institute of Mediaeval Studies), with spellings by Robert Millar from materials assembled by Haruko Momma. That fascicle is the lexicographic documentation of the *b*-forms. This research did not open the fascicle itself.
- German *werden* + infinitive, on the survey they follow (Westvik 2000), becomes a future marker in the early fourteenth century in East Middle German and Upper German, and replaces *wollen/sollen* futures by the seventeenth century. English *weorþan* dies out in late Middle English instead.

## Counts in the matched corpora

The corpus is about 80,000 words on each side. Token totals for the candidate source verbs:

| Verb | Old High German | Old English |
|---|---:|---:|
| *werdan* / *weorþan* | 682 | 274 |
| *wellan* / *willan* | 229 | 389 |
| *sculan* | 318 | 205 |
| *beon* | — | 586 |
| Total | 1,231 | 1,454 |

One OHG sermon collection, the *Speculum Ecclesiae*, supplies 122 of the German *sculan* tokens, mostly deontic, so the German *sculan* lead is partly that text.

In the Old English sample, 84% of *willan* and 96% of *sculan* are unambiguous auxiliaries with a bare infinitive. Neither verb has a non-finite form in the corpus. *Ne* fuses with *willan* (*nylle*, *nolde*) and does not fuse with *weorþan* or *wilnian*. German *wollen* and *sollen* keep an intentional source and, on their account, never become a colourless future. A Boethius slice they had already judged “closest to a mere future” is 11 *willan* (44%), 5 present indicatives, 4 *bēon*, 3 *sculan*, 1 present subjunctive, and 1 *weorþan*. That slice is the same hand-picked set as in [[references/wischer-2006-markers-of-futurity]].

Construction types, as shares of each verb’s tokens:

| Construction | OHG *werdan* | OE *weorþan* | OE *bēon* |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Intransitive | 16% | 34% | 7% |
| Copula | 17% | 22% | 52% |
| Lexical total | 33% | 56% | 59% |
| + past participle | 65% | 43% | 38% |
| + present participle | 1% | 1% | 2% |
| + *to*-infinitive | — | — | 1% |
| + bare infinitive | 2 tokens | — | — |
| Potential auxiliary | 66% | 44% | 41% |

Old English *weorþan* in this sample is past participle (118), copula (63), intransitive (91), or present participle (2). It never takes an infinitive. Old High German *werdan* does, twice, beside 441 past-participle tokens. They treat that gap, plus competition from *bēon*, as why English did not grammaticalize the become-verb and German did. They also split Bybee’s “be / have” path: a become-verb can go straight from ingressive to future, with no obligation or intention stage to bleach away.

Their own limit: language contact is not in the study.

## Limits

The file is the authors’ manuscript, not the typeset book page. The two bare-infinitive OHG tokens are real and rare. The 44% *willan* figure is a share of 25 pre-selected Boethius expressions, not of all future-referring clauses. Page numbers given for Kilpiö (1993) in secondary citations do not match one another, so none are repeated here. ^[ambiguous]
