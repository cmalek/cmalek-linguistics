# Thomas 2026 review of Griffith’s Battle of Maldon

Daniel Thomas, review of Mark Griffith, *The Battle of Maldon: A New Critical Edition* (Liverpool University Press, 2024), *JEGP* 125, no. 2 (2026), DOI [10.5406/1945662x.125.2.07](https://doi.org/10.5406/1945662x.125.2.07). The review text used here is the full text returned by OpenAlex. The journal PDF was not opened. Griffith’s edition was not read. Compare [[references/karasawa-2025-griffith-maldon]].

Thomas calls this the most sustained treatment the poem has had, nearly three times D. G. Scragg’s 1981 edition, and aimed past a first student reading. The poem occupies eleven pages. Griffith generally keeps the eighteenth-century transcript, the only witness to the destroyed manuscript, and records emendations in the commentary. Differences from Scragg are mostly punctuation, from a new reading of syntax and metrical grammar. The glossary marks poetic and prosaic words and hapax legomena and points into the *Dictionary of Old English*. The proper-name glossary summarizes what the poem says of each person and cites the Prosopography of Anglo-Saxon England. An appendix prints, as literary analogues, *Liber Eliensis*, the *Vita Sancti Oswaldi*, and the Chronicle annal for 755 (Cynewulf and Cyneheard).

## What Thomas reports from the introduction

“Text and Origins” gives a full account of the transcript and a more favorable view of its reliability than some earlier commentators, then capitalization, paragraphing, and punctuation. Heavy, apparently metrical pointing clusters on “more exciting” moments. Griffith suggests a rhetorical motive: those passages mattered to early readers (p. 9).

“Language”: slight evidence of a southern poet (p. 39), and no conclusive date (p. 26). The subject “probably indicates composition for an audience from Essex, or the south-east, sometime soon, or soon-ish, after the battle” (p. 53). The poet knew the verse tradition back at least to the time of *Beowulf*. Griffith rejects the claim that the poem prefers SVO order in non-dependent clauses (pp. 33–35) and still finds syntax that has moved on from older verse (pp. 36–37). Pronouns lead him to assume a male poet.

“Prosody”: departures from expected alliteration are treated as innovation (p. 64). The Old English system is “just about intact,” and “changes of various sorts are beginning to reshape the model” (p. 75). Unstressed syllables have affinities with Old Saxon practice (p. 69). Other features anticipate early Middle English verse (pp. 73–74).

“Style” asks whether the poem is restrained or elaborate, simple or complex, Beowulfian or something else (p. 97). Thomas’s summary: the poem sits in a transition, using older heroic habits and pointing toward later alliterative verse.

“Battle Narrative”: mass fighting uses traditional formulas. Individual combats, in syntax, prosody, lexis, and idiom, anticipate the later tradition (pp. 99–107) and “have a claim to be the first examples of Middle English alliterative poetry” (p. 105).

## The causeway and *ofermod*

Thomas’s report of “Structure and Ethos” (pp. 113–23): the defense of the causeway is a change of strategy. Byrhtnoth has already committed himself, in his first speech to the messenger, to fighting. He then defends the crossing because he has suddenly seen how large the enemy force is (p. 115). The poet never says this, and never says the English are outnumbered. Thomas reports that Griffith treats the silence as artistry (p. 116). The Vikings’ request for passage, the guile implied by *lytegian*, catches him between keeping that public word and exposing his men, or breaking the word and taking the shame. He keeps the word. The men who die because of it do not reproach him, and Griffith’s reading is that the poet does not either. *Landes to fela* (line 90a) is an emotional response, a wish that it had gone otherwise, rather than a moral verdict (pp. 118–19). *Ofermod* belongs to warrior ethics in which pride is a necessary quality of the fighting hero. The glossary defines it as “pride” (p. 293). In the language section Griffith had written that a secular poem of this kind calls for a meaning other than “sinful pride” (p. 52). Thomas then ties that other meaning to the requirement that Byrhtnoth honor his word.

## Limitations

Two reviewers of one unread edition. Karasawa’s paraphrase of the same *ofermod* pages stresses self-respect and reads *landes to fela* as an understatement of Viking numbers. Thomas’s paraphrase stresses a change of strategy, Viking guile, and an emotional rather than moral *landes to fela*. Both are reports of Griffith. The difference is recorded on [[concepts/ofermod]].
