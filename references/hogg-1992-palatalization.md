# Hogg 1992, palatalization and assibilation

Richard M. Hogg, *A Grammar of Old English*, volume 1: *Phonology* (Oxford: Blackwell, 1992; Wiley reprint 2011), chapter 7, "Palatalization and assibilation," §§7.15–43. The Wiley book is [10.1002/9781444341355](https://doi.org/10.1002/9781444341355). Sections 7.15–17, 7.23–7.32, and the Scandinavian paragraph in §7.42 were read for this pass. Richard M. Hogg and R. D. Fulk, volume 2: *Morphology* (2011), lists palatalization at its own §§18–20 and §85 in the contents. That volume's body was not in hand. Gersum and Versloot quote one morphological result, §3.116, noted below.

Hogg's 1979 article, "Old English palatalization," *Transactions of the Philological Society* 77: 89–113, DOI [10.1111/j.1467-968x.1979.tb00358.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-968x.1979.tb00358.x), is not open. Unpaywall reports no legal copy. The grammar cites it twice: pp. 93–96, that initial *g* was still a fricative when palatalization ran, and pp. 103–4, that the voiced palatal fricative merged with /j/.

## The rule (§7.16)

A velar palatalizes when it stands next to a front vowel or /j/ in the same syllable. Stops are stricter than fricatives.

- Initially, every velar palatalizes before a front vowel, or before a diphthong whose first element is front (*ċīdan*, *ġyllan*, *ċeaster*, *ġeaf*). A preceding diphthong does not do it, because its second element is back (*bēag*).
- Medially, every velar palatalizes before /i/ or /j/ (*sēċan*, *sige*). Before /æ/, only the fricatives do (*dæġes*, against *blæces*, *āces*).
- After a front vowel, fricatives palatalize unless a back vowel follows at once (*reġn*, *næġl*, against *stigan*, *wegas*). Stops in that seat palatalize only after /i/, and fail if a back vowel follows (*diċ*, *miċel*, against *dicas*, *gelicung*, and against *æcer*).
- For an ambisyllabic medial consonant, the vowel of the second syllable wins. That is why *sēċan* palatalizes and *dicas* does not.
- Finally, every velar palatalizes after /i/. After other front vowels, only the fricatives do (*dæġ*, *mǣġ*, against *frec*, *bæc*).

He records the rival view in the notes to §7.16. Campbell, *Old English Grammar* (1959), §§428–9 and 435, has the stops palatalizing in the same places as the fricatives, then reverting to velars when assibilation arrives, in order to keep *acre* and *back*. Hogg thinks the fricatives were simply more willing to palatalize. Those Campbell sections were not opened. The report is Hogg's.

## Unstressed *e* (§7.16 n2)

Unstressed /e/ is rare at the relevant date (his §6.46). He sees no reason to treat a following unstressed /e/ differently from a following /æ/. Before /æ/, fricatives palatalize and stops do not. So *hnīge* and *stīge*, with a fricative after a front vowel, fall in with the palatal cases. A stop before that *e*, as in a form of the *folgere* type, stays velar on his rule. *Āgen* is the awkward one: if its *g* was a fricative and the following vowel was already front, his rule palatalizes it. If that vowel was still back when the rule ran, and only later became *e*, it stays velar. The spelling *e* does not decide which of those two histories applied. ^[ambiguous]

## Chronology

§7.17(1), citing the 1979 article pp. 93–96: initial *g* was a fricative at the time of palatalization. A path "fricative, then stop, then palatal stop, then [j]" is the one he rejects. The change of initial unpalatalized *g* to a stop is later.

§7.32: palatalization ran before i-umlaut, and the phonemic split came later, after i-umlaut. §7.31: for the stop [g], a palatal and a velar can contrast only in gemination or after a nasal, and he finds no minimal pairs, so a phonemic split of the stop is not required on distribution alone.

§7.23: the early texts, including the Ruthwell Cross, do not show a clean dialect split in the change itself. Anglian *galla*, *cald* against West Saxon *ġealla*, *ċeald* comes from the *a*/*ea* difference, which feeds the palatalization rule, rather than from a different rule.

## Scandinavian velars (§7.15 n4 and §7.42)

§7.15 n4 warns that Modern English misleads, "because of loan-words and/or Scandinavian influence, such as PDE *gate* < *ġeat*." He also names words that always had /j/, such as *ġēar*.

§7.42, citing Campbell §438 against Luick: Scandinavian influence is the probable source of dialect *birk*, *brig*, *rig*, *steek*, *weg* beside *birch*, *bridge*, *ridge*, *stitch*, *wedge*; likewise *carve* beside *ċeorfan*, and *skin* beside *sċinn* (Old Norse *skinn*). Place-names in *-chester* and *-caster* mark how far that influence reached. He says this later substitution does not undo §7.23: palatalization was a general Old English change.

He does not, in the sections read, name *give*, *get*, *gift*, or *guest*. Those three are checked on [[concepts/old-english-g-palatalization]] from Gersum and the ordinary etymological dictionaries.

## *Gatwōn*, from the morphology volume as others quote it

The Gersum entry for *gate* "road" and Arjen Versloot's chapter on Yorkshire English both cite Hogg and Fulk §3.116 (Versloot also gives p. 129). For a feminine *\*gatwōn*, fronting and palatalization would have touched the nominative singular, and the oblique forms in *\*gat-* would have kept velar *g* after a restored *a*. The velar could then be levelled through the paradigm. That is their report of the section. The section was not read here.
