# English Benedictine Reform

Wikipedia, “English Benedictine Reform,” a survey of the late tenth-century movement, citing John Blair, Nicholas Brooks, Rosamond McKitterick, and others. [en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Benedictine_reform).

## Key claims

- In the mid-tenth century almost all monasteries were staffed by secular clergy, who were often married. Dunstan of Canterbury (archbishop 959–988), Æthelwold of Winchester (bishop 963–984), and Oswald of York (archbishop 971–992) worked to replace them with celibate monks under the Rule of St Benedict.
- The manifesto is the *Regularis Concordia*, written by Æthelwold and adopted at the Council of Winchester around 970. It uniformizes the liturgy, requires psalms for the king and queen, and requires royal consent for the election of abbots. The main continental models are Fleury and Ghent, and behind them the Aachen reform of Louis the Pious, not Cluny.
- Edgar (959–975) is the first king to push the programme hard. By 975, up to thirty male houses and seven or eight nunneries had been reformed, all in Wessex or the Midlands, perhaps a tenth of religious houses. The north was not reformed this way: the crown could not confiscate local church property there.
- Papal involvement was slight. The movement depended on the king. Reformed monks were a counterweight to local ealdormen.
- Æthelwold expelled secular clerks from the Old and New Minsters at Winchester and put monks in the cathedral. That was not continental practice. Oswald put monks at Worcester but kept the clerks. Canterbury became fully monastic only after Dunstan’s death. English monks also preached and taught, despite the Rule’s bar on outside ministry.
- The reformers condemned married clergy. [[entities/wulfstan-of-york]], of the next generation, cited Dunstan’s view that a married cleric charged with a crime should be tried as a layman.
- Surviving accounts are by the reformers. Blair and others treat the portrait of secular clerks as foul and lazy as Æthelwold’s propaganda.
- Æthelwold’s pupil [[entities/aelfric-of-eynham]] is the leading writer of the standard late West Saxon those schools produced.
- After Edgar’s death in 975, nobles seized back land in the so-called anti-monastic reaction. Ann Williams reads that crisis as politics among kin-groups, not a simple pagan or anti-monastic party.

## Limits

A survey article. The “holy society” quotation it attributes to Cooper is a modern historian’s phrase for the English aim, not a title Wulfstan used.
