# Locherbie-Cameron 1991, The Men Named in the Poem

Margaret A. L. Locherbie-Cameron, "The Men Named in the Poem," in Donald Scragg, ed., *The Battle of Maldon AD 991* (Oxford: Basil Blackwell, 1991), pp. 238–49. A companion chapter, "Byrhtnoth and his Family," is pp. 253–62, with a list of his lands at pp. 257–60. Both sit in [[references/scragg-1991-maldon-essays]]. An earlier note is "Ælfwine's Kinsmen and *The Battle of Maldon*," *Notes and Queries* 223 (December 1978): 486–87. That note was not fetched.

Google Scholar, OpenAlex, and Crossref return the volume and later citations. They do not return the chapter. The judgments below are what Shannon E. Miller records, with her page numbers, in *The Battle of Maldon: A Medieval Screenplay* (Trent University MA, 2013; [open PDF](https://batadora.trentu.ca/_flysystem/fedora/2022-04/The_Battle_of_Maldon_A_Medieval_Screenplay_History_and_Heroism_in_the_Cinematic_Adaptation_of_an_Old_English_Poem.pdf)). Miller is a witness to the chapter, and she also invents scenes. Only the footnote claims are used here. The working list is on [[concepts/people-named-in-maldon]].

## What she is reported to say

- **Ælfwine's father** (p. 241). Ælfric Cild, son-in-law of Ealdorman Ealhhelm, ealdorman from 983, banished in 985 or 986, is a probable and uncertain match for the poem's Ælfric. ^[ambiguous]
- **Ælfhelm Polga** (p. 241). Possibly a relative of Æthelric, of the Godric who stays and fights, and indirectly of Byrhtnoth. He is not a speaker in the fragment. ^[ambiguous]
- **Offa** (p. 246). Likely the nephew of a Bishop Theodric. The poem itself only calls him *Gaddes mæg* (line 287). Miller's spelling "Theodric" is kept as his report. ^[ambiguous]
- **Byrhtwold** (p. 243). Likely the *cniht* who receives land at Donyland in Essex in the will of Æthelflæd, Byrhtnoth's sister-in-law, between 975 and 991. ^[ambiguous]
- **Maccus** (p. 239). The name occurs as Maccus or Maccos in Old Irish and Old Cornish. A Norse *Magnus* is the other possibility she sets out. Miller's screenplay chooses the Norse one. ^[ambiguous]
- **Æscferth** (pp. 239, 242). Ecglaf could be an anglicized Old Norse Eilífr or Eglaf, so Æscferth would be the son of an Anglo-Danish family. The hostage story in the screenplay is Miller's addition. ^[ambiguous]
- **Godric, Odda's son, and his brothers** (p. 245). Hard to follow after the battle, because Godric is a common name, and because, in her words as Miller quotes them, "their descent into obscurity [was] to be expected."
- **Wulfbold** (p. 240). She summarizes the charter that forfeits his lands. He is context for the reign, and he is not a man the fragment names.

The family chapter, again only through Miller's pointer, is where the Ely gifts, Ælfflæd's will of about 1002, and the estate list belong. Those sentences in the thesis are Miller's narration with a "see also," so they are not copied here as her findings.

## Limitations

A citing thesis, not the essay. Where she is reported as "probable" or as offering two name-origins, the identification stays open.
