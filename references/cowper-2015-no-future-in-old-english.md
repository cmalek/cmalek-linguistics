# There's No Future in Old English (Cowper et al. 2015)

Elizabeth Cowper, Daniel Currie Hall, Bronwyn Bjorkman, Rebecca Tollan, and Neil Banerjee, “There’s no future in Old English,” *Actes du congrès annuel de l’Association canadienne de linguistique / Proceedings of the 2015 annual conference of the Canadian Linguistic Association* (2015). PDF: [Cowper et al. 2015](https://cla-acl.ca/pdfs/actes-2015/Cowper_Hall_Bjorkman_Tollan_Banerjee-2015.pdf).

They ask whether the rise of modals as inflectional heads changed what the simple present could mean. The test is one text in five versions: Greek Gospels (Westcott and Hort), the Vulgate, the Anglo-Saxon Gospels (ca. 993, from Bosworth and Waring), Purvey’s Wycliffite revision (ca. 1388), and the King James Version. A record enters the set if the King James verse has *will* or *shall*, or the Greek has a future indicative or aorist subjunctive. Of 4,538 records, 1,118 were coded as futurate.

## Key claims

- In those 1,118 clauses the Anglo-Saxon Gospels use a present form 948 times (84.8%): 784 present indicative (70.1%), 104 forms syncretic between indicative and subjunctive (9.3%), and 60 unambiguous present subjunctives (5.4%).
- Modal wording in the same set is 24 clauses (2.1%): *will/nyll* 14, *magan* 5, *scealon* 4, *wurðan* 1.
- Luke 13:24 is their showcase. The Old English has present *sēcaþ* and *magon*. Purvey has *schulen not mowe*. The King James has *will seek* and *shall not be able*.
- They find no preference for the subjunctive as the future form. Their conclusion is that Old English finite clauses contrast past with non-past, and that a grammatical modality feature is absent, so the present is free in future contexts.
- Purvey’s revision already uses *shall* in 911 of the 1,118 (81.5%). The King James uses a modal in 1,111 (99.4%), mostly *shall/should* (77.5% together) with *will/would* at 21.9%.
- They treat the change as the addition of contrastive modality to Infl between Middle and Early Modern English, in the line of Lightfoot, Roberts, Warner, van Kemenade, and van Gelderen. Once that feature exists, a bare present is contrastively non-modal, and predictive futures need a modal.

## Limits

The texts are scripture in translation, by few translators, in a formal register. The Anglo-Saxon Gospels are late West Saxon and follow Latin. The authors say a search of the Penn historical corpora for “tomorrow” would have biased which futures they found, which is why they used aligned gospel verses instead. The 2.1% modal figure is the share of these matched clauses, not the share of all *willan* tokens in Old English. The local proceedings PDF prints the same table: 948 present forms (84.8%) and 24 modals (2.1%) inside the 1,118 futurate clauses.
