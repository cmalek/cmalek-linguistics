# To Be, or Not Two Be's (Bradley 2011)

Luke Bradley, “To be, or not two be’s? Celtic substrate influence on the Old English copula,” undergraduate thesis, Queen Mary, University of London, April 2011. Queen Mary’s OPAL 28: [PDF](https://www.qmul.ac.uk/sllf/media/sllf-new/department-of-linguistics/documents/28)-QMOPAL-Bradley-long.pdf).

## Key claims

- Continental Germanic folds *b*-forms and *wes*-forms into one paradigm (Old High German) or lacks *b*-forms (Old Norse). Old English keeps two present paradigms. Bradley, citing Ringe (2006: 70), says this was not inherited as a “be” versus “be” contrast: *\*wesaną* meant “stay,” and *\*beunan* still carried future being or becoming.
- He treats the split as Celtic substrate, in the line of Keller (1925), Lutz (2009), and Wischer (2010). His own test is dialectal: more Celtic population in the north, west, and Midlands should preserve a stricter semantic contrast. The corpus is a small non-translated slice of York-Toronto-Helsinki prose, present tense only, because the past has no double paradigm.
- The canonical contrast he uses: *wes*-forms for a current, fleeting state; *b*-forms elsewhere. A southern or eastern text that uses a *b*-form for a fleeting state would count as fuzziness.
- Mitchell and Robinson (2003) are quoted as calling *bið* a special future and then giving the gnomic *wyrd bið ful āræd*. Bradley’s objection is that an eternal truth is not a future.
- Spanish enters once, as a living language that still has two copulas, *ser* and *estar*. He does not gloss Old English sentences with Spanish verbs. He also notes that Penny’s history of Spanish explains *ser* and *estar* from Latin form, not from contact.

## Limits

This is an undergraduate thesis. The Celtic population premise is assumed from genetics and contact literature, then used to interpret the copula counts. The Spanish remark is an analogy of “two copulas,” not a usage study.
