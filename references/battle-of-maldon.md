# The Battle of Maldon

> [!tldr] Incomplete alliterative poem on the 991 fight at Maldon, Essex: ealdorman Byrhtnoth refuses *gafol*, lets Vikings cross the causeway, and is killed.

## Problem & Motivation

The surviving text opens mid-sentence (*brocen wurde*) and is the chief literary witness for late West Saxon heroic ethos under Æthelred.

## Method / Architecture

**Historical frame (Aerobics + Wikipedia):** August 991. Vikings camp on Northey Island in the Blackwater estuary, tied to the mainland by a tidal causeway. Byrhtnoth (*beorht* + *nōþ*, “bright courage”), ealdorman of Essex (c. 931–11 August 991), liege of Æthelred, arrays the *heorðwerod*.

**Foard 2003 (Battlefields Trust, 33 pp.):** campaign paper, not a new edition of the poem. ASC E: Olaf + 93 ships; later tradition (Snorri) ~30 men per ship → ~2790, or up to ~5580 if the muster is larger. English force = Essex *fyrd* plus Byrhtnoth’s household; numbers unknown. Aftermath: first recorded *gafol* of £10,000. **Site:** Northey (Laborde 1925; Petty geology: sea-level ~6 ft lower, saltings later) is the consensus; Heybridge and Osea have been proposed. The battlefield is **not** archaeologically confirmed. *Liber Eliensis* is a late, unreliable source for the fight. ^[ambiguous]

**Poem’s action (opening):** retainers abandon horses; Offa’s kinsman looses his hawk and takes weapons; Eadric keeps faith. Byrhtnoth rides the line, teaching shield-wall. A Viking herald offers peace for *bēagas* / *gafol*. Byrhtnoth’s answer: they will give *gāras*, “Æthelred’s land” will be guarded, heathens shall fall; *ord and ecg* will settle it before tribute.

Later tradition (not in the fragment’s opening): allowing the Vikings off the island to fight “fairly” is fatal. Tolkien’s *The Homecoming of Beorhtnoth, Beorhthelm’s Son* is a modern verse play on the aftermath; the word *ofermod* in the poem is the usual ethical crux. ^[inferred]

**Byrhtnoth off the page:** father Byrhthelm; estates across ten shires; patron of Ely (buried there, later reburials; 18th-century measurement claimed 6′9″ and a missing head). Widow Ælfflæd’s hanging at Ely is compared to Bayeux technique. Statue (John Doubleday, 2006) faces Northey Island.

## Limitations

Opening and (in the usual account) ending of the poem are lost. Height/white-hair details are later colour. Foard: Northey vs Heybridge/Osea remains a landscape argument, not a findspot. ^[ambiguous]

## Manuscript

The poem’s leaves were in London, British Library, Cotton Otho A. xii, a manuscript that also held Asser’s *Life of Alfred*. Simon Keynes dates the Cotton fire to 23 October 1731 (*Anglo-Saxon England* 22, 1993). The poem’s leaves were lost. [[references/karasawa-2025-griffith-maldon|Kazutomo Karasawa’s review]] of Mark Griffith’s 2024 edition records the surviving witness: a transcript by David Casley, deputy keeper, now Oxford, Bodleian Library, MS Rawlinson B. 203, fols. 7–12, and Thomas Hearne’s print of it in the 1726 Glastonbury chronicle, vol. 2, pp. 570–77, made before the fire. Griffith, as Karasawa reports him, finds Casley more faithful to layout and pointing than earlier editors allowed, and he also notes readings from a lost Price transcript quoted by Conybeare (1826). The fragment was already headless and tailless when it was copied. [[references/insley-2026-wigelm|John Insley]] dates the transcript about 1724 and builds his line-300 emendation on the fact that the leaf is gone.

The standard historical essay collection is [[references/scragg-1991-maldon-essays|Scragg, *The Battle of Maldon AD 991* (1991)]]. Scragg’s 1981 edition was the working text until Griffith. The deeper critical map is [[synthesis/Research: The Battle of Maldon]].

## Names, purpose, and the speeches

The men the fragment names, and the few who can be matched outside it, are on [[concepts/people-named-in-maldon]]. The argument over *ofermod* is [[concepts/ofermod]]. The formal speeches — the herald’s demand, Byrhtnoth’s refusal, and the vows after Godric’s flight — are [[concepts/heroic-speech-in-maldon]].

[[references/griffith-2022-vengeance-of-offa|Griffith’s 2022 chapter]] argues that Offa’s killing of *þone sælidan* (line 286) is the killing of the messenger who opened that exchange (line 45). The shared failure of *sælida* to alliterate is the link he uses. Pope had treated the same lines as a gap in the copy.

## Reading in translation

[[references/glenn-1982-maldon-translation|Glenn's line-numbered translation]] (1982) runs the 325 lines of the fragment with 47 short glosses on words such as *ofermod*, *æschere*, *heorðwerod* and *cellod*. Its headnote gives the transcript in Rawlinson B 203 as John Elphinston's, found about 1935, where this page (after Karasawa on Griffith) says Casley's; the conflict is unresolved. ^[ambiguous] Glenn marks three lost leaves at the start and one at the end, and a missing half-line in Byrhtnoth's dying prayer.

Seamus Heaney's 2006 poem "Helmet" uses this battle, and Hrothgar's gift of a helmet in *Beowulf*, as a response to 11 September 2001. That claim is from the reception chapter [[concepts/old-english-after-1066]], not from an edition of the fragment.
