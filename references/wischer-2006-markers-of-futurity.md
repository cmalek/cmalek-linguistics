# Markers of Futurity in Old English (Wischer 2006)

Ilse Wischer, “Markers of futurity in Old English and the grammaticalization of *shall* and *will*,” *Studia Anglica Posnaniensia* 42 (2006): 165–178. Open PDF: [12Wischer.pdf](http://ifa.amu.edu.pl/sap/files/42/12Wischer.pdf). The sample is about 80,000 words from the Old English Helsinki Corpus, 880–1120: Alfred’s Boethius, *Cura Pastoralis*, and Orosius; the West Saxon and Lindisfarne Gospels; Chronicle MS E in two stretches; and a Gregory passage.

She takes a future gram, following Bybee, Perkins, and Pagliuca (1994), as a speaker’s prediction that a situation holds after speech time. Lexical and pragmatic markers can do that job without being grammar.

## Key claims

- Handbooks treat Old English as past and non-past. The present, plus lexical or pragmatic cues, carries future time. *Willan*, *sculan*, *weorþan*, *bēon*, and the prefix *ge-* are the items those handbooks also mention.
- She sets *ge-* aside. English later lost the verbal prefixes, and Bybee’s survey finds aspectual futures from perfective markers rare. The older perfective-*ge-* literature she names is Streitberg (1891), Bloomfield (1929), Mossé (1938), Brunner (1965), and Lindemann (1965).
- In this sample *bēon* is the most frequent candidate (586 tokens, 0.73%), then *willan* (389, 0.49%), *weorþan* (274, 0.34%), and *sculan* (205, 0.25%). Present-tense *bēon* is the whole 586, because she counts only present and infinitive for that verb. Present *willan* is 126 and present *sculan* 83.
- Construction type matters more than raw frequency. Of present-tense tokens, 109/115 suitable *willan* uses and 78/78 suitable *sculan* uses take a bare infinitive. *Bēon* and *weorþan* mostly take a noun, adjective, prepositional phrase, or past participle. One *bēon* token takes a *tō*-infinitive; seven take a present participle.
- *Weorþan* plus past participle is a passive, and she repeats the handbook claim that a present-tense *weorþan* passive can feel future: Kilpiö (1989), Mitchell, *Old English Syntax* (1985) §755, Visser (1973) §1918. She judges that pattern a poor path to an active future. Present-tense *weorþan* then collapses in the Helsinki Corpus: 239 (850–950), 97 (950–1050), 39, 10, and 1 across the three Middle English bands.
- *Willan* and *sculan* already share most of the morphological oddities of Modern English *will* and *shall* in this sample: no non-finite forms, no ordinary third-person *-þ*, irregular pasts *wolde* and *sceolde*, and *ne* fused with *willan* (*nylle*, *nolde*). Word order and the Modern English NICE tests do not apply. About 84% of *willan* and 96% of *sculan* take a bare infinitive.
- Some of those infinitive uses are already bare prediction. Her examples include *wile weaxan* of plants, *wille fleon* of doctrine, and Latin futures rendered by *sceall habban* and *wielle bion*. She quotes Denison (1993: 304): by late Old English, if not earlier, *will* and *shall* “had developed a use which was almost a future tense.” Modal colouring remains the usual reading. In one hand count inside Alfred’s Boethius, of expressions she judged closest to a mere future, 11 were *willan*, 5 present indicative, 4 *bēon*, 3 *sculan*, 1 present subjunctive, and 1 *weorþan*.
- Lexical ‘want’ and ‘owe’ had already moved to other wording: *wilnian*, and *wesan/bēon* + *scyldig*.

## Limits

The Boethius “mere future” count is 25 hand-picked expressions, not a census of future-referring clauses. Gospel sections in the same paper are thin in *sculan*. The comparative argument is continued in [[references/diewald-wischer-2013-futurity]].
