# The Functions of Weorthan and Its Loss (Petré 2010)

Peter Petré, “The functions of *weorðan* and its loss in the past tense in Old and Middle English,” *English Language and Linguistics* 14.3 (2010): 457–484. Author copy: [KU Leuven](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/fc67dcc4-422f-448f-bdad-c11b556a6d1e).

## Key claims

- *Weorðan* alternates with *is/bēon* in the present (distribution in Kilpiö 1993) and with *wesan* in the past. All three are mostly copulas or passive auxiliaries. They can also be intransitive, “happen” or “exist.”
- The semantic core is change of state. *Wearþ belocen* is the gates becoming shut. *Wǣron belocene* is the doors already shut. Some pairs translate as “be” either way.
- The passive literature does not agree. Frary (1929) and Kurtz (1931) made them complementary: *wesan* resultative, *weorðan* actional. Zieglschmid and Klingebiel found overlap, including actional *wesan*, elsewhere in Germanic. Mitchell (1985: 324) treated them as free variation, with Chronicle *wearð ofslagen* beside *wæs ofslagen*. Kilpiö (1989: 85) keeps a preference: *weorðan* for sudden, often grim, changes.
- Petré’s own claim is about narrative. Past *wearþ* prefers main clauses, time adverbials that advance the story, and inverted (verb-second) order. Those are the bounded sentences of Old English narrative, a situation presented as reaching its end. *Wæs* is freer, including durative passives (*ā geseted wæs* “was always set”).
- In the thirteenth century *þā* and verb-second recede, bounded narrative loosens, and *wearþ* falls with them. By the end of the fourteenth century the verb is under fifty occurrences per million. Replacements are *become*, *wax*, bare *be*, and occasionally *begin to* or *be made*.

## Limits

The article argues from past-tense narrative. Present-tense *weorþan*, including its future use, is the 2009 paper’s topic. Müller’s claim that *weorþan* was bleached into durative aspect, which Petré rejects, is reported rather than re-tested here.
