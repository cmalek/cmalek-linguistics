# Roach 2016 on the St Brice's Day massacre

Levi Roach, *Æthelred the Unready* (New Haven: Yale University Press, 2016). The book was not read. What was read is Roach's essay of 11 November 2016, ["The St. Brice's Day Massacre: Then and Now"](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/2016/11/11/the-st-brices-day-massacre-then-and-now/), written for Yale University Press from that research, and a Yale excerpt on the reign's purity politics ([23 October 2017](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/2017/10/23/aethelred-the-unready/)).

## The essay's account

- The Chronicle's "all the Danish men who were in England" means recent arrivals, probably the mercenaries taken into Æthelred's service in 994. In 1001 some of that group had already deserted and ravaged the south coast. A strike against the rest, tied to a rumor of conspiracy, is the reading he prefers.
- The earliest emotionally charged text is the 1004 St Frideswide diploma. Danes in Oxford took sanctuary in the church; the townspeople burned it. The draftsman calls them cockle among the wheat (Matthew 13:24–30) and the killing "a most just extermination."
- He rejects Stenton's "acts of spasmodic violence." The massacre belongs with other purges of supposed impurity. Four years later senior advisers were themselves killed.
- The practical result was a realm stripped of men it needed. The purges bred suspicion and division. Æthelred's death was soon followed by Cnut's conquest.
- The essay does not name [[entities/wulfstan-of-york]].

The 2017 excerpt puts the 1002 killing of "faithless Danish mercenaries" in a sequence: first the king and his counsellors treat themselves as the guilty party; when that fails, they root out other sinful elements, the mercenaries in 1002 and leading advisers in 1005–6. Wulfstan is not named there either.
