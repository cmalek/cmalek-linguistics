# The Vengeance of Offa (Griffith 2022)

> [!tldr] Griffith reads *Offa þone sælidan* (line 286) as Offa killing the Viking messenger of line 45. The verse breaks the alliterative rule on purpose so the audience hears that echo.

Mark Griffith, “The Battle of Maldon and the Vengeance of Offa,” chapter 4 of *Tradition and Innovation in Old English Metre*, ed. Rachel A. Burns and Rafael J. Pascual (Leeds: Arc Humanities Press, 2022), 79–101. Medieval Media and Culture. ISBN 9781641894586. Quotations of the poem follow Scragg 1981, with Griffith’s punctuation. He sets aside three easy exits: a bad copy, a poet who did not understand his metre, and a poem that is simply poor.

## Problem

Between Dunnere (lines 258–59) and Byrhtwold (lines 312–19) the poet spends about fifty lines on several followers, then lingers on Offa: he strikes *þone sælidan*, falls, and has kept the *beot* that he and his ring-giver would ride home or die on the field (lines 285b–94). Offa is otherwise second only to Byrhtnoth in how often the fragment names him. Griffith isolates three faults in that death-scene.

| Fault | The charge | Who made it |
|---|---|---|
| Syntax | *þone* in line 286 has no antecedent, so text is missing | Pope (1966); Fulk prints an omission before *Bærst bordes lærig* |
| Metre | *Offa þone sælidan* has a half-stress after the second stress and therefore requires double alliteration, which it lacks | Bliss, *The Metre of Beowulf* §117 |
| Story | Killing one unnamed Viking does not earn the praise that follows | The aesthetic gap Griffith sets out to close |

He also rejects the translators who turn *þone* into “a” seaman. As far as the grammar he uses goes, the demonstrative is definite, not an indefinite article.

## The reading

The same rare compound *sælida* closes the a-verse in only two lines, and in both it fails to alliterate:

- Line 45: *Gehyrest þu, sælida, hwæt þis folc segeð?*
- Line 286: *Offa þone sælidan, þæt he on eorðan feoll*

Every other Viking-word in the poem alliterates in the ordinary way. Griffith counts nine such items, nineteen times. The shared breach is the pointer. *þone* works like *þis* in line 45: it points back at the earlier *sælida*, the messenger who talked Byrhtnoth into the promise of battle. Offa, the man who had already seen through empty boasts in the *meþelstede*, cuts down that speaker. Truth kills deception. The parallel he offers is Beowulf killing the Frankish standard-bearer Dæghrefn after Hygelac’s death. He says his own sense of the original performance is that the audience cheered at this moment. ^[inferred]

That identification is his argument. Pope’s lacuna and the indefinite translations remain on the record as the readings he refuses. The poem never names the messenger, so the equation “the seafarer of line 286 is the herald of line 45” is not a fact printed in the text. It is the solution that makes the definite article, the bad metre, and the praise line up.

## The speech that runs the poem

He treats Byrhtnoth’s reply to the messenger as the pivot. Christopher Ball (1989) had already shown that the promised weapons come back in order: *gar* in the first Viking Byrhtnoth fights, *ætterne ord* in the second, *swurd* in the third. Griffith extends the same habit of echo. *Feallan* and *foldan* from the vow that heathens shall fall return when Byrhtnoth himself falls, when the narrator says the people’s lord has fallen, and when Ælfwine’s vengeance repeats *folc and foldan*. Lines 42 and 309 are near-verbatim (*Byrhtnoð / Byrhtwold maþelode, bord hafenode*). Verbatim repeats inside an Old English poem are rare. The two men are also the only speakers who teach the troop, and both are old. When Byrhtwold speaks, the hero’s public voice is still in the line. Byrhtnoth’s prayer, by contrast, is full of words that never return. ^[inferred]

Offa’s place in that design: he is kinsman of the hawk-bearer at the start, he had warned Byrhtnoth about hollow vows, he answers Ælfwine and curses Godric, and he dies *ðegenlice* beside his lord. Griffith assumes Edward the tall is not the chamberlain of lines 117–21. See [[concepts/people-named-in-maldon]].

## Limits

This is one chapter on lines 285–86 and on lexical echo. It is not an edition, and it is not the 2024 Liverpool edition reviewed in [[references/karasawa-2025-griffith-maldon]] and [[references/thomas-2026-griffith-maldon]]. The metrical description depends on Bliss. A reader who does not grant that *Offa þone sælidan* is “almost inexcusable” will not feel the echo as a designed breach.
