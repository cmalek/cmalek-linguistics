# The Three Orders in Late Old English Prose (Crișan 2024)

Andrei Crișan, “The Concept of the Three Orders of Society in Late Old English Prose,” *Studia Universitatis Babeș-Bolyai Philologia* 69, no. 3 (2024): 189–206. DOI 10.24193/subbphilo.2024.3.11. Received 13 July 2024, accepted 5 September 2024. Georges Duby’s book on the three orders barely treats England. Crișan follows the English scheme from the Old English Boethius into Ælfric and Wulfstan, and he asks how the late writers differ from that earlier text.

## Alfredian starting point

The scheme first appears in the Old English *Consolation*, as three tools to rule with (*tol mid to ricsianne*): praying men (*gebedmen*), fighting men (*fyrdmen*), and working men (*weorcmen*). Crișan leaves Alfred’s authorship open. Godden argues against it; Pratt and Bately defend it; Papahagi, in the same journal issue, treats Alfred as patron rather than translator. ^[ambiguous] Powell (1994) is his guide for what is distinctive: the king sits at the centre, and the Christian framing familiar from Haymo and Heiric of Auxerre is absent. Whether those Frankish texts are the source remains debated. The Alfredian translator, on Crișan’s reading, makes it the king’s job to put the three classes to use. They are unequal in rank and equal in importance to him.

Wulfstan glossed the preface to the *Pastoral Care* in Oxford, Bodleian Library, Hatton 20. Crișan takes that habit as reason to think he knew the Alfredian three tools as well. Ælfric’s letter to Wulfstan already assumes the archbishop knows the scheme. Crișan cannot tell whether the metaphor was common around 1000 and the copies were lost, or whether it spread only then.

## The crisis they write in

Raids begin in 980, two years after Æthelred’s accession, and worsen from 991 to 1012. After Maldon the tribute system starts. Crișan cites Keynes (2007) for the desperation of the measures, the Chronicle entry for 998 (the *here* in Dorset, English armies that flee, the enemy living off Hampshire and Sussex from Wight), Thorkell’s attacks on London, repeated *gafol*, and the killing of Archbishop Ælfheah in 1011–12. He also cites Baxter for a tribute total of £240,500 paid to the Danes under Æthelred, beside tithe. That figure is Baxter’s, as Crișan uses it, not a sum Crișan recalculates. Small estates multiply, more intermediaries stand between peasants and king, and he treats that speed of change as more than the old system or the new king could hold.

## Two sequences, two jobs

*Polity* is not a finished book. Jost’s I Polity (Cambridge, Corpus Christi College 201) and II Polity (added matter from Oxford, Bodleian Library, Junius 121 and London, British Library, Cotton Nero A. i) are the editorial habit Crișan drops. He cites his own *Notes and Queries* note (2024) and Reinhard (2020, 2021), and he names the versions Corpus, Junius, and Nero. See [[concepts/institutes-of-polity]].

In Corpus *Be cynestole* the throne stands on *oratores*, *laboratores*, and *bellatores*. If one pillar gives way, the throne breaks and the people are harmed. Ælfric’s *Lives of Saints* lists *laboratores*, *oratores*, *bellatores*, and he uses that list to keep priests off the battlefield: the plowman feeds, the soldier fights the earthly army, the servant of God fights unseen enemies. The king and the kingdom are not the point there (Powell 1994). In the later letter to Sigeweard the image changes. Counsellors should see which leg of the throne is broken. The legs are now *laboratores*, *bellatores*, *oratores*, and the fighters stand forward, with Paul’s line that the soldier does not bear the sword without cause. Crișan calls the throne-legs image new in that letter, and he says Wulfstan takes the warning over: each leg must hold or it ruins the other two. Writing in English, Ælfric is aiming past the clergy at the nobles who have failed to defend the country. ^[inferred]

Wulfstan’s law codes sometimes follow the same three-part shape. In V Æthelred, clauses 4–10.2 are clerical duty, the next block is dues, tithes, feasts, and fasts, and 26.1–28.1 is military obligation. VI Æthelred runs clerical and episcopal duty (2–6), then Christian men generally, then military clauses 33–35.

They do not agree about armed clergy. Ælfric blames priests who take up weapons more than the men who push them to it, and he barely mentions the raids around Eynsham and Cerne. Wulfstan mentions the issue twice: Canons of Edgar 68b (1002–1004; the ban is only in the Junius 121 copy, a book for bishops) and 8 Æthelred 30 (about 1014). Crișan cites wills that show the practical pressure Ælfric was answering: Bishop Theodred of London leaves his lord his best horses, swords, shields, and spears; Bishop Ælfwold of Crediton leaves military gear and a ship. In 1016 Bishop Eadnoth of Dorchester and Abbot Wulfsige of Ramsey die at Assandun. Possession of weapons is not the same as fighting in person. Crișan follows Nakashian: owning “the best that I have” still means the bishop knew what a good sword was.

## Where the king sits

Bethurum called Wulfstan’s shift revolutionary, “in implication both anti-royalist and anti-papal.” Crișan keeps the observation and rejects the modern labels. The unsent protest in London, British Library, Cotton Vespasian A. xiv, fols. 178–179, argues that an English archbishop should be ordained by English bishops, objects to the journey to Rome for the pallium and to its cost, and still allows penitential pilgrimages. The letter quotes the free gift of grace against simony.

Unlike the Boethius translator, Wulfstan does not charge the king with assembling the three orders. They must come together and hold the throne up, or it falls however strong the ruler is. When the king fails, the Church takes the people’s welfare. Junius *Polity* opens on the one eternal king. Corpus *Polity* tells a Christian king to stand as father and as Christ’s representative, to love Christianity, shun heathenism, honour churches, and rule with just law. Crișan ties that ideal to Edgar’s consecration at Bath in 973, the Chronicle’s *gehalgod*, and Deshman’s argument that *Christus rex* grows from Æthelwold’s Benedictional. The king must correct and save rather than merely punish. Bishops can trade death for a penalty that still allows repentance (Marafioti). The three pillars are to be strengthened with God’s teaching and with just worldly law. Crime and sin were usually punished together. Wulfstan’s attempt to distinguish the two jurisdictions and still make them cooperate is, for Crișan, rare in English legislation, and hard to prove in court practice.

His closing claim is the one the three writers share: only a coordinated unity of the orders can hold the king up in a desperate England. The sequences and the blame differ. The interdependence does not.

## Limits

The Old English quotations are printed from Corpus 201 and Junius 121, with translations Crișan marks as his own except where he names Clayton and Mullins or Marsden. This note paraphrases the argument. It does not recopy those editions. The £240,500 tribute and the “anti-royalist” label are claims he cites, not claims this page has checked against Baxter or Bethurum.
