# Jones 1990, lexical structure in The Battle of Maldon

Alex I. Jones, “Lexical structure in *The Battle of Maldon*,” *Sydney Studies in Society and Culture* (1990), open journal text at [Sydney](https://openjournals.library.sydney.edu.au/SSSC/article/view/8741/8791). His text is Sweet’s *Anglo-Saxon Reader*, revised by Dorothy Whitelock (1967). He divides the surviving 325 lines into 33 blocks, mostly of ten lines, and runs a factor analysis on word distributions.

## Purpose, as he frames it

The battle is the topic. He asks what the poem is for. He finds it a poor memorial for Byrhtnoth: there is no encomium on the dead leader, though his men hold him in esteem. As a celebration of heroic values it is ironical, because generosity and honor lead to disaster. It is a Christian poem in the narrow sense that Byrhtnoth prays for his soul (lines 173–80) against *hæþene scealcas* (line 181). The English fight for their king and their land. The whole does not stage a religious exemplum. His closing claim is that Byrhtnoth’s death is the center, and that he is an emblem balanced by *folc* at both ends: “The poem is not the elegy of one man, but of a country whose traditions, however noble, gave no answers.”

## The three dips

The dominant factor is shared vocabulary, with three weak points that he reads as high information and low use of the commonest words:

- Lines 91–99. Byrhtnoth’s invitation to cross, the end of the shouting and the start of the fighting.
- Lines 202–08. After the leader’s death and the flight, the remnant sees that only honor can be saved.
- Lines 309–19. Byrhtwold’s speech, preparing the others for death.

A second pattern loads relational verbs and passivity (*bēon*, *weorþan*, *forlætan*, *feallan*, *flēogan*, *of*) on lines 100–201, the stretch of stasis, retreat, and loss. A third pattern moves from plural *we* / *hī* early to singular *ic* / *hē* late: cohesion gives way to men cut down one by one. Speaking verbs (*sprecan*, *mæþelian*, *word*) load on that later part.

He expects a heroic poem to run from *beot* through action to victory or to collapse. Here the energy comes before the armies close, “and the boasts when there is little left to boast about.” The definite article thins toward the end, which he takes as rising formality. “Heroic dignity is at last intact, but there will be no-one left to see it.”

He also guesses at the lost lines from this shape: one opening leaf, about 33 introductory lines, and about 27 lines lost at the end. That reconstruction is his inference from the factor pattern plus an average of just over 54 lines a leaf. The manuscript facts he assumes are the usual ones (six surviving leaves). ## Limitations

The factors are a statistical summary of a teaching text, and the leaf-count reconstruction is a proposal. The article is cited here for the speech-centered dips and for his refusal to call the poem either a simple encomium or a Christian exemplum.
