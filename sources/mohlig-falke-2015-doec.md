# Möhlig-Falke 2015: Classifying the DOEC

Ruth Möhlig-Falke, “Using the Dictionary of [[concepts/old-english|Old English]] Corpus for linguistic analyses: a basic classification of the textual sources,” *Neuphilologische Mitteilungen* (2015), 395–420. The article was read from a text extraction of the author PDF, including the 2009 word-count table, the discussion of balance, and the remarks on Latin translation. A secondary citation gives the journal as volume 15, part 1, which cannot be right for this journal in 2015; the page numbers match the PDF. The volume number is therefore not repeated here. ^[ambiguous]

The 2009 release counts 3,033,142 Old English words and about 750,000 foreign words, mostly Latin. Cameron categories, as printed and checked against that total:

| Category | Old English words | Foreign words |
|---|---:|---:|
| A Poetry | 177,480 | 255 |
| B Prose | 2,128,781 | 52,038 |
| C Interlinear glosses | 699,606 | 635,655 |
| D Glossaries | 26,598 | 70,511 |
| E Runic inscriptions | 346 | 4 |
| F Non-runic inscriptions | 331 | 40 |
| Total | 3,033,142 | 758,503 |

The prose line in the extraction was broken after the millions digit. The figure 2,128,781 is the reading that makes the printed total exact. ^[inferred]

Möhlig-Falke’s point for lexical gaps is that this completeness is completeness of what survives. The DOEC is an electronic text database aimed at maximal coverage for the [[entities/dictionary-of-old-english]], not a balanced corpus. Gaps in spontaneous, colloquial, and everyday language exist because no text types representing vernacular oral language to any reliable extent have been preserved. She tells linguists to accept that before interpreting results. Most Old English prose was translated from Latin. Literacy, she quotes, was primarily a religious matter for a long time. Northumbrian, Mercian, and [[concepts/old-english-dialects|Kentish]] survivals are largely glosses to Latin, so they are weak evidence for ordinary prose in those dialects.

Glossaries are lexically important and token-poor: 26,598 Old English words beside 70,511 foreign words. Interlinear glosses are token-rich and repetitive. Inscriptions are negligible as a lexicon.
