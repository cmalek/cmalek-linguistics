# Hough 2010: Toponymicon and Lexicon

Carole Hough, *Toponymicon and Lexicon in North-West Europe: ‘Ever-Changing Connection’*, E. C. Quiggin Memorial Lectures 12 (Cambridge: Department of Anglo-Saxon, Norse and Celtic, 2010). The lecture was read in full. Author: [[entities/carole-hough]].

Hough restates the older tenet and then limits it. Place-names were long treated as ordinary everyday language, so the mismatch with literature was explained by subject matter and register. Names contain more words for physical surroundings and associated activities, and a more colloquial vocabulary than poetry or prose. She says that much of this still holds. Terminology attested only in Celtic and Germanic place-names does concentrate on flora and fauna, landscape features, and farming practices. Her own catalogues, cited in the notes, include Anglo-Saxon plant-names found only in place-names, and proposed animal and bird names such as OE *wearg* “wolf,” *brūn* used of a pig, *pohha*/*pocca* “fallow deer,” and *pur* “male lamb.”

The revision is that some name-elements belong to a toponymic register, developed partly apart from the lexicon. A term can be common as a place-name generic and still lack secure use as an ordinary word. Her example is OE *cumb*, a Brittonic loan for a short, broad, bowl- or trough-shaped valley in Margaret Gelling and Ann Cole’s sense. The *[[entities/dictionary-of-old-english|Dictionary of Old English]]* shows it in place-names and charter bounds, not in non-onomastic [[concepts/old-english|Old English]]. Hough suggests it was fully toponymicized, and that its later life as an ordinary noun is a borrowing back from names. On that view, absence from the dictionaries is real absence from the common lexicon, not a lost colloquial word waiting to be restored.

She also keeps the opposite traffic. Names can preserve the earliest evidence of a word that later appears in literature, often because names deal with subjects the literary texts ignore. Some of those words may have begun in the naming system and only later entered ordinary vocabulary. The lecture’s conclusion is that lexicon and toponymicon stay in “ever-changing connection,” rather than that every name-element is a missing dictionary headword.

A 1995 project description, read separately, had planned a handlist of Old English words unattested outside place-names, generated from an “Attested: No” field, as a supplement to dictionaries that rely on literary sources. The published successor is *The Vocabulary of English Place-Names*. As of the Nottingham Institute for Name-Studies page checked in this research, the issued volumes reach *Ceafor–Cock-pit*, and later letters were still in preparation. ^[inferred]
