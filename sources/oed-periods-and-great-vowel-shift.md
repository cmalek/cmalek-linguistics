# OED: Period dates and the vowel shift

Two Oxford English Dictionary “Discover” essays. They are reference prose, not a phonology monograph.

**Middle English – an overview** states the OED3 boundaries: Middle English is 1150–1500. Before 1150 is Old English. After 1500 is early modern English. The essay says the boundaries are hard to define and that scholarly opinions vary. Externally, the period runs from the aftermath of 1066 to printing in Britain in 1476 and to the Reformation from the 1530s.

**Early modern English: grammar, pronunciation, and spelling** states:

- From the late fifteenth century, printers issued books in the form of London English. Between 1475 and about 1630, spelling became regularized. After the mid-seventeenth century, printed English looks largely like modern English. Handwriting kept noticeable variation into the eighteenth century.
- By the sixteenth century, spelling was out of step with pronunciation because printing was fixing late Middle English forms while sound change continued. The chief case is the Great Vowel Shift.
- The essay’s simplified path, using *mite*, *meet*, and *mate*: Middle English long *i*, *e*, and *a* had values like continental [iː], [eː], and [aː]. After the shift, long *i* was a diphthong, probably [əi] in the sixteenth century; long *e* had become [iː]; long *a* was a front vowel, at first nearer the vowel of *air*, later [eː]. A parallel change hit the back vowels of *mouth* and *moot*.
- Some sets that had been distinct became identical while the spelling difference remained. Examples include *mane* with *may*/*main*, and *sloe*/*so* with *slow*. Long vowels also shortened in some words, so *blood*, *flood*, *good*, and *hook* no longer match the long vowel suggested by *oo*.
- Consonant losses are listed separately, after the vowel-shift illustration: initial *k* and *g* before *n*, initial *w* before *r*, and the old *gh* fricative, among others. The essay does not define those consonant losses as the vowel shift.
