# Krug 2003: Inception of the Great Vowel Shift

Manfred Krug, “(Great) vowel shifts present and past: Meeting ground for structural and natural phonologists,” *University of Pennsylvania Working Papers in Linguistics* 9.2 (2003), article 10. Papers from NWAV 31. Text from the Penn repository page.

Krug argues that the upper half of the shift was a drag chain, and that the trigger was in high-frequency pronouns. As Middle English *I* and *thou* bonded to vowel-initial *am* and *art*, he proposes that speakers avoided hiatus by inserting /j/ and /w/. Those glides, on his account, pulled the long high vowels toward diphthongs. He dates the usual beginning of the shift at around 1400, and he reports a steep rise in potential *I am* liaison sites around 1350 in the Helsinki Corpus.

He states the limit himself: there is no direct evidence for liaison in *I am* sequences. The case is circumstantial: more potential sites just before or as the shift is usually said to begin, loss of hiatus, and similar intrusion in modern dialects. He presents evidence in detail for *I am*, and says the same bonding should apply to *thou art* because word order was becoming fixed.

**Limits.** A working-papers article, not the 2012 or 2017 handbook chapter that surveys the whole shift. Those chapters were not read. The 2017 abstract only was seen: consensus is limited, and many aspects remain poorly understood ([10.1515/9783110525069-014](https://doi.org/10.1515/9783110525069-014)). This 2003 paper is one proposed inception, and Krug is arguing a view he calls the majority drag-chain position.
