# Prichard 2014: Northern evidence for the Great Vowel Shift

Hilary Prichard, “Northern dialect evidence for the chronology of the Great Vowel Shift,” *Journal of Linguistic Geography* (2014). DOI [10.1017/jlg.2014.9](https://doi.org/10.1017/jlg.2014.9), which resolves to the Cambridge article page. The text used here is the article HTML, including the abstract and the debate and methods sections. Affiliation given in the article: University of Pennsylvania.

Prichard’s claims, in her words or close paraphrase:

- After a century of debate, the chronology is still unclear, and scholars have not reached consensus on cause, chronology, or phonological mechanism.
- Outside southern standard dialects, modern reflexes of the Middle English long vowels are often unexpected. The most northern parts of England still keep a monophthongal Middle English *ū* in MOUTH words.
- Jespersen (1909) is the drag-chain: diphthongization of the high vowels first. Luick (1896) is the push-chain: mid vowels *ē* and *ō* raised first. Luick’s crux is that some dialects keep Middle English *ū* and had earlier fronted *ō*, so there was no mid back vowel to push *ū* out.
- Stockwell and Minkova (1988) concluded that the Great Vowel Shift is “the linguist’s creation through hindsight,” and objected that the shift did not proceed in the dialects as it is assumed to have done in London.
- Lass (1992) defends Luick’s internal coherence and calls the traditional pattern too valuable to discard.
- Her own result, from Eduard Kolb’s *Linguistic Atlas of England: Phonological Atlas of the Northern Region* (Survey of English Dialects fieldwork, 1950–1961), is evidence for a push-chain, in the sense that the high-mid long vowels raised before the high long vowels diphthongized. She treats irregular northern outcomes as diffusion of a chain, following Labov (2007), rather than as proof that there was no chain.

**Limits.** The informants were mostly older rural men, one variant per word in most places, and the geography is the north of England plus northern Lincolnshire. A push-chain reading of twentieth-century dialect geography is not a dated manuscript chronology. The 1896 Luick work and the 1988 Stockwell and Minkova article were not opened for this note; their positions are as Prichard reports them.
