# Kay 2012: Old English Semantics and Lexicon

Christian Kay, “Old English: Semantics and Lexicon,” in *English Historical Linguistics: An International Handbook*, vol. 1, ed. Alexander Bergs and Laurel J. Brinton (Berlin: De Gruyter, 2012), 313–325. The reprint of the opening section, “The nature of the lexicon,” was read. The rest of the chapter was not.

Kay’s figures, derived from [[entities/thesaurus-of-old-english]], are about 34,000 separate Old English word forms. Counting polysemy and occasional homonymy raises the total to 50,700 meanings. Nouns are just over half of the forms, verbs about 24 percent, and adjectives about 19 percent. She compares an incomplete *Dictionary of Old English* A–G (2007), which then held 12,568 headwords, and says the Old English figures will change as that dictionary continues. She treats 34,000 as less than half the number of forms in a modern desk dictionary. She does not treat it as the size of the spoken language.

On contact, she writes that Scandinavian words often replaced Old English words in core vocabulary because transmission happened in everyday speech. Only a few of those words appear in the Old English written record. Many more appear in early Middle English. *Tacan* is recorded late in the Old English period; *sky* is not listed until the thirteenth century, although she says it was probably in use before then. Latin loans cluster in religion, education, and newly introduced commodities. Individual medicinal plant-names are a conspicuous Latin group. Native *wyrt* remains productive beside borrowed *plante*.
