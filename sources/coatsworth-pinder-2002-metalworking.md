# Coatsworth and Pinder 2002: Metalworking Vocabulary

Elizabeth Coatsworth and Michael Pinder, “Appendix A: The Anglo-Saxon Vocabulary of Metalworking,” in *The Art of the Anglo-Saxon Goldsmith: Fine Metalwork in Anglo-Saxon England, its Practice and Practitioners* (Woodbridge: Boydell, 2002), 247–257. The appendix’s opening statement was read from the publisher extract. The word-lists themselves were not.

The lists are drawn from Bosworth–Toller, *A Thesaurus of Old English*, and the Toronto dictionary. Bold marks a term found only in poetry. Italics mark a term found only in glosses. The authors’ historical claim is that the vocabulary has to be teased out of sources, most of them from the latest part of the period. No surviving Anglo-Saxon document has as its principal concern the description of a metalworking process or a recipe such as a solder. A vocabulary for some materials, tools, techniques, and workers exists. It rarely shows how the words were used in the workshop. Poetry, homilies, and saints’ lives use the terms too briefly to describe an object’s structure or a smith’s skill, and homiletic instances may derive from non-Anglo-Saxon sources. Glosses help when they are not the only evidence, because they supply a Latin equivalent. Old English has a rich vocabulary for heating, melting, mixing, thinning, and thickening, all needed in metalwork, but those verbs are generally found in the preparation of food or medicines.

The archaeological chapters, sampled through the publisher summary of the tools chapter, locate workshops and tools from the fifth century onward and say that many techniques are deduced from the objects because moulds and process descriptions are scarce. That is a gap in records of practice, parallel to the lexical gap.
