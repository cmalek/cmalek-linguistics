# Stenbrenden 2016: Long-vowel shifts in English

Gjertrud F. Stenbrenden, *Long-Vowel Shifts in English, c. 1050–1700: Evidence from Spelling* (Cambridge University Press, 2016). DOI [10.1017/CBO9781107295469](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781107295469). The DOI resolves to the Cambridge book page. What follows is from the open introduction excerpt, not from a reading of chapters 2–9.

The book studies long-vowel change from about 1050 to 1700, using occasional spellings in LAEME, LALME, and SMED. Stenbrenden says scholars usually split that history in two. One group is late Old English or early Middle English: unrounding of *ȳ*, monophthongization of *ēo*, southern rounding and raising of *ā*, and northern fronting of *ō*. The other group is the Great Vowel Shift. On the account she reports as general, that shift began around 1400 and was completed about 1750. In it, the non-close vowels rose one step, and the close vowels *ī* and *ū* diphthongized.

She treats push-chain and drag-chain as descriptive labels, not explanations, and says both are open to empirical test. She argues that functional stories in which a language shifts in order to avoid merger are teleological, and that a phonetic basis has to be found for recurring types of change. The introduction does not itself name that phonetic cue.

Later outcomes she lists as following the shift, in the traditional diagram: most reflexes of Middle English open *ē* reach [iː], with *yea*, *break*, *steak*, and *great* as diphthong exceptions; late Middle English *ā* ends as [eɪ]; the reflex of Middle English open *ō* ends as [oʊ], and as /əʊ/ in Received Pronunciation.

The conclusion that there was no single process called the Great Vowel Shift is reported in [[sources/ikegashira-2020-stenbrenden-review]]. It is not established by the introduction alone.
