# Ikegashira 2020: Review of Stenbrenden

Atsuko Ikegashira, review of Gjertrud Flermoen Stenbrenden, *Long-Vowel Shifts in English, c. 1050–1700*, *English Linguistics* 36.2 (2020): 295–304. The copy used here is the J-STAGE PDF. The reviewer is at Otsuma Women’s University; the PDF gives the address `aikegashira@otsuma.ac.jp`. Received 29 November 2018, accepted 14 August 2019.

Ikegashira’s own summary of the field, before she turns to the book:

- The name “Great Vowel Shift” comes from Jespersen (1909), *A Modern English Grammar*, Part I.
- The usual contents are diphthongization of *ī* and *ū*, and raising of the other long vowels.
- The consecutive changes are conventionally dated from the fifteenth century.
- A drag-chain account, high vowels first, is attributed to Jespersen (1909), Dobson (1968), Prins (1972), and Stockwell (1972).
- A push-chain account, mid vowels first, is attributed to Luick (1914–40) and Lass (1976).
- A simultaneous, non-teleological account is attributed to Jordan and Crook (1974).

She reports Stenbrenden’s conclusion in these terms: there is no unique process called the Great Vowel Shift, but a continuous, simultaneous movement of raising and diphthongization from about the tenth century to the sixteenth or seventeenth. A phonetic cue is required, and that cue has not been identified. She also reports Stenbrenden’s claim, citing p. 313, that isolating some of these changes under the Great Vowel Shift label misses how the quantitative changes of English vowels fit the larger history. On p. 321, as reported here, raising of Middle English *ā* is associated with middle-class, upwardly mobile women from London, following Raumolin-Brunberg (2006) on who leads early modern changes.

**Limits.** This is a review, so claims about spelling dates inside the book are second-hand. Table 9.2 in the PDF is too badly extracted to use cell by cell. One reference line prints “Stockwell, Peter (1972)”; the 1972 paper on the vowel shift is Robert Stockwell’s. That slip is the reviewer’s, or the PDF layer’s, and is not followed here.
