# Biggam 2013: Anglo-Saxon Plant-Name Studies

C. P. Biggam, “An Introduction to Anglo-Saxon Plant-Name Studies and to this Special Issue,” *Leeds Studies in English*, n.s. 44 (2013), 1–9, the opening of *Magic and Medicine: Early Medieval Plant-Name Studies*. The introduction was read. Author: [[entities/carole-biggam]]. A Glasgow teaching unit on [[concepts/old-english|Old English]] plants, on the Historical Thesaurus site, was read as her later plain-language statement of the same project.

Biggam dates the surviving written records mostly from the seventh century to about 1100. She says the principal evidence is manuscripts, so the illiterate majority, mostly agricultural workers and country-dwellers with regional names, cannot pass those names on directly. That is “a great loss of evidence.” Educated writers, almost all ecclesiastics, knew many English plant-names, but they did not represent every village, and Latin often shaped the names they wrote. Latin *canis caput* is translated *hundes heafod*, both “dog’s head.” She asks whether any Anglo-Saxon villager would have used that English name. A glossary or herbal pair shows only that the scribe who added the English believed the two names equivalent. The English name may have meant something else elsewhere, may have been unknown to much of the population, or may have been a physician’s technical term.

The teaching unit states the quantitative judgment the article leaves qualitative: only a small proportion of Old English plant-names is likely to have been recorded, because much larger numbers of folk-names are known from later times. The comparison she uses is the foxglove, with 93 recorded English folk-names in the British Isles and at least 45 in Somerset alone. She treats comparable numbers in Old and Middle English as highly likely. One species can have many names, and one descriptive name, such as “wound-healer,” can denote different species in different districts. A small record cannot recover that system.

The same unit separates a missing referent from a missing record. Searches for *tulip* and *potato* return nothing because those plants were not yet in England. It also separates a lexical record from a practice. Herbal texts describe the plant’s role in a remedy. Archaeology of well-healed fractures shows that setting and splinting also belonged to treatment, and the texts do not describe that whole procedure. Dye-plants are a case where words and remains meet: *mæddre* “madder” and *wad* “woad” occur in texts and place-names, and archaeobotany finds dye traces and plant remains.

Peter Bierbaumer, *Der botanische Wortschatz des Altenglischen*, 3 vols. (Bern: Lang, 1975–79), is the collection she points to for the attested vocabulary. This research did not re-read Bierbaumer.
