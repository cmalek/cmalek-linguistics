# Banham and Faith 2014: Anglo-Saxon Farms and Farming

Debby Banham and Rosamond Faith, *Anglo-Saxon Farms and Farming* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014). The publisher preview of the introduction was read, through the survey of written sources and the turn to archaeology. The crop, livestock, and landscape chapters were not read.

Banham writes that one reason farming has been under-studied is the paucity of primary written evidence. Anglo-Saxon writers cannot be said to have taken a huge interest in how their food was produced. The usable Old English estate texts are few: *Rectitudines singularum personarum*, *Gerefa*, and the eleventh-century Ely Farming Memoranda. *Gerefa* lists a reeve’s equipment and seasonal work, but it lifts batches of words from glossaries and other texts. Much of that vocabulary is otherwise unknown, so the text has to be used with caution. Ine’s laws, late seventh century, concern farming more than other codes and still leave most agrarian activities unlegislated. Irish and Scandinavian law, by contrast, support whole farming monographs. Royal diplomas care about boundaries, and those clauses, with some wills, are where crops and livestock surface. No narrative source is concerned primarily or even secondarily with farming. Ælfric’s *Colloquy* and the Latin–Old English glossaries help with agricultural vocabulary precisely because that vocabulary is sparsely attested and often obscure. Bede’s month-names incidentally record early agriculture.

She treats Margaret Gelling and Ann Cole’s “language of landscape” as a precise vocabulary for hills, streams, and roads, a way of seeing those features as Anglo-Saxon speakers distinguished them. Place-names and charter bounds are evidence for land use. Archaeology carries the weight where literacy does not: farming “scarcely came into the orbit of literate culture.”

A review by a place-name reader, available as the Glasgow eprint of the *Speculum* review, notes that the book itself says some words appear to be unique to place-names (pp. 207–8) and would have been strengthened by Hough’s 2010 lecture. That review was skimmed for this point only.
