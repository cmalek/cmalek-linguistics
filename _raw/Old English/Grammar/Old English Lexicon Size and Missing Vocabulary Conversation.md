# Old English Lexicon Size and Missing Vocabulary Conversation

## User

what is the average size of the working vocabulary across the most spoken languages today

## Assistant

There is no scientifically reliable single average across the world’s most-spoken languages, because “word” and “working vocabulary” are not measured consistently across languages.

A useful rule of thumb for an adult native speaker is:

- **Regularly used, active vocabulary:** roughly **3,000–10,000 lemmas**, with about **5,000** as a practical midpoint.
- **Receptive vocabulary**—words understood but not necessarily used: roughly **20,000–50,000 lemmas**.
- **Everyday conversational core:** approximately **2,000–3,000 high-frequency word families** account for most ordinary speech. In English, about 1,800 words provide over 80% of spoken-corpus coverage. [Cambridge overview](https://www.cambridge.org/elt/teacher-support/pdf/McCarten-Teaching-Vocabulary.pdf)

For comparison, a large study estimated that the average 20-year-old American English speaker recognizes about **42,000 lemmas**, corresponding to **11,100 word families**—but recognition could be as shallow as merely knowing that a word exists. [Brysbaert et al.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01116/full)

So, if you need one compact answer:

> Across major languages, an ordinary adult probably has a practical working vocabulary on the order of **5,000 actively accessible words or word families**, while understanding several times that number.

Treat that as an informed approximation, not a measured multilingual mean. Mandarin compounds, Arabic root-and-pattern formations, Hindi inflection, and English derivation make raw “word” counts fundamentally non-equivalent; linguists note that even defining a comparable word across languages is difficult. [PLOS One](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0281041)

## Assistant

Yes—the revised question is much more tractable. The figure of **200,000 words for an ordinary modern English speaker is almost certainly an artifact of permissive counting**, not 200,000 independently known dictionary headwords.

For your purposes, the best modern comparison is approximately:

- **17,000 base words** under a strict definition requiring meaningful receptive knowledge in educated adults. [Goulden, Nation & Read 1990](https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/11/4/341/245279)
- **42,000 lemmas at age 20**, rising to about **48,000 at age 60**, under a relatively permissive recognition test. The 20-year-old range was about 27,000–52,000 between the 5th and 95th percentiles. [Brysbaert et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4965448/)
- The latter study also estimated around **4,200 recognized non-transparent expressions**, which illustrates that a mental lexicon contains more than isolated lemmas.

The authors explicitly note that their test’s “knowledge” can be as shallow as recognizing that an item is a real word. If the criterion is “recognizes the word and can give an approximately correct definition,” I would use **roughly 20,000–40,000 lemmas** for a literate modern adult, not 200,000.

The high estimates generally arise by counting inflected forms, transparent derivatives, compounds, proper names, specialized terminology, or every item that can be inferred morphologically. One influential study estimated **88,500 distinct items in printed school English**, but that was an estimate of the vocabulary found in the literature—not the number known by each reader. [Nagy & Anderson 1984](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/how-many-words-are-there-in-printed-school-english/)

## What this means for Old English

There are three quantities that must be kept separate:

1. **The attested lexicon:** conventional lexemes that happen to survive in writing.
2. **The contemporary communal lexicon:** all conventional lexemes available somewhere within an Old English speech community.
3. **An individual mental lexicon:** the subset recognized by a particular speaker.

Bosworth–Toller is not a clean count of any of these. One computational study describes **62,745 Bosworth–Toller headwords**, but many are spellings, inflected forms, cross-references, or other lexicographic artifacts. A more conservative scholarly estimate is only **23,000–24,000 lexical items in surviving Old English**, while a count of main parts of speech in online Bosworth–Toller yielded just over 40,000 entries. [Minkova’s summary of Kastovsky and the dictionaries](https://varieng.helsinki.fi/series/volumes/16/minkova/index.html)

The surviving Dictionary of Old English Corpus has only about **three million running Old English words**, spread across centuries, dialects, genres, authors and manuscript copies. [DOEC description](https://varieng.helsinki.fi/CoRD/corpora/DOEC/) That is quite a small and heavily biased sample of a language—especially one whose surviving writing overrepresents religion, law, medicine and learned translation while badly underrepresenting domestic conversation, trades, children, women’s activities, local ecology and routine agricultural work.

Consequently:

> The normalized attested lexicon may contain roughly 24,000–35,000 defensible lexemes, but this is almost certainly only a subset of the actual communal Old English lexicon.

## A defensible estimate for an educated Anglo-Saxon

My proposed starting hypothesis would be:

> A mature, educated Anglo-Saxon probably recognized approximately **20,000–35,000 conventional Old English lexemes**, with a deliberately broad plausible interval of perhaps **15,000–40,000**.

That is a research prior, not a result. It rests on the following reasoning:

- Modern educated adults recognize approximately 17,000 base words under strict testing or around 40,000–50,000 lemmas under permissive testing.
- Old English speakers had the same human cognitive capacity and equally complex communicative lives.
- Their technological and institutional vocabularies were smaller in some areas, but their ecological, agricultural, kinship, craft and oral-cultural vocabularies could be much richer than the surviving texts suggest.
- Old English compounding and derivation allowed speakers to understand or create many transparent formations without necessarily storing each as an independent lexeme.
- An educated Anglo-Saxon would probably possess specialist ecclesiastical, legal or administrative terminology, while knowing fewer terms from crafts outside his experience.
- A genuinely learned speaker might possess a substantial **Latin lexicon in addition to**, rather than inside, the Old English total.

The apparent agreement between a 20,000–35,000 personal estimate and a roughly 24,000–35,000 normalized dictionary inventory would be partly accidental. The dictionary simultaneously:

- includes words no single speaker would know—different periods, dialects, professions and poetic traditions;
- omits words known in speech but never preserved;
- splits some items that should be combined;
- combines or fails to distinguish some senses that speakers may have represented separately.

You will also need to define “educated Anglo-Saxon” much more narrowly. A Kentish cleric in 750, a West Saxon monk in 1000, and a bilingual aristocrat in 1100 did not draw from the same lexical population. A particularly useful reference persona might be:

> A mature, literate West Saxon ecclesiastic around 975–1025, able to read vernacular prose and possessing at least functional Latin.

That population is unusually well aligned with the surviving record, although it is not representative of Anglo-Saxons generally.

## Finding expected but unattested words

This should not be framed as producing a flat list of “missing words.” It should produce **candidate unattested lexemes with graded evidence**. I would combine five methods.

### 1. Semantic-domain gaps

Use the [Thesaurus of Old English](https://oldenglishthesaurus.arts.gla.ac.uk/classification/) as the organizing ontology. It already contains detailed domains for animals, plants, agriculture, implements and activities.

Within each domain, compare:

- concepts demonstrably present in Anglo-Saxon life;
- concepts with an attested generic term but no specific term;
- unusually empty categories;
- comparable terminology in better-attested domains.

A gap is suggestive only when the referent is independently known to have been salient. Modern taxonomies should not be imposed mechanically: an Anglo-Saxon need not have lexicalized every species distinguished by a modern botanist.

### 2. Material-culture alignment

Build concept inventories from archaeological and historical evidence:

- excavated tools and household objects;
- archaeobotanical and zooarchaeological species;
- textile manufacture;
- agriculture and food preparation;
- shipbuilding and woodworking;
- bodily care, childhood and domestic labor;
- customary law and local administration.

Then align those referents with TOE and dictionary senses. A physically attested, frequently used object with no surviving ordinary name is a strong lexical-gap candidate.

### 3. Comparative Germanic evidence

Search Old Frisian, Old Saxon, Old High German and Old Norse for cognate or structurally parallel terms. A candidate becomes much stronger when:

- the concept existed in Anglo-Saxon England;
- cognates occur in more than one neighboring Germanic branch;
- the projected Old English form follows regular sound correspondences;
- there is no known Old English replacement;
- the meaning is temporally appropriate.

This can establish that a form is **probable**, but usually not that it was definitely an Old English lexeme. A reconstructed Proto-Germanic ancestor might have been lost before the Old English period.

### 4. Morphological paradigms

Look for gaps in productive derivational and compounding patterns:

- agent nouns corresponding to common verbs;
- names of tools formed from an action;
- plant or animal compounds built from attested heads;
- feminine, diminutive or collective derivatives;
- antonymic or privative formations;
- occupational compounds.

Here the crucial distinction is between:

- a word speakers could have understood if coined;
- a word speakers probably used conventionally;
- a word historically inherited from an earlier stage.

Productive morphology provides evidence for the first much more readily than for the other two.

### 5. Statistical unseen-vocabulary estimates

Frequency-of-frequency models can estimate how much lexical probability remains in unseen types. Hapax legomena are especially informative. However, they cannot reliably tell you the final number of missing lexemes, because rare classes can have an arbitrarily long tail and the Old English corpus is not a random sample. Research on unseen vocabulary explicitly warns that no globally preferable estimator exists. [Evert and Baroni’s overview](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220874854_Knowing_the_Unseen_Estimating_Vocabulary_Size_over_Unseen_Samples)

For Old English, these models would work best as:

- estimates within a defined genre, period and dialect;
- comparisons between progressively enlarged corpus samples;
- lower bounds on unseen lexical mass;
- tests of whether particular semantic domains are undersampled.

They should not be used alone to announce that “Old English had exactly 47,200 words.”

## Strong formulations of the questions

I would sharpen the research questions to:

1. **Which concepts demonstrably salient in Anglo-Saxon life lack an attested Old English lexicalization in the surviving corpus?**
2. **For each lexical gap, what evidence supports an unattested conventional lexeme rather than circumlocution, polysemy or productive nonce formation?**
3. **How much of the period-and-dialect-specific Old English lexicon is likely absent from the surviving textual record?**
4. **What receptive lexicon size is plausible for specified Anglo-Saxon speaker profiles, after separating ordinary Old English, specialist terminology and Latin?**

The most defensible near-term conclusions would probably be ranges and confidence classes—not a reconstructed dictionary pretending that every proposed word is equally real.

## Best starting set

1. **Dieter Kastovsky, “Semantics and Vocabulary”**, in *The Cambridge History of the English Language*, vol. 1 (1992), pp. 290–408.

   This is probably the single most important orientation to the structure of the Old English lexicon. It discusses the familiar estimate of roughly 23,000–24,000 attested lexical items, productive word formation, compounds, derivation and why the surviving vocabulary cannot be treated as a closed inventory.

2. **I. S. P. Nation and Averil Coxhead, *Measuring Native-Speaker Vocabulary Size* (2021).**

   This directly addresses why published adult estimates range from 12,000 to over 200,000 words. Chapters 3, 6, 9 and 10—dictionary sampling, adult vocabulary studies, measurement and test construction—are especially relevant. It should help you formulate exactly what “know a word” and “word” mean in your project. [Publisher and contents](https://benjamins.com/catalog/z.233)

3. **Marc Brysbaert et al., “How Many Words Do We Know?” (2016).**

   The best compact modern empirical model to adapt. Pay particular attention to its distinctions among word forms, lemmas, word families, proper names and non-transparent multiword expressions—not merely to the 42,000-lemma result. [Open-access article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4965448/)

4. **Jane Roberts and Christian Kay, with Lynne Grundy, *A Thesaurus of Old English*, 2nd ed. (2000).**

   This should be the conceptual backbone of the missing-vocabulary study. Rather than asking which Modern English words lack translations, you can identify comparatively sparse Old English semantic fields and historically plausible conceptual gaps. The introduction’s discussion of classification and uncertain meanings is particularly valuable. [Free online thesaurus](https://oldenglishthesaurus.arts.gla.ac.uk/) and [publication details](https://www.degruyter.com/document/isbn/9789004488922/html)

5. **The Dictionary of Old English and Dictionary of Old English Corpus.**

   The DOE’s principles and citations are more useful for this research than treating Bosworth–Toller entries as a flat word list. The corpus contains approximately three million running Old English words and aims to include at least one witness of every surviving text. [DOE project](https://doe.artsci.utoronto.ca/?page_id=11) and [corpus description](https://varieng.helsinki.fi/CoRD/corpora/DOEC/)

6. **The Nerthus Project.**

   This may be especially useful computationally. Nerthus integrates textual and lexicographic evidence, word formation, lemmatization, lexical relations and semantic analysis; its current database describes 32,812 headword files. Its publications include studies of derivational paradigms, lost adjectives and lexical gaps. [Project](https://www.nerthusproject.com/) · [database description](https://www.nerthusproject.com/database-description) · [publications](https://www.nerthusproject.com/publications)

## For establishing the observed inventory

- **Anna Cichosz et al., *A Frequency Dictionary of Old English Prose for Learners of Old English and Historical Linguists* (2022).**  
  This is freely downloadable and gives frequency and textual dispersion for more than 2,700 lemmas occurring at least 25 times in the York–Toronto–Helsinki Parsed Corpus. Dispersion—the number of independent texts containing a word—may prove more valuable than raw frequency when estimating likely speaker familiarity. [Open-access book](https://dspace.uni.lodz.pl/xmlui/handle/11089/42506)

- **Mark Faulkner, “Corpus Philology: Using the Dictionary of Old English to Get Bigger Data for Old English Spelling Variation” (2023).**  
  Although about spelling, this is an excellent worked example of combining DOE headwords, attested spellings and corpus searches while measuring precision and recall. It also exposes the scale of Bosworth–Toller’s headword and generated-form problem. [Article](https://academic.oup.com/dsh/article/38/4/1508/7308409)

  A useful comparison of Bosworth–Toller, Clark Hall and the DOE, particularly their treatment of headwords, variant spellings, cross-references and textual evidence. [Article](https://publicaciones.unirioja.es/ojs/index.php/jes/article/view/3208)

## For estimating unseen vocabulary statistically

- **R. Harald Baayen, *Word Frequency Distributions* (2001).**  
  The standard substantial treatment of vocabulary-growth curves, frequency spectra, hapax legomena and large-number-of-rare-events models. It provides the mathematical background needed to avoid naïve type-token extrapolation. [Book description](https://books.google.co.uk/books?id=FWicxYWJP9sC)

- **Suma Bhat and Richard Sproat, “Knowing the Unseen: Estimating Vocabulary Size over Unseen Samples” (2009).**  
  A relatively short, freely available paper comparing unseen-vocabulary estimators. Particularly important is its finding that classical Good–Turing methods tend to underestimate vocabulary growth. [Paper and PDF](https://aclanthology.org/P09-1013/)

I would use these methods to estimate unseen vocabulary within controlled subcorpora—not the total Old English language at once. For example:

- Ælfrician prose versus other prose;
- medical texts versus religious prose;
- early versus late West Saxon;
- poetry versus prose;
- independent works rather than duplicated manuscript witnesses.

That lets you validate an estimator by hiding one corpus partition and asking how accurately another predicts its new lemmas.

## For comparative reconstruction

- **Guus Kroonen, *Etymological Dictionary of Proto-Germanic* (2013).**  
  Essential for determining whether a seemingly missing Old English word has cognates in neighboring Germanic languages and a plausible inherited preform. It contains approximately 2,800 Proto-Germanic headwords plus derived formations. [Bibliographic record](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/publications/etymological-dictionary-of-proto-germanic-2/)

- **Don Ringe and Ann Taylor, *The Development of Old English* (2014).**  
  The relevant foundation for projecting Proto-West-Germanic forms into plausible Old English outcomes. Chapters on Proto-West-Germanic and Old English phonological and morphological developments will help prevent merely “Old-English-looking” inventions. [Book and contents](https://academic.oup.com/book/36266)

- **Dieter Kastovsky, “The Typological Status of Old English Word-Formation” (1990), pp. 205–224.**  
  Useful for deciding whether a missing formation is historically probable, synchronically productive or merely structurally possible. [Publication record](https://www.benjamins.com/catalog/cilt.65.14kas)

Comparative reconstruction should produce evidence labels such as *inherited with high confidence*, *probable West Germanic formation*, *possible native formation* and *transparent hypothetical coinage*. Those should not all enter the reconstructed lexicon on equal terms.

## For plants, animals, tools and daily life

- **The Dictionary of Old English Plant Names and the Anglo-Saxon Plant-Name Survey.**  
  Plant names are an ideal pilot domain because they illustrate nearly every difficulty you will face: Latin gloss equivalence, uncertain species identification, folk taxonomy, multiple names, manuscript corruption and misleading modern translations. The project collected research beyond the older dictionaries. [Project background](https://www.fwf.ac.at/en/research-radar/10.55776/P21064)

- **Carole P. Biggam and the Anglo-Saxon Plant-Name Survey publications.**  
  Biggam’s work is especially valuable methodologically: it demonstrates why an Old English gloss of a Latin plant name does not automatically establish botanical identity. [Survey discussion and bibliography](https://digital.library.leeds.ac.uk/12201/1/LSE2013.pdf)

- **Peter Bierbaumer, *The Old English Complex Plant Names: A Linguistic Survey and a Catalogue*.**  
  Particularly relevant to deciding whether compounds should count as conventional lexemes or transparent formations. Its introduction notes 336 surviving native botanical names. [Introduction](https://beckassets.blob.core.windows.net/product/preamble/12791904/9783631642696_intro_005.pdf)

- **Debby Banham and Rosamond Faith, *Anglo-Saxon Farms and Farming* (2014).**  
  This explicitly combines texts, archaeology, place-names and linguistic evidence to examine crops, livestock, tools and techniques. It is almost a ready-made source of concepts whose lexical attestation you can test. [Book and contents](https://academic.oup.com/book/11963)

- **Helena Hamerow, David Hinton and Sally Crawford, eds., *The Oxford Handbook of Anglo-Saxon Archaeology* (2011).**  
  The sections on food production, plants, animal husbandry, raw materials and craft production would support a material-culture concept inventory independent of the textual record. [Contents](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/27981)

- **Maren Clegg Hyer and Gale Owen-Crocker, eds., *The Material Culture of Daily Living in the Anglo-Saxon World* (2011).**  
  Especially useful for household objects, clothing, food production and ordinary activities that religious and literary texts underrepresent. [Scholarly review](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/tmr/article/view/17697)

## Assistant

I agree with both observations. They expose why the published counts are talking past one another.

### Bosworth–Toller entries

The digital BT count is not a lexeme count. The electronic transcription contains both the 1898 main volume and the 1921 supplement as separate source layers. The supplement may:

- introduce a genuinely new headword;
- add citations or senses to an existing headword;
- correct or replace an earlier analysis;
- record an inflected form;
- provide a spelling variant;
- redirect the reader elsewhere.

The Germanic Lexicon Project confirms that both volumes are present in the downloadable transcription. [Project description](https://www.germanic-lexicon-project.org/texts/oe_bosworthtoller_about.html)

Consequently, figures such as Mark Faulkner’s **62,745 BT headwords** should be understood as something like “machine-recognized headword records,” not 62,745 distinct Old English lexemes. Similarly, the count of just over 40,000 obtained by searching the online BT for major parts of speech still includes alternative spellings and other duplicate representations.

Kastovsky’s **23,000–24,000 lexical items** is closer to the quantity you actually care about, but it is explicitly only a rough scholarly count.

I don’t think there is presently a defensible exact answer to “how many unique entries are in BT,” because *unique* requires editorial decisions that BT itself does not encode consistently. At minimum, one would need to count several different levels:

| Level | What is counted |
|---|---|
| Entry records | Every main-volume and supplement entry block |
| Headword strings | Distinct printed headword spellings |
| Lemmas | Inflected forms and spelling variants collapsed |
| Lexemes | Lemmas separated or combined according to meaning and part of speech |
| Word families | Transparent derivatives and perhaps compounds grouped together |

For example, a supplement record that adds a quotation should disappear at the lemma level, while a supplement record introducing a genuinely new sense might remain part of the same lemma but add a lexeme or sense. A spelling cross-reference should normally disappear; a substantivized participle might or might not.

So I would not seek one “corrected BT total.” I would produce a **BT reconciliation table** with relationships such as:

- `SUPPLEMENTS`
- `VARIANT_OF`
- `INFLECTED_FORM_OF`
- `DERIVED_FROM`
- `SAME_LEMMA_AS`
- `DISTINCT_LEXEME_FROM`
- `CROSS_REFERENCE_TO`
- `CORRECTS_OR_REJECTS`

That would let you publish reproducible counts at every level rather than burying the lexicographic choices inside a single number.

Nerthus is useful as an independent comparison here: its current database reports **32,812 headword files**, drawing principally from Clark Hall and secondarily from BT, Campbell and DOE. That is still not automatically 32,812 lexemes, but its relational and morphological organization may save a great deal of reconciliation work. [Nerthus database description](https://www.nerthusproject.com/database-description)

### The three-million-word DOE Corpus

The DOE’s “approximately three million words” means **three million running Old English tokens**: three million occurrences separated as words in the surviving texts. It includes inflected forms as they actually occur, but it does not mean three million distinct inflected forms.

If a text contains:

> se cyning … þæs cyninges … þām cyninge

those are three corpus tokens and three surface forms, but normally one lemma, *cyning*. If *se* occurs another 20,000 times, every occurrence contributes to the three-million-token total.

Thus the relevant hierarchy is:

```text
3,000,000 running OE tokens
        ↓ deduplicate spelling
attested surface-form types
        ↓ morphological analysis
normalized lemmas
        ↓ distinguish meanings
lexemes and senses
```

The DOE corpus also contains roughly another million words of Latin, separately identified in the corpus description. [DOEC overview](https://varieng.helsinki.fi/CoRD/corpora/DOEC/)

### Why 17 million generated forms is entirely plausible

Your `wyrdcraeft` result is not in tension with the DOE figure. It is measuring a different universe:

- DOE: forms that **actually occur**, with repetition, in surviving texts.
- `wyrdcraeft`: forms that the morphology permits the generator to **construct**.
- BT/DOE dictionaries: lexicographically recognized **headwords or lemmas**.

One lemma can generate many combinations of:

- case, number and gender;
- person, number, tense and mood;
- strong and weak variants;
- participial forms;
- syncopated and unsyncopated forms;
- dialectal alternants;
- orthographic variation;
- prefixes or separable morphological analyses.

Faulkner’s experiment is strikingly close to yours: a morphologizer generated about **14.5 million putative forms from 62,745 BT headword records**. Expanding three common spelling alternations raised the theoretical search space to approximately **115 million strings**. [Faulkner 2023](https://academic.oup.com/dsh/article/38/4/1508/7308409)

That makes `wyrdcraeft`’s 17 million unsurprising. But I would inspect exactly what an “entry” represents:

- unique surface strings;
- `(lemma, feature bundle, surface string)` analyses;
- dialect-specific realizations;
- alternative derivational paths;
- or database rows containing duplicates produced by different rules.

Those quantities can differ by orders of magnitude. In a morphologically ambiguous language, a single string may correspond to several lemma-and-feature analyses.

### The important consequence

The DOE’s three million tokens tell you about the **size of the surviving sample**, not the size of the Old English vocabulary. The 17 million generated analyses tell you about the **productive capacity of a morphological model**, not how many conventional words speakers stored. BT’s entry count tells you about the **architecture and history of a dictionary**, not directly about either quantity.

The quantity you ultimately want is between these levels:

> the number of conventional lexemes an individual could recognize, excluding predictable inflections but including lexicalized derivatives, compounds and participles.

That is precisely why the BT reconciliation work could become a substantive research contribution of its own. Once main entries, supplemental additions, variants, forms and genuine new lexemes are distinguished, you would have a much better empirical base than the currently repeated figures of 24,000, 40,000 or 62,745.

## Assistant

I sympathize. The project digitized BT as a historical printed artifact, but the interface presents it as though it were a modern dictionary. That leaves the reader performing the editorial join manually—exactly the work software should remove.

A better interface would provide a composite entry:

```text
Normalized lemma
├── Bosworth–Toller main entry
├── Toller supplement: additions/corrections
├── Campbell addenda and corrigenda
├── variant and inflected-form redirects
└── consolidated citations and senses
```

Each piece could retain its provenance and original scan, so merging would not erase the dictionary’s history. Ambiguous cases could simply be marked “possible match” rather than used as an excuse not to merge obvious ones.

There is legitimate editorial difficulty: a supplement record may extend, correct, supersede or merely resemble the main entry. But the majority of relationships should be mechanically recoverable from headword identity, explicit cross-references and alphabetical proximity, followed by human review. Even a provisional `SUPPLEMENTS` link and a “show unified entry” button would dramatically improve daily use.

In effect, the current site is a very good searchable transcription, not a fully edited digital dictionary. The distinction matters because its URL-level “entries” look countable even though they are actually fragments of a layered publication. Nerthus appears much closer to the relational model that BT’s interface ought to have adopted.

## Assistant

A defensible estimate for the **full communal lexicon of Modern English** is approximately:

> **500,000–1,000,000 conventional lexemes**, with perhaps **700,000–800,000** as a reasonable working estimate.

The range depends heavily on what is counted.

| Scope | Approximate size |
|---|---:|
| Current general English—ordinary, regional, slang and moderately specialized vocabulary | **300,000–400,000** |
| Broad modern English including extensive scientific, technical and professional terminology | **500,000–1,000,000** |
| Historical English, including obsolete vocabulary and phrases | **600,000+** documented items |
| All predictable inflections, compounds and systematic technical names | Millions, with no meaningful finite ceiling |

Oxford’s current-English dictionary contains more than **350,000 words and phrases** drawn from global varieties of English. [Oxford Languages](https://languages.oup.com/google-dictionary-en/) That is a curated dictionary, however, rather than an exhaustive inventory of every technical and regional lexeme.

The OED describes itself somewhat differently depending on the counting unit:

- current OUP announcements call it a historical dictionary containing more than **500,000 entries**;
- OUP’s broader description says it documents more than **600,000 words, past and present**;
- an OED accounting reported about **636,000 lexical items** and **864,000 senses**.

These are not contradictions so much as counts of entries, lexical items, phrases, derivatives and senses under different editorial systems. ## The useful comparison with Old English

For your research, I would distinguish these Modern English quantities:

- **Individual educated speaker:** perhaps 20,000–50,000 recognized lemmas.
- **General dictionary lexicon:** approximately 300,000–400,000 contemporary words and expressions.
- **Whole communal lexicon:** approximately 500,000–1,000,000, once specialist and regional vocabulary is included.
- **Morphological possibility space:** effectively open-ended.

Thus no individual comes close to knowing the full Modern English lexicon. A well-read person recognizing 40,000–50,000 lemmas might know only around **5–10% of the broad communal lexicon**.

That distinction is highly relevant to Old English. Even if a normalized surviving inventory contained 25,000–35,000 lexemes, it would not follow that:

- an individual speaker knew all of them; or
- the communal Old English lexicon contained only that many.

The dictionary aggregates across dialects, centuries and specialist communities, while the surviving corpus omits much of the spoken and occupational vocabulary. Those forces run in opposite directions, just as they do—more visibly—with Modern English.
