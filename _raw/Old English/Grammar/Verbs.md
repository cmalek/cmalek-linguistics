This guide to Old English grammar gives descriptions and guidelines for all the verb types: weak verbs (classes I, II, III), strong verbs (classes 1-7), preterite[^1]\-present verbs, the anomolous or irregular verbs, contract verbs and impersonal constructions.

We get the terms strong and weak from Jacob Grimm (yes, one of the Grimm’s Fairy Tales authors), based on how each verb forms the past tense \[p. 66,  §3.7\].  Strong[^2] verbs form the past tense by internal vowel change (e.g. sing, sang, sung); weak verbs form the past tense via changing endings to have a dental suffix — \-d or \-t (e.g live, lived, lived).

* Weak verbs constitute almost 74% of OE verbs   
* Strong verbs constitute almost 24%  
* A few irregular verbs constitute the remaining 2%

\[p. 67, §3.7\]

To give a specific meaning to a verb, we conjugate it.  This allows us to change the mood (the speaker’s attitude towards the statements in the sentence), the tense (when it occured), the person (1st (I/we), 2nd (you), 3rd (they, other noun), the number (in OE, singular, dual, plural) and the aspect (how an action unfolds over time — continuing, finished, repeating).

Here is a guide to identifying the conjugation by the ending \[p. 96, §4.1.5\]:

* *\-an*: infinitives in OE (listed in glossaries and dictionaries) almost always end in *\-an.*    
  * Class I verbs end in *\-an*  
    * Class II weak verbs end in *\-ian*.  
  * *\-eþ*: Though there are other endings for the 3rd person singular (e.g. *\-aþ* for Class II weak verbs), *\-eþ* is distinctively 3rd person present tense*.* It maketh sense (makeþ), doth it not?  
  * *\-on*: This ending almost always represents the plural past tense, even in strong verbs.  In preterite-present verbs (helper verbs), however, this represents the plural present tense.  
  * *\-anne*, *\-enne*: the inflected infinitive

### Non-finite verb forms

So-called because they don’t carry **finite information**: person, number, tense, aspect or mood.

* **infinitive**:  The bare verb.  They are called *infinitives* because they carry no finite information: person, tense, mood, aspect or number \[p. 96, §4.1.5\].  OE infinitives do not take a starting tō like in ModE.  
  * **participle**: this is the part after a helper verb like *to have*, or *be* e.g. *have lived. “lived”* here is the past participle; in the sentence “*I am living”* living is the a participle (in this case, the *present participle, but it is the helper verb “be” in its present tense form that denotes the tense (and person, number, aspect, and mood for that matter))*.  Note that living itself carries no finite information.  
  * **inflected infinitive**:  these are infinitives that do require a preceding *t*ō.  In this tense, the verb (more accurately a **verbal**[^3]) acts as a noun (some grammars call it the **gerund**[^4], which in ModE we denote with a *\-ing* suffix for a verb, confusingly just like we do for the present participle) \[p. 77, §3.11.2\]

## The dimensions of verb conjugation

### Person and number

Person (1st, 2nd, 3rd) refers to who is acting and number refers to how many are acting. In OE, there are three cardinalities for number: singular, dual (2 things/people exactly), plural (more than 2 things/people).

* 1st person:  
  * Singular: I ate the spaghetti.  
  * Plural: We ate the spaghetti.  
* 2nd person:   
  * Singular: You ate the spaghetti.   
  * Plural: Y’all[^5] ate the spaghetti.  
* 3rd person:  
  * Singular: He ate the spaghetti/The man ate the spaghetti.  
  * Plural: They[^6] ate the spaghetti/The men ate the spaghetti.

### Tense

The tense of a verb tells you when the action happened: in the present, past or future.  Below, we exemplify this with the indicative mood.

* **indicative present**: I eat the spaghetti.  
* **indicative past**: I ate the spaghetti.  
* **indicative future**: I will eat the spaghetti[^7].
  
#### Future Tense in Old English

OE has no systematic way to signal the future tense and uses the present tense with an implied future meaning.  \[p. 134, §5.2.5\].  Thus OE has three different possible present tense translations:

| OE                  | tense           | ModE                     | tense                      |
| :------------------ | :-------------- | :----------------------- | :------------------------- |
| *hēo gāþ*           | present         | she goes (everyday)      | present                    |
| *hēo gāþ*           | present         | she is going (now)       | present progressive        |
| *hēo gāþ*           | present         | she will go              | future                     |
| *hēo gāþ*           | present         | she went                 | past                       |

| *No OE equivalent*  |                 | she will have gone       | future perfect             |
| *No OE equivalent*  |                 | she will have been going | future perfect progressive |


### Aspect

Aspect refers to how an action **unfolds over time**: ongoing (*progressive*), completed (*perfect*), repeated, etc.

### Mood

The mood of a verb indicates the speaker’s attitude towards the statements in the sentence \[pp. 71-74, §3.10.1\].  There are three moods: indicative, imperative and subjunctive.

#### **The indicative mood**

The **indicative** is used by the speaker to express that the speaker/writer believes this to be a statement of fact \[p. 71, §3.10.1\]: 

* *S*ē *hæle **gieldeþ** wurðscipe t*ō *þ*ǣ*m cyninge. (The hero **pays** his respects to the king.)*

#### **The imperative mood**

The **imperative** is used to express a command \[p. 71, §3.10.1\].  You will know it is the imperative because the sentence will lack a subject in OE (and in ModE).  This is an important point because mostly the imperative and subjunctive will have the same endings, and you’ll need to know how to identify each when translating. 

* ***G*****ā** *t*ō *þ*ā*m h*ū*se.* (*Go to the house.)*

#### **The subjunctive mood**

The **subjunctive** \[pp. 71-74, §3.10.1\] is the mood we use for expressing a wish, a desire or a hypothetical/doubtful/contrary situation.  In OE, we also use it after impersonal[^8] verbs.  “In general, if you suspect that a verb might be subjunctive try translating it with a ‘should’ or ‘might’ \+ the verb”:

* Expressing a wish or desire: *I wish that he **were** here* (Indicative: *He **was** here.)*  
  * *Nō ðæt ȳðe byð tō be-flēonne — **fremme** sē þe wille* — (Not at all is \[it\] easy to escape — try as he might he who will — \[Beowulf, II 1003-04\])  
  * Expressing an idea that is doubtful, hypothetical or contrary to fact: *If I **were** you, I wouldn’t do that.* (Indicative: *If I **was** you)*  
    * Ġ*yf mæsse-pr*ē*ost his* ā*gen lif r*ī*htli*ċ*e **fadie,** þonne is r*ī*ht þæt his wurðscipe **wexe.** (If a priest his own life rightly **lives**, then \[it\] is right that his honor **grows**.)*  
  * Expressing an obligation or command: *I command that he **go** to the meeting* (Indicative: *He **goes** to the meeting.*)  
    * *\[Hroðgar\] sume worde h*ē*t þæt i*ċ *his* ǣ*rest ð*ē ē*st* **ġe-sæde …** (Hrothgar by a certain word commanded that I first **should declare** to you his regard \[Beowulf, II 2156-57\])*  
  * Expressing a call to do something:   
    * ***Bidden** w*ē*. (Let us **pray.**)*  
  * After **impersonal**  verbs.  Example impersonal verbs in OE are *dafenian* “to be seemly, fitting” and *þyn*ċ*an* “to seem”.  
  * *Ūs dafenaþ ðær wē **wacien***. (*For us \[it\] is fitting that we should stay awake.*)

## Important, yet odd constructions

### Accusative \+ infinitive

In Old English, an accusative noun (or pronoun) with an infinitive is used in what we call the Accusative and Infinitive construction (A \+ Inf.). It is especially common after verbs of:

* **perception** (geseon “see”, gehȳran “hear”)  
* **commanding / causing** (hātan “command”, lǣtan “let”, macian “make”, wyċean “do, make, prepare”)  
* **mental activity** (witan “know”, þencan “think”, though less freely than Latin)

For this to apply, the accusative noun must be:

* Grammatically object of the main verb  
* Logically subject of the infinitive

Examples:

* Verbs of perception  
  * *Iċ **ġeseah** hine gān.*  (I saw him go).  *hine* \== accusaive; *gān* \== infinitive  
* Causative verbs  
  * *Hē **hēt** hine gān*.  (He commanded him to go).  *hine*  \== accusative,  *gān* \== infinitive

### Making a causitive verb from a strong verb

For example *drincan* (to drink) *\-\>* *drenċan* (to make drunk).   

You take the past singular 3rd person of the source verb (in this case, *dranc*) and use umlaut (i-mutation) to turn the *a* to an *e*; presumably in proto-OE, there was an *i* or a *j* in *drenċan,* so *\*draincan* or *\*drajncan*, which through umlaut causes the *a* to move to the front of the mouth, making it *e \-\> \*dreincan,*  and then the *i* was lost, so *drenċan*. 

### Weak Verb Collapsed Endings

The past tense ending of weak verbs consists of a dental suffix (*\-d* or *\-t*) and a personal ending (*\-e, \-est,* or  *\-on*).  Verbs whose roots **already end with a \-t or \-d** often absorb the following **\-(e)d** or **\-t** of the past tense ending since the ending consonants of the root and conjugation are so similar.

Such verbs will most often lose that final *\-(e)d* or *\-t,* making it a **collapsed ending**.

For example, s*endan* has past tense forms of *sende, sendest, sende,* and *sendon* instead of *send**ed**e, send**ed**est, send**ed**e,* and *send**ed**on.*  See? It has collapsed, losing its interior *\-ed.*

### Verbs followed immediately by a pronoun

When a verb ending with a consonant is followed directly by a pronoun, the pronoun "eats" the verb's trailing consonant.  Examples:

* *Ne **sceole ġē** swā sōfte    sinc ġegangan*  (You must not so easily acquire     our treasure).  *sceole* here really needs to be plural - *sceolon* - because of the subject *ġē* (2nd person nom pl), but the *ġē* eats the "n" and we don't typically end a word with "o" so it becomes *sceole*. \[*Battle of Maldon* line 59\]
* *Ne **þurfe wē** ūs spillan    ġif ġē spēdaþ tō þām;* ( We do not ourselves need to destroy     if you have success in this).  *þurfe* again needs to be plural - *þurfon* - because of the subject *wē*  (1st nom pl) but *wē* "eats" the "n" again. \[*Battle of Maldon*, line 34\]

### Verbs of motion can be dropped so long as the context is clear

When a clause needs a verb of motion, it may be omitted if it is obvious from the context that we need it.  Example:

* *Ne mihte þǣr for wætere    werod tō þām ōðrum;* (The host was not able **to go** there    to the others because of the water;).   Here the context is clear -- "they were not able ... to the others", so we can insert any verb of motion there to make it clear. \[*Battle of Maldon* line 64]

## Weak Verbs

### Weak Verbs: Class I

As opposed to Class II, most Class I verbs **end in *\-an***.  These verbs fall into two subtypes: 

* **verbs that are followed by a doubled consonant** (*fremman, lettan,* and *trymman*) or a **\[with a short vowel followed by a — ed\] single *r \+ i*** (*nerian, herian*)  
* **verbs with a long vowel** (*d*ē*man, h*ī*eran*) or **verbs with a short vowel followed by two consonants which are not typically doubled** (*sendan, wyrċan*).

Rules:

* v+K: short  
* possibly v+KK: short (where the K’s are the same letter), e.g. *fremman*  
* V+K: long  
* vv+K: long  
* v+K1K2  long, where K1 and K2 are different consonants  
* V+K1K2\.: long

\[p. 70, §3.9\]

These are the “classic” forms of OE verbs, which typically have simply a suffix change, although there are some Class I weak verbs that combine a suffix with an internal vowel change.  We will list them below.

### Weak Verbs: Class II

Infinitives for Class II weak verbs always end in *\-ian,* and mostly have different endings than those of Class I.

#### **Distinguishing between Class I and Class II**

When you are looking at a conjugated verb and trying to decide whether it is weak I or weak II, there is an ambiguity between weak  I and weak II verbs in the third person singular present tense.  In Class I, *\-eþ* denotes a third person singular present, while *\-aþ* is plural for all three persons.  In Class II, however, *\-aþ* denotes the third person present *singular* while *\-iaþ* denotes the plural.  Thus a verb ending in *\-aþ* could be either a Class I plural or Class II singular.  The only way to know the difference is to look up the infinitive and see if it ends in *\-an* (Class I) or *\-ian* (Class II) \[p. 80-82, §3.12.1\].

Past tense or past participle endings with an *\-o-* (sometimes an *\-a-*) are another clear sign that a weak verb belongs to Class II: *lufode* (Class I: *fremede*),  *lufodon* (Class I: fremedon), *lufod* (Class I: *fremed*).  Imperative singulars that end in *\-a* also point to Class II: *lufa* (Class I: *hīer*).

## Anomolous verbs

Some of the most important verbs in OE \[as in ModE — ed.\] have irregular patterns.  In many cases, these verbs consist of at least two originally separate verbs that have somehow fallen together.  In almost every Indo-European language, the “to be” verb appears as something of an irregular jumble. \[…\] The OE verb wesan “to be” is made up of several verbs which were originally separate but fell together to form a “composite” verb.

Issac says these are called “suppletive” forms – the new root has supplanted the old one without removing some of the old conjugations.

\[p. 100, *§*4.5\]

### “to be”: wesan, bēon

* *b*ē*on* is generally the equivalent of things that not change.  **It refers to a permanent state of being.**  Not a hard and fast thing though.  Think of this as being simliar to Spanish *ser*.  
* *wesan* describes things that *can* change.  Think of this as being similar to Spanish *estar*.

\[p. 101, *§*4.5.3\]

In later OE, they are interchangeable, and authors will use both *b*ē*on* and *wesan* to mean “to be.”

## Strong verbs

Strong verbs descend from PIE verbs, while weak verbs are a Germanic invention.  Strong verbs change a root vowel to form the past tense and the past participle, while weak verbs form the past tense and past participle by adding a dental suffix: *\-d, \-ed* or *\-t*.  This change in root vowel in other grammers is usually called ***gradation*** or ***ablaut*****,** and some grammars will refer to strong verbs as ***ablaut verbs*****.**  There are seven classes of these verbs, differentiated by how the internal vowel shift works in each class  \[pp. 254, §8.1\].

### Endings: All strong verbs

These rules govern the way strong verbs are formed:

1. The present indicative, present subjunctive, imperative, gerund and the present participle take their base form from the ***infinitive stem*** of the verb.  
   1. *bindan* (class 1 strong)  
      1. present indicative: *binde (1), bindest/bintst (2), bindeþ, bint (3)*  
      2. present subjunctive: *binde, binden*  
      3. imperative: *bind (s), bindaþ (pl)*  
      4. gerund: *tō bindanne*  
      5. present participle: *bindende*  
2. The past indicative second person singular, past indicative plural and past subjunctive take their base form from the ***past plural*** stem of the verb.  
   1. *bindan* (past plural stem: *bund-*)  
      1. past indicative second person singular: *bunde*  
      2. past indicative plural: *bundon*  
      3. past subjunctive: *bunde (s), bunden (pl)*

## Contract Verbs

\[pp. 336-345, , §11.1\]

A small but important subclass of strong verbs are the “**contract verbs**.”  These differ from regular strong verbse in that their infintive ends with *\-on* instead of *\-an*, and during conjugation an *h* or *g* can appear or disappear in various congugated forms.

These are verbs which between prehistoric Old English (c. 500-700 CE)  and Old English (700-1100 CE), various sound changes occured in these verbs to make them evolve into their late forms.  Here is an example with the verb *tēon*”to pull, tug”.   The evolution of the suppsed prehistoric form \**tēhan* to the late form *tēon* may have gone something like this.

* The *h* in \**tēhan* caused the preceding vowel to “break” into the dipthong *ēo*:  *\*tēhan \-\> \*tēohan*  
* In late prehistoric OE or early OE, the *h* was lost when it occured between two vowels: *\*tēohan \-\> \*tēoan*  
* Finally \-*ēoa-* **contracted** or simplified into *\-ēo-* : *\*tēoan \-\> tēon*

### History of the Second and Third Person Singular Present Indicative Forms

Here is an example of *tēon* to show how the **second person singular present indicative form**  of contract verbs may have occured: 

\**tēhis* \[Prehist Gmc; the suffix *\-is* is an early form; later *\-est* is used universally\] \-\>  
*\*tīhis* \[Prehist OE; mutation from PGmc **e** to PrehistOE **i** that occured in PGmc before i-mutation by several centuries\] \-\>   
\**tīohis* \[breaking before the *\-h*\] \-\>  
\**tīehis* \[OE i-mutation\] \-\>  
*tīehes(t)* \[change of unstressed **i** to **e**; the **\-est** suffix is universal in historical OE\] \-\>  
***tīehst*** \[syncopation\]

The **third person singular present indicative** form sequence may have gone like this:

\**tēhiþ* \[PGmc\] \-\>   
\**tīhiþ* \[Prehist OE, Gmc mutation\] \-\>   
*\*tīohiþ* \[breaking before **h**\] \-\>  
\**tīehiþ* \[i-mutation\] \-\>  
\**tīeheþ* \[change of unstressed **i** to **e**\] \-\>   
***tīehþ*** \[syncopation\]

### History of the Past Tense and Past Participle Forms

Strong verbs use **ablaut** (changing their root vowels for different forms) in their past tense forms and their past participle, the vowel series of which depends on which strong verb class they belong to (see above).  For *tēon,* a strong class 2 verb, the vowel series is 

***ēo** (infinitive) \- **ēa** (past singular) \- **u** (past plural) \- **o** (past particple).*

So we would expect the following conjugations for *tēon*:

* Past singular: *tēah*  
* Past plural: \**tuhon*  
* Past Participle: *\*tohen*

But we don’t: they are instead *tēah, tu**g**on and (ġe)to**g**en.* Where does the **g** come from when we expect an **h?**  

**This is Verner’s Law:** when surrounded by voiced sounds and preceded by an unaccented vowel (as in this case), a voiceless stop (like **h**) becomes voiced (**g**)*.*  The final **h** of *tēah*  does not satisfy Verner’s Law, so we retain the **h** (it is not between vowels)**;** the **h** in the past plural and past participle **do** satisfy Verner’s Law (**u/o** and **o/e** are voiced, and the **u** and **o** were unaccented in PGmc\[?\]), so **h \-\> g**.   Note how this is regular across the verbs.

Note that this happened in PGmc, not in PrehistOE; that may explain the “preceeded by an unaccented vowel”; in OE the first syllable is always accented, so it can’t have happened there.

## Impersonals

\[pp. 345-348, , §11.2\]

Impersonal constructions use “it” as a dummy noun in statements about natural processes, time, mental and physical states, etc.  Since the “it” doesn’t function like a normal subject, grammarians have called this construction an “impersonal.”

* *It* is raining.  
* *It* is half past two.  
* *It* seems to me …


Recall that pronouns, to be pronouns, must refer back to an actual noun.   In these cases however, “it” does not refer to anything, but is a stand-in subject for verbs that require such.    Impersonal constructions are in fact associated with certain verbs that describe

* conditions of the body  
* mental states (such as desire, belief, etc.)  
* natural processes  
* etc.

… all cases where the *doer* of the action is in some way “impersonal.”   Note that we still have such constructions in ModE, as in the examples above.  Observe people talking for a while and you will see just how often this kind of construction appears.

Here are some OE examples:

* ***Hit*** *rīnþ* (it is raining)  
* ***Hit** ðunrað* (it is thundering)  
* ***Hit** liht* (it is lightening)  
* ***Hit** hagelað* (it is hailing)  
* ***Hit** fryst* (it is frosting)  
* ***Hit** is wyrse nū.* (It is worse now.)  
* *Ġif **hit** sīe willa þīn* (If it be your will/desire)  
* *swā **hit** on bōcum cwiþ* (as it says in books)

OE impersonal constructions, however, can appear without the *hit*, causing such constructions to seem to lack a subject at all.   In this case you, as the translator, must supply the *it* as the subject in the appropriate place.

* *Hwīlum of heofnum hāte scīneð.* (Sometimes from heaven \[it\] shinse hotly.)  
* *Eaðe is tō understandenne.*  (\[It\] is easy to understand.)  
* *Forþan ūs dafeneþ ðæt wē waċien symle.* (Therefore \[it\] is appropriate for us that we always stay awake \[i.e. hold vigils\])  
* *Ðonne him ðyncð ðæt hē ryhte lade funden hæbbe.* (Then \[it\] seems to him that he has found the right way.)

Some impersonals are more difficult because they are not idiomatic in ModE; the most challenging of these are those that combine an impersonal subject with a reflexive pronoun.   In these cases it is often good to tranlate the reflexive pronoun as the subject after changing it into the appropriate form.

* *Ðonne **him** hingrað, hē yt grǣdlīċe.* (When he is hungry, he eats ravenously \[lit. when \[it\] hungers for him\])  
* *Ðonne **him** ðyrst, hē drincð.* (When he is thirsty, he drinks \[lit. when \[it\] thirsts for him\])  
* *Ðonne **him** cælð, hē cēpð him hlȳwðe.* (When he is cold, he keeps himself in a covering. \[lit. when \[it\] is cold for him\])  
* ***Mē** lyste slǣpan.* (I want to sleep \[lit. \[it\] pleases me to sleep\])
