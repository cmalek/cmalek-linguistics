There are six classes of pronouns in OE:

* [Personal pronouns](#Personal%20pronouns) stand for another noun, and are “personal” because they have different forms for the three “persons”: singular, dual and plural.  In ModE, example personal pronouns are *I, me, she, her, you, y’all, they, them,* etc.  
* [Possessive pronouns](#Possessive%20pronouns) are those which in ModE would end in *\-self*  or *\-selves*, like *myself, herself, himself, themselves.* They are called “reflexive” because they refer back to the subject of the sentence. 
* [Relative pronouns](#Relative%20pronouns) introduce an entire clause that interrupts the main clause and provide further information about a noun or pronoun in that main clause.  They are called “relative” because they relate back to or explain a noun or pronoun that appears earlier.  In ModE, the relative pronouns are *who, whom, whose, which* and *that.*  
* [Interrogative pronouns](#Interrogative%20pronouns) appear in questions.  In OE, *hwā, hwæs* and *hwǣm* as the ancestors of ModE *who, whose* and *whom.*  
* [Demonstrative pronouns](#Demonstrative%20pronouns) are so-called because they point out or *demonstrate* a particular thing.  ModE examples are *this, that, these*.  
* [Miscellaneous pronouns](#Miscellaneous%20pronouns)  There are a handful of other pronouns that don’t fall into the above categories, like *hwilċ* (which), *æġ-hwilċ* (each), *swilċ* (such \[a person or thing\])*, ælċ* (each), *æniġ* (any).  Several adjectives like *eall* (all), *sum* (one, a certain), and *ān*  (one, a single) can be used as either adjectives or pronouns.

## Personal pronouns

Personal pronouns in OE have three different numbers: singular, plural and **dual**[^1], a special number that corresponds to exactly two things or people.  Note that there are generally two forms of the accusative, one of which generally ends in *\-ċ* or *\-iċ* and another which is identical to the dative form.

The nouns to which pronouns refer are called their *antecedents*.  In general OE uses pronouns much in the same way that ModE does. 

* If the pronoun’s antecedent is an inanimate object or an animal with no discernible sex, OE generally uses *hit*, the neuter singular nominative and accusative pronoun \[p. 218, §7.1.5 \].  
  * *Sēo **bōc** cōm tō ūs and wē  ā-wendon **hit** on Englisċ.*  (The book came to us and we translated it into English.)  
* Note however that some **nouns like *wif* “woman”** **which are neuter** but refer to the physically and culturally feminine, **take their *natural* gender,** so we would use *hēo (or hīo)* \[p. 218, §7.1.5 \]  
  * *Þæt earme **wif** andswyrde and cwæð þæt **hēo** wolde tō ċyrcan gān.* (The poor woman answered and said that she wanted \[to\] go to church.\]  
* **There are still a fair number of instances where OE pronouns** (like other languages with grammatical gender) **agree in gender with their antecedent nouns**, *even when the antecedent noun itself refers to an object with no discernable gender* \[p. 218, §7.1.5\].  
  * *Englas brōhton mē āne **bōc,** ac **hēo** wæs swȳðe ġe-hwæde.* (Angels brought me a book, but it \[lit. she\[ was very slender.)  
  * *Sē **fisc** ġe-tācnað ġe-lēafan: swa **hyne** swīðor þā ȳlþa wealcað swā **hē** strenga bið.* (The fish symbolizes belief;: the more the waves toss it \[lit. him\] the stronger it is) (lit., … as him more powerfully the waves toss, so he the stronger is}  
* The **definite article (sē, sēo, þæt, etc.) can also function as a personal pronoun** \[p. 219-220, §7.1.6\]**.**  
  * *Hēo fongeþ ond hēo eteð **þā**.* (She catches fish and she eats them.)  
  * *Sē rideð forþ.* (He rides forth.)

#### Collapsed endings

When personal pronouns follow directly after a verb, the verb sometimes has a reduced set of endings **in the present tense**, particularly the first and second person plurals, so that the normal set of endings *\-aþ* and *\-iaþ* become *\-e*.

* *Myċel **habbe wē** ġe-synegod.* (Much have we sinned.)  
* *And hwæt **wille** ġe?* (And what do you want?)

\[p. 220, §7.1.7\]

### Possessive pronouns

Personal pronouns in the genitive, while they can occasionally function as proper pronouns (not signifying possession), mostly act like adjectives and modify a noun (signifying possession) \[p. 221-224, §7.2\].  

#### 

#### As a pronoun (not possessive)

\[p. 221-222, §7.2\] When functioning as a **pronoun**, there is **no implied meaning of possession**.  It’ll be one of these cases:

* It is the the implied \[of\] version of the genitive.  
* The pronoun is a direct object and the verb requires the genitive.  
* The pronoun is part of a larger whole (the partitive genitive) – modifying words like *eall*, *sum,* etc.

Examples:

* *Hīe **his** eġe habbað.* (The have fear \[of\] Him).  *his* is a genitive pronoun that is dependent on *eġe* “fear, awe”. [^2]  
* *Hīe hlystaþ **his**.*  (They listen to it.) *his* here is the neuter genitive direct object of *hylstaþ,* which is a **verb that requires a genitive object** (because it is a sensory verb)..  
* *Þæt **ēower** fela ġe-seah.* (Many of you saw that).  Here *ēower* is a genitive dependent on *fela* “many” – the **partitive genitive**.  
* *Nǣfre iċ māran ġe-seah … ðonne is **ēower** sum.* (I never saw a larger warrior than a certain one of you is.) *ēower* is dependent on *sum* “one, a certain” – the **partitive genitive** again**.**

#### As an adjective (possessive)

\[p. 222, §7.2\] When functioning as an **adjective**, the pronoun **signifies possession** of the noun modified.  It is common practice in these cases for the pronoun to follow the noun it modifies. When they are used as adjectives, the genitives of the first and second person pronouns (*mīn, þīn, ūre, ēower, uncer, incer*) and an old reflexive pronoun *sīn* (his, her, its) **take strong adjectivival endings.  All of the possessives here are considered long-stemmed.**

Examples:

* *wine **mīn*** (my friend)  
* *hæleð **mīn** sē lēofa* (my beloved hero, lit. “hero, mine the beloved”)  
* *Wolde **hire** bearn wrecan.* (\[She\] wanted to avenge her child.)  
* *Nū iċ **ēower** sceal frumcyn witan.* (Now I must know your lineage.)[^3]

* *Ġe-sette **mīnne** hyht on þeċ.* (\[I will\] set my joy on you) Note the *\-ne* on the end of *mīn* – that’s the adjectival ending for the masculine accusative singular.  
* *Þonne bēoð **ēowere**  ēagan ġe-openode.* (Then your eyes \[will\] be opened).  Note the *\-e* ending on *ēower* – that’s the feminine accusative singular adjectival ending.

#### Third person possesives

\[p. 223-224, §7.2\] The third person possessives are **not** **declined** (*his, hi(e)re, hiera, hi(e)ra, hēora*).

* *Hē ā-sette his handa ofer **hēora** ēagan.* (He set his hands over their eyes.)  
* *Hēo ā-hof **hire** ēagan upp tō heofonum.* (She lifted her eyes up to the skies.)  
* *Ne dorste **his** ēagan up ā-hebban.* (\[He\] did not dare to cast up his eyes.)

#### Datives of possession

\[p. 224, §7.3\] Possession of body parts takes the **dative**, not the genitive – the “dative of possession.”

* ***Him on bearme** læġ mādma mæniġo.* (On his lap lay many treasures, lit. Him on lap lay \[of\] treasures many.)

## Reflexive pronouns

A **reflexive pronoun** is one that in ModE would end in *\-self* or *\-selves[^4]*.  It usually appears in a sentence as the direct object, indirect object or object of the preposition and reflects back to the subject of the sentence – hence “reflexive pronoun.”  **In OE, there are no separate forms for reflexive pronouns[^5].**   Instead we just use the appropriate personal pronoun \[p. 224-225, §7.4\].  

* *Sēo sāwol hæfð on **hire** ðrēo ðing, þæt is ġe-mynd, and and-ġit, and willa.* (The soul has in itself \[lit. herself\], that is, mind, and understanding, and will)  
* *Wearp **hine** þā on wyrmes līċ.* (\[He\] transformed himself into a serpent’s likeness.)  
* *Sē cyning un-hēanliċe werede **hine**.* (The king bravely \[lit. uncowardly\], defended himself.)  
* *Þā ċēap-menn bicgdon þā gōd for **him**.* (The merchents bought the goods for themselves.)

You may also see the word “self” or *sylfe* in use with reflexive pronouns. While it appears to be a reflexive pronoun, this should be considered an **emphatic pronoun** more so than reflexive, as it is generally treated as a strong adjective (and declined as such) used to emphasise an existing noun or pronoun. So in the sentence *Iudas hine sylfne aheng  “*Judas hung himself”, *hine* is the object “himself”, not *sylfne*. *Sylfne* just emphasises the *hine* and *hine* would still mean ‘himself’ without it.  – [https://oldenglish.info/pro2.html](https://oldenglish.info/pro2.html)

### Verbs which require a reflexive pronoun

Some OE verbs require a reflexive object. Some ModE verbs function similarly (think of *to avail* – a person can only avail *themselves*, not another person or object).  For such verbs, **we often don’t translate the reflexive pronoun** \[p. 225-227, §7.4.1\].

* Verbs that describe mental or emotional states.  
* Verbs of motion also often take reflexive objects.    
* *Mǣtan* “to dream” generally takes a reflexive object.  In OE, the dream dreams you; you do not dream the dream.  
* *Ġe-wītan* “to go, depart” generally requires a reflexive object **and** an infinitive verb. 

Examples:

* *Nǣfre iċ **mē on-drǣde** dōmas þīne.*  (I never fear your judgments.) *on-drǣdan* is a verb which requires a reflexive object, and in this case *mē* is that object.  Note that we don’t translate it.  If we did it would be “I myself never fear your judgements.”  
* *Hēo **hire** hām **ġe-wāt** fram Affrica-lande.* (She went home from Africa.) *ġe-wītan* (as said above) is another verb which requires a reflexive object, and *hire* is that object.  We could more pedantically translate this sentence as “She betook herself home from Africa.”

#### Mental or emotional states

\[p. 225-226, §7.4.1\]

* *Hēo **hire on drǣdeþ** helle wītu.* (She fears hell’s torments.) Note that *hire* is not present in our translation.  You *could* transltate this as “She fears *for herself* hell’s torments.”  
* *Ða on-gān hēo **hire onemn-þrōwigan** þæs ealdan witan staðol-fæstnesse.* (Then she began to sympathise with the steadfastness of the old wise man.)  *Onemn-þrōwigan* means “to suffer along side of” so we **can’t** translate *hire* here – it wouldn’t make sense.

#### Verbs of motion

Example motion verbs: *hwearfan* “to turn, go, move about”,  *wendan* “to wend, turn”,  and *ġe-ċierran* “to turn” \[p. 226, §7.4.1\].

* ***Hwearf him** þā tō heofonum hāliġ drihten.*  (The holy Lord went to the heavens. \[lit. Turned himself then to the heavens holy lord.\])  
* *Þonne **wendeð him** Sanctus Pētrus þanon fram þǣrea helle-dura.* (Then Saint Peter turns away from the door of hell.  \[lit. Then turns himself Saint Peter away from the hell-door.\])  
* *Godes wer **him** eft **ġe-ċierde** tō his mynstre.* (God’s man went back to his monastery. \[lit. God’s man turned himself back to his monastery.\])

#### Mǣtan

*Mǣtan* “to dream” generally takes a reflexive object.  In OE, the dream dreams you; you do not dream the dream \[p. 227, §7.4.1\].

* *Iċ swefna cyst secgan wylle, hwæt **mē ġe-mǣtte** tō midre nihte.* (I want to tell the choicest of dreams, what i dreamed in the middle of the night. \[lit. I of dreams choisest to tell wish, what to me dreamed in middle night.\])

#### Ġe-wītan

The most literal way to translate *ġe-wītan* \+ reflexive pron. \+ infinitive is to render them with the rather archaic word “betake”, although it is often more modern to translate it ias “leave” or “depart”   In the latter case we do not translate the reflexive \[p. 227, §7.4.1\].

* ***Ġe-wāt him** þā sē hālga heofonas **sēċan**.* (The saint betook himself \[or departed\] to seek the heavens \[i.e. he died\]) \[lit. Betook himself then the saint heavens to seek.\]  
* ***Ġe-wāt him** on naca **drēfan** dēop wæter.* (\[He\] betook himself \[or departed\] into the boat to stir up the deep water \[i.e. to sail\])  
* ***Ġe-witon** him þā **fēran**.* (Then they left.) \[lit. \[They\] betook themselves then to go.\]

## Relative pronouns

Relative pronouns introduce an entire clause, the purpose of which is to provide further information about a noun or pronoun in the main clause. \[p. 230, §7.5\].   It is called a “relative” pronoun because it *always* relates back to a noun or pronoun that appears earlier.   The relative pronoun can tak on any function of normal nouns and pronouns within a sentence: subject, direct object, indirect object, object of preposition, and a possessive adjective.

ModE example (main clause has a grey background, the relative clause is underlined, relative pronoun in bold italic):

The sentence ***that*** you see before you has a relative clause in it

Unlike ModeE (which has *who, whom, whose, which)*, **OE has no separate system of relative pronouns.** OE signals a relative pronoun in one of four basic ways:

1. With the unchangeable word *þe* “who, which”.  This is unchangable because it *never* accepts case, number or gender markers, and is always just *þe*.   If you see *þe* alone, just translate it to the approprate ModE word by context: *who, to/for whom, which* \[p. 232-233, §7.5.1\]*.*  
   * *þæt wif **þe** wēfeð  …* (the woman who weaves …)  
   * *On-gān him winn ūp ā-hebban wið þone …, waldend, **þe** siteð on þām hālgan stōle.* (\[He\] began to raise up battle with the Lord, who sits on the holy throne.)  
   * *Is þēs … stede un-ġelic swiðe ōðrum hām **þe** wē ǣr cūðon.*  (This place is very unlike the other home which we knew before.)  
2. With a combination of a definite article \+ *þe* \[p. 233-235, §7.5.2\]  
3. With the definite article acting alone (acting as a relative pronoun) \[p. 236, §7.5.3\]  
4. In “headless” sentences, in which the relative pronoun acts as the subject of *both the main and relative clauses.* \[p. 236, §7.5.4\]

### Definite article (*sē, sēo, þæt*) \+ *þe*

This isn’t *much* different than the bare *þe* case since *þe* here acts identically to that example as the relative pronoun.  With a definite article preceding it, we should translate however as something like “he who”, “she who”, “that which”, “they who”, etc.) depending on the article. \[p. 233-237, §7.5.2\].

The problem is: what case does the article take?  Sometimes, its case comes from the relative clause itself, and sometimes from the main clause.  Don’t worry – there are patterns to this.

* When the definite article is in the nominitive, the entire relative pronoun is always the subject of the relative clause  
  * *Wæs sē grimma gǣst Grendel hāten …, **sē þe** mōras heold.*  (The grim spirit was called Grendel, he who held the moors.)  
  * *Eart þū Bēowulf, **sē þe** wið Brecan wunne*? (Are you the Beowulf, who \[lit. he who\] fought against Breca?)  
  * *Fēleþ sōna mīnes ġe-mōtes, **sēo þe** meċ nearwað, wīf wunden-locc.*  (The woman with braided hair, she who nears me, feels immediately my contact) (lit. Feels immediately my meeting, she who nears me, woman braided-locked.)  
* When the definite article is in any of the other cases, it may take its case from either the relative clause (as in ModE):  
  * *Ān æl-mihtiġ god is **þone[^6] þe** Bartolomēus bodað.*   (\[There\] is one almighty God, him who Bartholomew preaches about.)  
  * *Þæt is sēo fæhðo and sē fēondscipe, **ðæs ðe** iċ wēn hafo.*   (That is the feud and the hostility \[of\] which I have an expectaction.)[^7]  
  * Þēs brōðor would his hwītlas, ***ðām ðe*** *hē in cumena būre brūcende[^8] wæs, in sǣ wæscan*.  (This brother wanted to wash his blankets, those which he was using in the hostel \[lit. stranger’s bower\], in the sea.)  
* Or, more frequently, from the main clause.  Note that in the following examples, the article belongs to the main clause, while *þe* belongs to the relative clause.  
  * *Hē þā ġe-hyrde hēah-gnornunge **þǣre[^9] ðe** ġe-hundene bitere wǣron.* (He then heard the deep groanings *of those who* were bitterly bound.)  
  * *Mihtiġ drihten syleð eallum mēte **þām***[^10] ***þe** his eġe habbað.*  (The mighty lord gives food *to all those who* have fear of him.) (lit. Mighty lord gives \[to\] all food, \[to\] those who his fear have.\]  
  * *Sē wæs ord-fruma earmre lāfe **þǣre þe** þām hǣðenan hȳran sceolde.* (He was the leader of the wretched remnant, \[the leader\] of that \[remnant\] which had to obey the heathens.)[^11]

### As a lone definite article

When we see a lone definite article with no *þe*, it functions as “who”, “whose” or “which” in the relative clause \[p. 236, §7.5.3\].

* *Rēoteð mēowle, **sēo** hyre bearn ġe-sihð brondas þeċċan.* (The woman mourns who sees flames cover their child.) \[lit. Mourns the woman who her child sees flames to cover\]  
* *Ðonne is sum ēolond on **þǣm** bēoð men ā-cende **þāra[^12]** ēagan scīnaþ …* (Then \[there\] is a certain island on which men are born whose eyes shine …)[^13]  
* *Ðā cwōm semninga helle gæst **þone** hēo ǣr ġe-bond ond mid wītum swong.* (Then came suddenly the demon of hell, whom she previously bound and lashed with torments.)

### Headless sentences

Sometimes the pronoun from the relative clause functions as the subject of both the main and relative clauses; this can be confusing because it may look like the main clause lacks a subject.  In these cases, it’s often best to translate the relative clause first \[p. 236-237, §7.5.4\].

* *Hrēoh bið þonne **sēo þe** ǣr gladu wæs.* (She who was formerly glad will be sorrowful then.) \[lit. Sorrowful is then she who before glad was.\]  
* *Wel dēð **sē ðe** unwitigum styrð mid swinglum.* (He who motiviates the ignorant with lashes does well.) \[lit. Well does he who the ignorant motivates with lashes.\]  
* *Beald bið **sē þe** on-hyreġeð[^14] bōca cræftes.*  (Bold is he who tastes the power of books.)

## Interrogative pronouns

The pronouns *who* and *what* appear in questions and are thus called *interrogative* \[p. 242 §7.6\].  In OE, we have the following interrogative pronouns:

|     | Masculine, Feminine | Neuter    |
| :-- | :------------------ | :-------- |
| N   | hwā[^15]            | hwæt      |
| A   | hwone               | hwæt      |
| G   | hwæs                | hwæs      |
| D   | hwǣm, hwām          | hwǣm      |
| I   | hwī, hwȳ            | hwȳ, hwon |

* ***Hwā** hwylċ mann swā Drihten on-drǣde?* (Who is the man who fears the Lord?)  
* ***Hwæt** is þeōs wundrung?* (What is this wonder?)  
* ***Hwæs** wānaþ se ðe nyle gemunan?* (What does he expect that \[he\] will not remember?)  
* *Bī **hwon** scealt ðū lifgan*? (By what art must you live?)

### Indefinite usage: hwā and hwæt

*Hwā* and *hwæt* can also be used as **indefinite pronouns,** which unlike demonstratives (see below), do not indicate a particular known person or thing, but instead refer to any person or thing.  In this case, you translate them as “anyone” and “anything,” respectively \[MB82, p. 19, §§14\].  You may also see them spelled *ġehwā* and *ġehwæt* in their indefinite forms.

Sometimes, you will see them as sometimes framed by two *swā’s*, which adds the sense of “ever” to the indefinite pronoun. 

* *swā hwā swā*: whoever  
* *swā hwæt swā*: whatever

### Hwilċ/hwelc

There is another pronoun not in the table above that we can use as an interogative: ***hwilċ/hwelc*****.**   In its interogative use, translate it as “which” \[p. 243, §7.8\].  

|     | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter  | Plural  |
| :-- | :-------- | :------- | :------ | :------ |
| N   | hwilċ     | hwilċ    | hwilċ   | hwilċe  |
| A   | hwilċne   | hwilċe   | hwilċ   | hwilċe  |
| G   | hwilċes   | hwilċre  | hwilċes | hwilċra |
| D   | hwilċum   | hwilċre  | hwilċum | hwilċum |

Like *hwā* and *hwæt* above, it can also be used as a kind of **indefinite pronoun** meaning “whoever,” “whichever”, or “anyone”, “anything.”   *hwilċ* is sometimes framed by two *swā’s* in a construction which adds the sense of “ever” to it:

* *hwilċ*: anyone  
* *swā hwilċ swā*: whoever, whatever person or thing


## Demonstrative pronouns

Like the definite article, the demonstrative pronoun *this* is also declined according to case, number and gender.  These are called a “demonstrative” because they point out or **demonstrate** a particular thing (*this, these*).  Demonstrative pronouns can function both as pronouns and adjectives, in which case it agrees in gender, number and case with the noun it modifies \[p. 242 §7.7\].

|  | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter | Plural |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| N | þēs | þēos | þis | þās |
| A | þisne | þās | þis | þās |
| G | þisses | þisse, þeosse | þisses | þissa, þisra |
| D | þissum | þisse | þissum | þissum |
| I | þȳs | \*\*\* | þȳs | \*\*\* |

* *Hwæt is **þeōs** wundrung?* (What is this wonder?)  
* *In **ðeosse** abbudissan mynstre wæs sum brōðor syndriġlīċe mid godcundre ġife ġemǣred ond ġeweorðad …* (In this abbess’s monastery, a certain brother was specially glorified and honored with a divine gift ...)


## Miscellaneous pronouns 

There are also a number of miscellaneous pronouns for various uses.  Most of these pronouns require a genitive “object” when they appear as pronouns \[p. 244 §7.9\].  As pronouns, it seems that these are all declined by case, number and gender, similarly to *hwilċ*.  Pronouns with the a superscript † are indeclinable.
