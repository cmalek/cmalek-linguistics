# Inflection and conjugation

In ModE, a noun tells us its role in the sentence almost always by word order (syntax) – Modern English is a primarily a [**syntactical language**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntax) and has a proscribed order that words must follow in order for the sentence to make sense.   Subjects (S) come first, followed by verbs (V) then possibly an indirect object (IO) and possibly the direct object (O).   Independent clauses follow the same structure, and dependent clauses follow at least some of this.

* **S \+ V \+ O**: Sharks eat fish.  
* **S \+ V \+ IO \+ O:** Father gave us treats. (**Father**: S, **gave:** V, **us:** IO, **treats**: O)  
* **S \+ V:** I ate.

Old English is weakly syntactical (word order matters somewhat, but is flexible) but is very [**synthetic**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_language): words have roots that we change in order to convey meaning and role in the sentence.  

The synthesis that happens in nouns in OE is called ***inflection*****,** meaning have their endings changed and possibly internal vowels of the noun stem,  to reflect their grammatical role in the sentence and their number.  The change in ending is specifically called ***conjugation***.

In the OE system, nouns in a sentence have four characteristics:

* **Declension**: what kind of noun is it?   
* **Gender**: masculine, feminine and neuter – this affects declension and inflection.  
* **Number**: how many of this thing are we talking about?  
* **Case**: what role does this noun have in the sentence (subject, object, indirect object, possessive), along with some other uses

# Articles

Nouns also may or may not have an **article** attached to them (always before the noun).   We’re going to talk about articles here because in OE, each gender, number and case combination has its own specific article, so knowing the articles is a shortcut to decoding the case, number and gender of the noun[^1].

## Definite articles

The definite article is just the various forms of the word “the” in OE, varying by number, gender and case.  We will discuss the specific cases of OE  below, but we will name them here.

| Singular | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter | Plural |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Nominitive (N) | sē | sēo | þæt | þā |
| Accusative (A) | þone | þā | þæt | þā |
| Genitive (G) | þæs | þǣre | þæs | þāra, þǣra |
| Dative (D) | þǣm, þām | þǣre | þǣm, þām | þǣm, þām |
| Instrumental (I) | þȳ, þon | \*\*\* | þȳ, þon | \*\*\* |

* Note that the genitive and dative masculine and neuter forms are the same, and the dative plural is the same as the masculine and neuter singular.    
* ***þā*** is especially problematic, since it is feminine accusative and nominitive singular, and accusative plural, **and** is also a separate adverb which means “then” or “when”.  
* The singular definite article in the nomitive case can also be used as a **personal pronoun**: *sē \= he, sēo \= she, þæt \= it*

## Indefinite articles

OE has no consistent indefinite article like *a* or *an* in ModE.   When decoding a sentence, you might need to supply an “a” or an “an” if there is no definite article and the context seems to require it.

* *Þæt is miċel hūs.*  (That is \[a\] large house.)

Just because there are no *consistent* indefinite articles in OE, that does not mean that there are *no* indefinite articles.  Here are two you may encounter:

* *ān* (one, a certain)  
* *sum* (one, a certain)

Both words when used as articles get conjugated as strong adjectives; see the [Adjectives](https://docs.google.com/document/u/1/d/1FDbcDwng8lK7DRlmAf7B-7Zog7hQ2qjO8JsaRFZHclQ/edit) guide.

We still do a tiny bit of inflection in ModE when we make a noun plural by adding *\-s* or *\-es* to it, and some holdovers from Old English, Middle English or Old Norse, Greek, etc.  are inflected in their own way:

* ox (singular) \-\> ox**en**  
* child \-\> childr**en**  
* man \-\> m**en**  
* woman \-\> wom**en**  
* person \-\> pe**ople**  
* foot \-\> **feet**[^2]

… and so on.  There are not many of such nouns in ModE.  Not so in OE\!

When decoding a sentence, it is very important to know what the inflections of the nouns tell us about their role and number so that we can piece together the ModE translation.

## Instrumental

The instrumental is the case we use when a noun modifies a verb, and we usually prefix it with “by means of” or “with.

The instrumental is **only for masculine singular nouns**, so if you see something like “fāgum swyrde”, you can translate that as “adorned sword”, but if you see a bare “fāgum swyrdum” (Judith, line 301b), this must be an indirect object, and must be dative instead of instrumental.
