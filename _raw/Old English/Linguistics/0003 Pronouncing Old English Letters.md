This lesson is a practical reading aid, not a claim that we can recover one perfectly exact Old English accent. The goal is a safe, usable pronunciation system you can carry into texts.

**Win for today:** by the end, you should be able to look at an Old English spelling and make a good first-pass pronunciation decision for the vowels, the voiced-versus-voiceless fricatives, and the tricky letters `c`, `g`, `h`, `sc`, and `cg`.

## 1. Two Cautions First

**First:** older grammars often describe sounds by comparison with Modern German, French, or older English pronunciation rather than with strict IPA. That is normal.

**Second:** some values are safer than others. For example, `f` as `[f]` or `[v]` by environment is secure. The exact phonetic value of palatal `c` is less secure, so good teaching texts sometimes differ.

## 2. Vowels

The simplest way to handle the vowels is to keep two layers in view at once: a **traditional classroom cue** like “as in `bet`” or “as in `hate`,” and a **rough IPA approximation**. The classroom cue is often what grammars actually teach; the IPA is a modern convenience layered on top of that.

| Letter | Traditional classroom cue                                                       | Rough IPA                                    | Audio cue | Practical note                                                              |
| ------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | --------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `æ`    | like the vowel in English `hat`                                                 | `[æ]`                                        | [æ]       | Short front open vowel.                                                     |
| `a`    | like the vowel in English `father`, but short                                   | `[ɑ]`                                        | [ɑ]       | A back `a`-sound.                                                           |
| `ǣ`    | older grammars often compare it to the vowel of English `air` or French `père`  | `[æː]` or a somewhat higher long front vowel | [æ]       | Long counterpart of `æ`, though some descriptions place it slightly higher. |
| `ā`    | like the vowel in English `father`                                              | `[ɑː]`                                       | [ɑ]       | Long `a`.                                                                   |
| `e`    | like the vowel in English `bet`                                                 | `[ɛ]`                                        | [e]       | Short open-mid front vowel.                                                 |
| `ē`    | like the vowel in English `take`, or German `See`, without a strong final glide | `[eː]`                                       | [e]       | Long close-mid front vowel.                                                 |
| `i`    | like the vowel in English `tin` or `bit`                                        | `[ɪ]`                                        | [i]       | Short high front vowel.                                                     |
| `ī`    | like the vowel in English `seen` or `eel`                                       | `[iː]`                                       | [i]       | Long high front vowel.                                                      |
| `o`    | like the vowel in English `cough` or `not`                                      | `[ɔ]` or `[o]`                               | [u]       | Short back rounded vowel; exact openness varies by teaching tradition.      |
| `ō`    | like the vowel in English `goad` or German `so`, without a strong final glide   | `[oː]`                                       | [u]       | Long close-mid back rounded vowel.                                          |
| `u`    | like the vowel in English `put` or the `o` of `wolf`                            | `[ʊ]`                                        | [u]       | Short high back rounded vowel.                                              |
| `ū`    | like the vowel in English `cool`                                                | `[uː]`                                       | [u]       | Long high back rounded vowel.                                               |
| `y`    | like German `ü` or French `tu`                                                  | `[y]`                                        | [y]       | A front rounded vowel, not consonantal `[j]`.                               |
| `ȳ`    | like a long German `ü`, as in `grün`                                            | `[yː]`                                       | [y]       | Long front rounded vowel.                                                   |

**Why there can seem to be disagreement:** many Old English grammars teach pronunciation through comparison words like `bet`, `hate`, `put`, and `cool`, while modern readers often try to convert those directly into a single exact IPA symbol. That conversion is useful, but it is still an approximation layered on top of the older classroom descriptions.

## 3. Voicing Rules for Fricatives

This is one of the most useful pronunciation rules in Old English reading. Several fricatives change from voiceless to voiced when they stand between voiced sounds.

|Letter|Voiceless value|Voiced value|Safe rule|
|---|---|---|---|
|`f`|`[f]`|`[v]`|Use `[f]` initially, finally, before voiceless consonants, and when doubled; use `[v]` between voiced sounds.|
|`s`|`[s]`|`[z]`|Use `[s]` in most positions; use `[z]` between voiced sounds.|
|`þ`, `ð`|[θ]|[ð]|Use `[θ]` initially, finally, doubled, and next to voiceless sounds; use `[ð]` between voiced sounds.|

**Important spelling note:** OE scribes did _not_ consistently reserve `þ` for `[θ]` and `ð` for `[ð]`. The letters overlap in manuscripts. The environment usually tells you more than the symbol itself.

## 4. The Tricky Consonants

|Letter|Best beginner value|When|Examples / note|
|---|---|---|---|
|`b`|`[b]`|always|A straightforward stop.|
|`d`|`[d]`|always|A straightforward stop.|
|`l`|`[l]`|always|Keep it clear, not dark or vocalized.|
|`m`|`[m]`|always|Ordinary `m`.|
|`n`|`[n]`, or [ŋ] before velars|before `g`/`c`/`k`|Before velars it becomes the sound of English `ng`.|
|`p`|`[p]`|always|A straightforward stop.|
|`t`|`[t]`|always|A straightforward stop.|
|`w`|`[w]`|always|Ordinary `w`.|
|`r`|[ɾ] or lightly trilled `[r]`|always|Wright and the Texas materials both allow a rolled or tapped value; a light tap is a good classroom compromise.|

## 4a. `c`

|Value|Environment|Examples|
|---|---|---|
|[k]|Before another consonant, or next to back-vowel environments|`cnēo`, `cwide`, `saca`, `bōc`|
|[tʃ] as a learner-safe value|In palatal environments, especially before front vowels|`cild`, `ciese`, `benc`|

**Why I am not writing one exact IPA symbol for palatal `c`:** older grammars do not all agree on the narrow value. Wright describes it more conservatively, while many teaching texts use the modern learner-friendly [tʃ]-value. For reading practice, [tʃ] is the safest classroom choice.

## 4b. `g`

|Value|Environment|Examples|
|---|---|---|
|[g]|Initial before back vowels, before consonants, after `[ŋ]`, and when doubled|`gōd`, `gold`, `hogg`, `lang`|
|[ɣ]|Medially between back vowels, after `l`/`r` before back vowels, and often finally after back vowels|`dagas`, `burga`, `boga`, `ġenōg`|
|[j]|In palatal environments, especially initially before front vowels|`giefan`, `geoc`, `giest`|

**What I corrected from your draft:** initial non-palatal `g` is not usually taught as `[ɢ]`. The normal beginner value is plain `[g]`. Intervocalic back-vowel `g` is better treated as `[ɣ]`; in some positions it may devoice toward `[x]`, but `[ɣ]` is the safer base value.

## 4c. `h`

|Value|Environment|Examples|
|---|---|---|
|[h]|Initially before a vowel or consonant|`habban`, `hlūd`, `hring`|
|[ç]|After front vowels in non-initial position|`riht`, `siehþ`|
|[x]|After back vowels or consonants in non-initial position|`hēah`, `sōhte`, `beorht`, `meaht`|

Your instinct about OE `h` being “guttural” away from initial position is right. The main cleanup is just to separate the front-vowel value `[ç]` from the back-vowel value `[x]`.

## 4d. Letter Combinations

|Combination|Best beginner value|Example|
|---|---|---|
|`sc`|[ʃ]|`scip`, `sceal`|
|`cg`|[dʒ]|`ecg`, `brycg`|

## 5. A Mini Reading Routine

**Step 1:** identify long vowels from macrons in teaching editions.

**Step 2:** check whether `f`, `s`, or `þ/ð` sits between voiced sounds.

**Step 3:** ask whether `c`, `g`, or `h` is in a front-vowel or back-vowel environment.

**Step 4:** treat `sc` and `cg` as units, not as two separate letters.
