A quick-reference map for the changes and processes that show up most often in beginner Old English historical grammar.

## Use This Order First

**1. Inheritance:** Start from Proto-Indo-European.

**2. Grimm's Law:** Major PIE stop consonants shift into the characteristic Proto-Germanic system.

**3. Verner's Law:** Some of Grimm's new fricatives become voiced when the older PIE accent was not on the preceding syllable.

**4. Proto-Germanic:** Shared Germanic stage after those big common innovations.

**5. Branch-specific vowel changes:** i-mutation, a/o-mutation, breaking, raising, and similar changes happen later inside individual daughter languages or subgroups.

**6. Prosodic and local phonological changes:** syncope, assimilation, gemination, and reduction reshape forms further.

**7. Analogy and leveling:** Paradigms get regularized, which can hide older sound changes.

## What Each Name Usually Means

|Term|What it is|Why it matters|
|---|---|---|
|Grimm's Law|A chain shift of PIE stops into Proto-Germanic stops and fricatives.|Explains why English `f` often matches Latin or Greek `p`, and why Germanic looks consonantally distinctive.|
|Verner's Law|A correction to Grimm's Law conditioned by older PIE accent placement.|Explains many apparently irregular alternations such as `f` versus `b` or `þ` versus `d` in related forms.|
|i-mutation / i-umlaut|A vowel-fronting or raising effect triggered by `i` or `j` in the next syllable, often in an older form whose second syllable later disappeared. Here `j` is the glide [j], and `i` is the high front vowel [i].|Very important for Old English plurals and derivation: `mann ~ menn`, `fōt ~ fēt`.|
|a-mutation|A vowel change caused by following `a` in some Germanic languages.|Useful for understanding why expected front vowels may retreat or lower in certain environments.|
|o-mutation|A vowel change caused by following `o` or `u`.|Important in Old Norse and some other branches; helps explain divergent daughter-language vowels.|
|Breaking|A vowel becomes a diphthong or more complex vowel near certain consonants.|Very common in Old English histories of forms and spellings.|
|Palatalization|A consonant is pulled forward toward the palate, often near front vowels or `j`.|Important for Old English `c`/`g` histories and for understanding dotted `ċ`/`ġ` in modern teaching.|
|Syncope|Loss of an unstressed vowel, often in medial syllables.|Helps explain shorter later forms and alternations inside paradigms.|
|Assimilation|One sound becomes more like a nearby sound.|Explains many smaller local shape changes that are regular but less famous than Grimm's Law.|
|Gemination|A consonant is lengthened, often after a triggering segment such as `j`.|Important in Old English phonological history and spelling patterns.|
|Analogy|Paradigms get remodeled by pattern pressure rather than direct sound law.|Explains why sound change alone is not enough.|

## Two Structural Facts Behind Grimm and Verner

|Fact|Short version|Why it matters|
|---|---|---|
|Major PIE stop classes|Traditional presentations group them as voiceless stops, voiced stops, and voiced aspirated stops.|Grimm's Law is easiest to understand as a reshuffling of these whole classes.|
|Older PIE accent|PIE had an older mobile accent system, not later fixed Germanic first-syllable stress.|Verner's Law depends on that older accent pattern.|

## Why This Order Is Reconstructed

1. Grimm must be earlier than Verner because Verner voices fricatives created by Grimm.
2. The older mobile accent must still be relevant when Verner applies.
3. Fixed Germanic stress and later mutations like i-mutation belong to later stages.

## Two Beginner Rules

1. Consonant laws like Grimm and Verner are usually earlier and broader.
2. Mutation rules like i-mutation are usually later and more local to particular branches.

## Quick OE Palatalization Reminder

|OE spelling area|Safe beginner reminder|
|---|---|
|`c`|Can stay velar, but in palatal environments it can move toward a `ch`-like value.|
|`g`|Can stay velar, but in palatal environments it can move toward a `y`-like value.|
|Modern teaching notation|Dotted `ċ` and `ġ` are a modern aid, not a fully native manuscript norm.|
