# Prepositions

Prepositions (words usually indicating a position in time or space like in, under, over, after, before, to, out, etc.) demand an object, either a noun or a pronoun.  As the name “pre-position” implies, prepositions normally appear before their objects.  Since the nounds or pronouns are “governed” by the preposition, they are called objects of that preposition.  In OE, prepositions require their objects to be in a particular case.

\[p. 43, §2.11, pp. 198-206, §6.15\]

This guide to Old English grammar gives rules for when to apply what case to the object of a preposition, as well as a glossary of all the Old English prepositions I could find with definitions, case of its object, example phrases/sentences and the explanation of why the preposition takes that case for that example.

# Post-positions

Especially in poetry, prepositions can sometimes appear *after* their objects, in some cases separated from them by several words, so be vigilant\!

\[p. 200, §6.16.1\]

Examples:

* ***Scedelandum** in* (in Sweden)  
* *Ða cōm **mē** tō, mīn sunu Petrus.*  (Then came to me my son Peter.)  
* *Þæt wif **him** cwæð tō.* (lit. The woman him spoke to.)

# Governed Cases

Preopositions take objects (“the object of the preposition”), and each preposition requires a certain case for its object.   This is called the “governed case”.  Below we break down the cases you will see, with an idea of when they should be used.

## Accusative

Certain prepositions always require objects in the accusative case — these will be labeled in the glossary with “acc” in .  It’s impossible to tell by looking at a preposition which case its object requires.  You must simply look it up.  

\[p. 43, 2.11.2.b\]

Accusative prepositional objects are used (generally) when the phrase expresses:

* *motion toward a goal or from one place to another*    
  * Motion across a boundary is a goal oriented action, so even though we normally think genitive for boundary relations, a motion verb indicates we should use the accusative.  
* *direction, motion in space, expresses a limit or extent in space (does this mean an area?)*  
* *duration of time, expresses a limit or extent in time*


## Dative

Dative prepositional objects are used when the phrase expresses *where* or *with what.*

* *where something is (*locative dative) — **static placement**  
  * *when something occurs in time (*temporal dative) — **static point in time**  
    * *the means — the noun assisted in the action?*  
      * *where something is relative to another thing –* **relative location**  
      * or when something *comes from* or is *taken away from* something else


## Genitive

Genitive prepositional objects is used when the phrase expresses:

* Being in relation to a whole  
  * partition/separation, specifically when one is referring to something being related to the **boundary of a whole from the outside of the situation;**  there is a “boundary relation”.   The key part is the genitive describes the state of being within  
    * exclusion: marking **what is left out** or **excluded from the whole.**   It’s still related to boundaries, o being outside a house means you are excluded from the boundary of the house and thus we use the genitive here too  
      * cause/reference: marks what something is **in reference to** or **by reason of**  
      * temporal (within limits of time): marks a **time span as a whole**, within which the event takes place.

## Instrumental

Generally, no Old English preposition governs the instrumental as a distinct case.  Instrumental *sense* is preserved but expressed through dative forms (especially with **mid** or with bare nouns).  But, in \[p. 202-203, §6.16.3\], we see that “prepositions that take the dative often substitute the instrumental with no change in meaning.

Sometimes *mid* takes the instrumental, but the only way to know is if the article is *þy/þon* or the demonstrative is *þys* .  Those words aren't always present, though\!

* *mid þām folce* (with, among the people)  
* *mid þȳ folce* (with, among the people)


  

## Multiple cases

Certain prepositions (**under, on, in**) can take both dative and accusative objects.  The accusative signals some sort of “motion toward” (into, onto up to, up under, across to, etc.) and the dative signals a set state of being or stasis (on, in, under, over, etc).

\[p. 201, §6.16.2\]

When there is a choice of cases for a preposition (as with *bufan, būtan, in, innan, ofer, etc.*),  to determine which to use when writing, or why a particular case was used when reading, we should look at the **verb** in the clause..  

* If the verb is a **rest** verb (does not indicate motion from one place to another (an action can still be happening within the confines of the space)), e.g. *sittan* (to sit), use the **dative**  
* when it is a **motion** verb (*does* indicate motion from one place to another), e.g. *gān* (to go), use the **accusative**.

**innan** is a special case that can take the accusative, dative OR genitive — see the Glossary.
