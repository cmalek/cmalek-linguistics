The minimum vocabulary you need to read historical-linguistics explanations without getting buried.

|Term|Plain meaning|
|---|---|
|Reconstructed form|A form not directly attested in writing, inferred from evidence. It is written with an asterisk, like `*fōtiz`.|
|Proto-language|The common ancestor of a language family or subgroup, such as Proto-Germanic.|
|Reflex|A later form descended from an earlier one. Old English `fōt` is a reflex of an earlier Germanic form, and Modern English `foot` is a reflex of Old English `fōt`.|
|Cognate|A related word inherited from a shared ancestor, not just a similar-looking word.|
|Sound correspondence|A recurring match across related languages, such as English `f` corresponding to Latin `p`.|
|Comparative method|The main method for reconstructing unattested ancestors by comparing related languages and finding regular correspondences.|
|Internal reconstruction|Using irregularities inside one language to infer an earlier stage of that same language.|
|Ablaut|An inherited vowel alternation inside the same word-family or paradigm. It is **not** caused by a neighboring sound in the current form. Good intuition examples are PIE `*ped-` beside `*pod-`, and English strong-verb patterns like `sing / sang / sung`.|
|Umlaut / mutation|A later sound change where one vowel is altered because of a vowel or glide in the next syllable. In Old English, a classic example is `fōt` beside plural `fēt`, where the later `i`-sound in the ending triggered the fronted vowel.|
|Ablaut grade|One member of an inherited vowel-alternation pattern, such as `e`-grade, `o`-grade, or zero-grade. So `o-grade vocalism` means the form uses the `o`-vowel version of that older pattern. A classic example is the "foot" family: compare PIE `*ped-`-type `e`-grade, `*pod-`-type `o`-grade, and zero-grade forms where the root vowel disappears. In later Indo-European languages you keep meeting these inherited alternations in paradigms and word-families rather than as brand-new sound changes.|
|Analogy|Reshaping by pattern pressure, often making a form more regular or extending an existing pattern. A modern English example is `bring / brang / brung` modeled on `sing / sang / sung`.|
|Leveling|A kind of analogy where differences inside a paradigm are reduced so the forms become more alike. A simple intuition is when an older pattern with alternations gets smoothed out because speakers prefer one form to spread across the set.|
|Sound law|A regular sound change applying across a class of forms under stated conditions.|

**Quick ablaut intuition:** if `e-grade` is the version with `e`, and `o-grade` is the version with `o`, then `zero-grade` is the version where that vowel disappears. A very rough schematic example is `*bher-` (e-grade), `*bhor-` (o-grade), and `*bhr-` (zero-grade). In old verb systems, English still gives you a faint feel for this kind of inherited alternation in patterns like `sing / sang / sung`, even though the full PIE system is older and more complex.

**Quick contrast:** **ablaut** is an _old inherited pattern_ inside a family of forms, while **umlaut** is a _later triggered sound change_ caused by a nearby vowel or glide. A compact Old English way to remember this is: `sing / sang / sung` feels like ablaut, but `fōt / fēt` is the classic umlaut example.

**Quick leveling intuition:** suppose older sound history leaves a verb with alternating consonants in different forms. If later speakers reduce that variation and let one pattern spread, that is **leveling**. A modern English-style comparison is when speakers regularize an irregular set, as with `dive / dived` instead of older competing patterns like `dove`.
