Like nouns, OE adjectives have case, gender, and number endings which agree with the case, gender, and number markers of the nouns they modify. \[...\] Luckily, though, most of the adjective endings mirror those you’ve already learned, so the process of memorization should be *much* easier.

\[p.168, §6.1\]

# Types of adjectives

Like nouns, there are **strong** and **weak** forms of adjectives.  Unlike nouns, the same adjective can be either strong or weak depending on context.

* If an adjective is preceded by a definite article (*the*), a demonstrative pronoun (*this, that*) or a posessive (*his, hers, your,* etc.), it takes the **weak** form (because the preceding word carries the “grammatical weight”, allowing the adjective itself to carry less “load”).  
* **Strong** forms are used when they carry the grammatical weight themselves – they are not preceded by an article, demonstrative or possessive, or if they appear by themselves after a *to be* verb (*the gift is good*).

\[pp. 170-171, §6.2\]

# Strong adjectives

\[pp. 168-170, §6.1\]

Like feminine and neuter weak nouns, **strong adjectives** come in two flavors: short-stemmed and long-stemmed.  Determining which is which is, like for nouns, determined by the last vowel \+ consonant combination in the *stem* of the word.   Remember that a *dipthong* (two vowels commonly found together and pronounced as a single sound) counts as a single vowel in this case

* **Short-stemmed** adjectives: a short vowel followed by a single consonant  
* **Long-stemmed** adjectives:  
  * a long vowel \+ one consonant   
  * a short vowel \+ 2 or more consonants

## Adjectives ending in \-u

\[p. 170, §6.1.3\]

Some adjectives have a final *\-u* or *\-o* that is part of the stem.  In thse cases, the *\-u* changes to a *\-o* before consonants but to a *\-w-* before vowels.

Example: *ġearu “ready, prepared”*

* Singular masculine: \[N\] *ġearu, \-**o**,* \[A\] *ġear**o**ne,* \[G\] *ġear**w**es,*  \[D\] *ġear**w**um,* \[I\] *ġear**w**e*  
* Singular feminine: *ġearu **\-o**, ġear**w**e, ġear**o**re, ġear**o**re, \-*  
* Singular neuter: *ġearu **\-o**, ġearu, ġear**w**es, ġear**w**um,  ġear**w**e*  
* Plural masculine: \[N\] *ġear**w**e,* \[A\] *ġear**w**e,* \[G\] *ġear**o**ra,*  \[D\] *ġear**w**um*  
* Plural feminine: *ġear**w**e, \-a,*  *ġear**w**e, \-a, ġear**o**ra,*  *ġear**w**um*  
* Plural neuter: *ġearu,*  *ġearu, ġear**o**ra,*  *ġear**w**um*

# Weak adjectives

\[p. 171, §6.2.1\]

Note that weak adjectives correspond almost exactly to the **weak noun endings**.  Weak adjectives are called **weak** becuase some other word *before* the adjective carry some of the *grammatical weight*.   This will typically be a demonstrative pronoun or article like so:

* *sē æl-mihtiga dryhten*: because of the *sē*, the adjective *æl-mihtiġ* becomes weak, since *sē* takes some of the grammatical weight

# Example phrases

\[p. 171-172, §6.2.2\]

* **weak:**  *sēo gōde eorðe*  \[**the** good earth\]  
* **strong**: *gōd eorðe* \[(a) good earth, note the lack of any article\]  
* **weak:** *tō þām gōdum mannum* \[to **the** good men\]  
* **strong**: *tō gōdum mannum* \[to good men, no article\]  
* **weak**: *Sē leornað āa gōdan flāre.* \[He learns the good teaching\]  
* **strong**: *miċelu weorc* \[many works\]  
* **strong:** *Þæt wæs weorc gōd.* \[That was good work\]  
* **strong:** *Iċ hǣbbe mihte miċel.* \[I have great power\]  
* **weak**: *waldend þone gōdan* \[the good ruler – lit. ruler the good\]

### More complex sentences

* **strong/strong:** *Ġif  þāno* *scēap bēoð* ***gōd*** *þonne bēoð* ***gōd*** *hydras.* \[If the sheep are good (ones) then the shepherds are good (ones)\]; nominitive because of *to be* – subject complement  
* **weak/weak:** *And*  *of þām* **gōdum*** *scēapum* *cumað þā* *gōdan* *hydras.*  \[And from the good sheep come good shepherds\]; *of* takes the dative; these are weak adjectives because of the *þām* and *þā*  articles

# Adjectives as nouns

\[p. 173, §6.2.3\]

Sometimes, adjectives function as independently as nouns.  In these cases, we need to add a noun like *one, man, woman,* or *person* to your translation.  When an adjective functions as a noun we call it the *substantive*.

Examples:

* **weak:** *Sēo* *hāliġe* *spræc* *ofer* *hēanne* *weall.*  \[The holy (woman) spoke over the high wall\] weak because of *Sēo.*  \[This is from the story of Judith\!\]  
* **weak:** *Hē* *for-lēt* *þone* *gōdan.* \[He abandoned the good (man)\], weak because of *þone*  
* **strong:** *Ġe-seh* *þā …* *hāliġ* *hāliġne.* \[Then the holy man saw the holy man, strong because no articles, etc.\]  
* **strong:** *Þonne bēoð þȳ hefigran heortan benne, sāre æfter swǣsne.* \[Then are the heart’s wounds the heavier, pains for the dear (one)\]

# Comparative and superlative forms

\[p. 182-, §6.4\]

OE adjectives can be intensified by adding comparative (*\-er*) and superlative (*\-est*) endings: *fast, faster, fastest.* In ModE, most multi-syllabic adjectives take *more* or *most* instead of those endings.  OE has no such distinction: all adjectives take the *\-ra[^4]* and \-*ost* endings.

These intensified adjectives often have **two** endings: 

* Comparative: (*\-ra-*) always take the **weak** adjectival ending even if there is no preceeding article, demonstrative or possessive.  
* Superlative: (*\-ost-*) take the regular strong or weak adjectival endings like normal adjectives.

## Comparatives

### Comparative \+ personal ending

\[pp. 183-184, §6.5\]

These intensified adjectives often have **two** endings: 

Comparatives (*\-ra-*) always take the **weak** adjectival ending even if there is no preceeding article, demonstrative or possessive.

For comparatives particularly, sometimes this can be tricky.  The only way to recognize the difference is to know what the adjective is modifying, and look whether we have only the appropriate strong/weak ending or whether there is something **extra** there \[context\].

It’s very important to be able to recognize the difference between regular adjectival endings and comparative endings, but you’ll typically only be able to do that if you know the adjectival endings by heart (or by reading the entire context).

Examples:

* pronn*Þæt*nom1 n:weakn*wæter*nom1 v:i*wæs*3ipa adjweak***biterre***compnom1 con*ond* adjweak***grimre***compnom1 prep*tō*dat v:weak1*drincanne*inflinf con*þonne* pron*ǣnig ōðer.*  \[The water was bitterer and more horrible \[lit. grimmer\] to drink than any other.\]  Here, the adjectives *biterre*  and  *grimre* are comparative (base forms are *biter* and *grim*); for *biterre*, the second *r* indicates the comparative (the first *r* is simply part of the stem *biter*) while the *\-e* is the weak neuter nominitive ending.  
* pronn*Wē*nompl v:weak?*libbað*3ipr prep*on*dat adjstrong***biterre***dat1 *ond* adjstrong***grimre***dat1 n:weakf/n*tīde*dat1*.* \[We live in a bitter and horrible time\].  Note that here the *\-re* ending on *biterre and grimre* is simply the strong dative singular adjectival ending.

### *Than* marker

**Comparative** adjectives often require a “than” prhase to complete them (which makes sense – better than what?).  In OE, there are two ways to do this, a familiar way (1) and a more confusing way (2).

1. artf*Hēo*nom1 v:i*is*3ipr adjweak*swiftre*compnom1 adv***þonne*** pron:pers*iċ*acc1*.* \[She is swifter than I (am).\]  
2. pron:pers*Mē*dat1 v:i*is*3ipr artf*hēo*nom1 adjweak*swiftre*compnom1*.* \[(Than\] I she is faster.\]  In this method, the dative or instrumental takes the place of *than*.

### Comparative as intensifier

In some instances, the **comparative** may simply intensify an adjective rather than make a true comparison.  This is *reasonably* rare.    You can tell this is the case like so:

If you see a comparative with no real or implied *þonne* prhase or its dative/instrumental equivalent, you can suspect that this is merely an intensification and add “very” to the positive[^5] form of the adjective in your translation.

## Superlatives

Again, comparatives and superlatives often have **two** endings:

Superlatives (*\-ost-*) take the regular strong or weak adjectival endings like normal adjectives.

Remember that superlative adjectives have an *\-ost-* in them before the ending for the number and case of the modified noun.  They are a lot easier to deal with than the comparative ending *\-ra*.

Examples:

* pronm*Hē*1 v*hæfde*3ipr *ġyt* adj?*ǣnne*acc1 adjstrong***lēofostne***superacc1 nm:wa-stem*sunu*acc1*.* \[He had still one most beloved son.\] *\-ost-* here is the superlative ending, while *\-ne* is the strong accusative singular ending.  
* pron:int*Hwā* v:pretpres*mihte*3ipr pron:possm*his* adjweak***lēofostan***superacc1 *nmfrēond*acc1 v*for-ġietan*inf*?* \[Who could forget his dearest friend?\] Here, again *\-ost-* designtes *lēofostan* as a superlative, with the *\-an* ending signifying the masculine singular accusative.

# Irregular adjectives with different roots

Some adjectives show an internal vowel change in the comparative and superlative forms and take *\-est* rather than *\-ost* in the superlative.

Often this is because of an *i-mutation*, meaning that at one time, those adjectives must have had an \[i\] in them which has since disappeared and this \[i\] had the power to change the root vowel.

| Positive | Comparative | Superlative |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| eald “old” | ieldra | ieldest |
| ġeong “young” | ġingra | ġingest |
| lang “long” | lengra | lengest |
| strang “strong” | strengra | strengest |
| hēah “high” | hīerra | hīehest |

Then there are some simply purely irregular forms, that you just have to memorize (like the verb *wesan*).

| Positive | Comparative | Superlative |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| lȳtel “little, small” | lǣssa | lǣst |
| miċel “much; great” | māra | mǣst |
| yfel “bad, evil” | wiersa | wierst |
| gōd, “good” | betera, sēlra | betst sēlest |
