A quick-reference sheet for spotting common West Saxon, Anglian, and Kentish writing differences without having to rebuild the whole phonology from scratch each time.

## First Principle

Start from **early West Saxon** as the classroom baseline, then notice repeated non-WS spellings. Mercian and Northumbrian are often grouped together as **Anglian**, so it is often enough to recognize "Anglian-like" before trying to decide which one.

|`sceal`|`scæl`|Anglian|
|`meaht`|`mæht`|Anglian|
|`weax`|`wæx`|Anglian|
|`dæg`|`deg`|Kentish|
|`fæder`|`feder`|Kentish|
|`wæter`|`weter`|Kentish|
|`ceaster`|`cester`|Kentish|
|`geat`|`get`|Kentish|
|`sceal`|`scel`|Kentish|
|`wudu`|`wiodu`|Kentish|

|`hafoc`|`heafuc`|Mercian|
|`faran`|`fearan`|Mercian|
|`earm`|`eorm`|Northumbrian|
|`heard`|`heord`|Northumbrian|
|`ēar`|`æhher`|Northumbrian|
|`tēar`|`tæhher`|Northumbrian|

## Mini Rules

|If you see...|Think...|
|---|---|
|`ea → æ`|Often Anglian|
|`æ / ea → e`|Often Kentish|
|WS `ie / y`-type spellings reduced to `e`|Often non-WS|
|`wio-` where WS has `wu-`|Kentish|
|Odd-looking umlauted forms like `featu`|Mercian|
|Unusual Northumbrian-looking spellings like `eorm`, `æhher`|Northumbrian|

## Best Caution

Copied manuscripts mix forms. A single spelling can be suggestive, but a **cluster** of spellings is much better evidence.
