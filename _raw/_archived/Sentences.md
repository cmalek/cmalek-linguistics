# Translating OE Sentences

\[...\] It may seem logical to start translating by looking up the first word in the sentence and working your way sequentially through every word.  In fact, especially for beginners, this approach will make your life more difficult than it needs to be.

\[p. 116, Ch 5 introduction\]

## The translation algorithm

1. Locate clauses  
2. Identify the [verb](Verbs.md)  
3. Identify the [subject](Nouns%20and%20Articles.md)
4. Fill in the other elements  
5. Repeat.

## Sequence of tenses and moods

If we start in the past, in OE we typically remain in the past for the rest of the sentence (See [Tenses](Verbs.md#Tense)).  If we transition to the subjunctive mood, we typically remain in the subjunctive for the rest of the sentence (See [Mood](Verbs.md#Mood)).

## Step 1: Locating clauses

Since a sentence can have several clauses, each with its own subjects, verbs, objects, etc. it’s important to locate the boundaries between clauses within a sentence so that you can match the right verb with the right subject. *This is crucial for accurate translating.*

\[p. 116, §5.1\]

Each sentence consists of **independent** and **dependent** clauses.  A true sentence has at least one independent clause, and zero or more **dependent clauses**.  

* **Independent clauses** stand on their own: it must have a subject and verb, and must at the very least express a complete thought.  
* **Dependent clauses** have at least a subject and verb, but cannot stand alone as a sentence.
* **Relative clauses** begin with *who, which* or *that* but which isn't headed by a subordinate conjunction like (?)
* **Impersonal clauses** have no subject.

See [Conjunctions](#Conjunctions) for detailed informtion on how we join clauses to one another.

### Dependent clauses

Dependent clauses are often preceded by a **subordinating conjunction** (See [Subordinating conjunctions](Conjunctions.md#Subordinating%20conjunctions)] like *that, if, when, while, until, because, after, before, who, which, etc. * and have a recognizable subject and verb (at least).  

### Relative clauses

A **relative clause** is one that begins with *who, which* or *that* (See [Interrogative pronouns](Pronouns.md#Interrogative%20pronouns) and [Relative pronouns](Pronouns.md#Relative%20pronouns)) but which isn’t headed by a subordinating conjuction as such (?).  These are still dependent clauses and *who, which*  and *that* acts much like a subordinating conjunction \[p. 123, §5.1.1\]

### Impersonal clauses

Some clauses have no subject (from *The Story of Cædmon*):

*Ond hē for ðon nǣfre nōond his þā adjǣfestan tungan ġedeofanade singan.*

We could translate this as:

… and befitted his pious tongue to sing.

But, here, what is the subject?  It is impllied and it is *it*. So

… and \[it\] befitted his pious tongue to sing.

## Step 2: Finding the complete verb

Once you’ve located the clause boundaries and have chosen a clause to translate, start looking for that clause’s verb (See [Verbs](Verbs.md)).  In many cases, finding the verb should be fairly easy: **look for a verb with personal or tense endings (*\-eð, \-að, \-iað, \-ede, \-edon,* etc.)** Every **main** verb must have such “finite” endings.

**Word order is also helpful in locating the verb.**  In independent clauses, the verb usually prefers the **second position**. [^3]  \[...\]  The verb often gravitates to the second position even when the first word of the clause is not the subject but (let’s say) an adverb.

\[p. 126, §5.2\]

* Some verbs have only one part: **gangeð**  
* Some verbs have multiple parts (are *periphrastic*) like **ā-sungen hæfde** (had sung), or in ModE **want to analyze** (modal \+ infinitive); **have analyzed** (have \+ past participle), **had been analyzing** (have \+ past participle \+ present participle)  
* Note that not every clause has a subject; sometimes it has an implied subject.  E.g. *hafað* could be translated as “he/she/it has”.

### Having and being

***Bēon** (to be)*  (and also ***weorðan** \[to be, to become\]*) and ***habban** (to have)**,*** like in ModE, can be both verbs on their own (“I have a book”, “It is cold”) and also work as helping verbs requiring a participle to complete them (See [Anomolous verbs](Verbs.md#Anomolous%20verbs))

Thus when we see one of those verbs, it’s our job to determine whether they stand alone or whether they are part of a more complex verb structure.

* **On their own:**  *Hēr **is** fȳr miċel.* (Here is a great fire (*bēon)*); *Þēos stōw **hafað** nǣdran.* (This place has serpents (*habban*))  
* ***Habban*** **as a helper verb with a participle makes a “perfect[^4]” verb phrase (its action has completed)**:  *Hīe **habbað** mē tō hearran **ġe-corene**, rōfe rincas.* (They, the renowned warriors, have chosen me as their leader); *Ac hine sār **hafað** mid nyd-grīpe nearwe **be-fongen**.* (But sorrow has seized him tightly with a coercive grip.)  
* ***Bēon*** **with a participle makes a passive: *Is*** *mīn flet-werod, wīg-hēap **ġe-wanod**.* (My hall-troop, \[my\] warband is diminished); ***Wæs** þēah-hwæðere his martyrdōm **ġe-fremmed**.* (His martyrdom was nevertheless accomplished.)  
* ***Bēon***  **with a verb of motion functions like *habban*** **to produce a perfect:** *Nū **is** sē dæġ **cumen**.*  (Now the day has come); *Hē is ā-risen of dēaðe.* (He has risen from death).  
* ***Weorðan*****:** The verb *weorðan* “to be, become” (a strong verb we’ll learn more about later) can sometimes act exactly like ***bēon*** in that it takes a past participle: *Þā **wearð** sē **ġe-bolgen**.* (Then the mighty \[one\] was/became enraged).   Isaac: *weorðan*  is more commonly used when you want the passive sense.

### Modal auxiliary verbs

\[pp. 128-132, These are verbs like *can, will, shall, may, must, might,* etc..  Such verbs **must** have an infinitive to complete them (see [Preterite-Present Verbs](Verbs.md#Preterite-Present%20Verbs)): 

* *Hēo **wolde** hire mǣġ **wrecan**.* (She wanted to avenge her kinsman).    
* *Sē engel **on-gan** ofer mōd **wesan**.* (The angel began to be proud).  
* *Iċ ā-wyrged **sceal**, þēoden[^5], of syhðe þīnre **hweorfan**.* (I, accurse, must, lord, depart from your sight).

These are exactly the *preterite-present* verbs from pp. 97-100, §4.3.  Look on the **verbs** guide for them.

Called **modal** because they indicate the **mood** in which the action of the verbs take place (hope, desire, doubt, compulsion, etc), and **auxilliary** because they cannot stand on their own (unlike ***bēon, habban and weorðan***).  

**Rule:** if you find one of these verbs, you should immediately look for the infinitive (ending in *\-an, \-in \-anne,* or more rarely in *\-on*).  Though this can be tricky, and direct translations can lead us astray.  For example:

* *Sceal* usually means “must, have to”, not "shall" as you might think from the sound.
* *Wille* usually means “want, wish” rather than indicating the future (*she will go*) 
* *Mihte* does not indicate possibility (might) but usually means “can” (*Hēo mæġ gangan* “She is able to go”)

**Exceptions**

* The infinitive that usually accompanies a modal auxiliary verb can sometimes be left out **if it is a verb indicating motion** (*go, rush, fall, etc.*).  *Hēo **wolde** ūt þanon.* (She wanted \[to go\] out from there).  
* The “to be” verb (**bēon** or **weorðan**) can also sometimes be omitted usually when the main verb is a modal auxiliary verb: *Hæle sceal wīs-fæst ond ġe-metliċe, glēaw in ġe-hygdum.* (A warrior must \[be\] wise and moderate, keen in thought).

| OE verb | ModE | OE verb | ModE |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| *willan, wille, willaþ, wolde* | to wish, desire; shall will | *cunnan, cann, cunnon, cuðe* | can, be able; know how (to do something) |
| *nyllan, nylle, nyllaþ, nolde* | not wish, not desire  (= *ne “not” \+ willan*) | *\*\*\*\*, dear, durron, dorste* | to dare (to do something) |
| *\*\*\*\*, sceal, sculon, scolde* | ought to, must; shall | uton, wuton (1st person pres pl only) | let us (do something) |
| *magan, mæġ, magon, meahte/mihte* | can, be able |  |  |

### Compound verbs

Any sentence element may be “compounded” or doubled.  OE poetry prizes artful repetition, so every reader of OE needs to reckon with this.

* A scribe **reads** and **copies** books.  (compound verb – finite verb doubled)  
* A scribe **must read** and **copy** books. (compound verb – infinitive doubled)  
* The scribe **has read** and **copied** the books. (compound verb – past participles)  
* A **king** and **queen** must protect their warriors. (compound subject)

### Verbs of perception, command and permitting (accusative with infinitive)

With certain verbs of **perception** (*to see, to hear, to perceive, to discover),* of **commanding** and ordering (*to command, to bid, to order, to ask*), and of **permitting** (*to let, to allow, to permit*), OE often adds an infinitive construction \[...\] ModE has a similar construction: *I ask him to write a letter.*  “*him to write a letter”* is called an **infinitive phrase** and functions as the direct object of the verb *ask*.

Similarly, “*I saw her write the letter*”: “*her write the letter”* is also an infinitive phrase in which we have omitted the “to” – common in ModE after perception verbs.

In OE, the construction is similar:  *Iċ ġe-seah men gangan.*  “I saw men go”, but *gangan* (to go) is an infinitive.  *men* is the object of *ġe-seah*, but the subject of the infinitive *gangan.* You could also translate this as “I saw men going” in which *going* is the ModE present participle[^6].  Other examples:

* *Iċ ġe-hȳrde hine þine dǣd and word lofian.*  (I heard him praising your deed and word.  OR I heard him praise your deed and word).  
* *Hē þæt wīf ġe-seah on eorð-riċe stondan.*  (He saw the woman standing on the earthly kingdom. OR He saw the woman stand on the earthly kingdom).

**Commanding verb clauses** are somewhat touchy.   They also use the “accusative with infinitive” and typically begin with some form of the verb *hātan* “to command, order”.   These clauses come in two forms: those with **active** infinitives and those with **passive** infinitives.

### The “finding the verb” algorithm

1. If the finite verb consists of only one part, proceed to step 3\.  
2. If the finite verb is a *to have* or *to be* form (or a form of the verb *weorþan*), check the rest of the clause (especially the end) for a past participle.  If you find none, then translate the *to have* or *to be* form as the main verb and proceed to step 3\.  
3. If the fininte verb is a modal auxilliary, look for an infinitive.  If you cant find an infinitive, you must probably supply a verb of motion or a *to be* verb.  
4. If you can’t find a complete verb, check to see …

* if the verb is the simple past tense of a strong verb.  Past tense strong verbs in the singular have no endings and may look like nouns or other parts of speech.

| Past singular | Infinitive |
| :---- | :---- |
| *bær* | *beran* “to carry” |
| *sōc* | *sacan* “to quarrel” |
| *bōf* | *bebban* “to lift” |
| *bæd* | *bidden* “to ask; pray” |
| *cnēow* | *cnāwan* “to know” |

* if the verb (either the finite form or the modal auxilliary) carries over from the previous clause to this one.  
* if the verb is an implied but missing verb of motion or *to be* verb.

# Step 3: Finding the subject

1. Look for a noun or pronoun that is in the nominitive case that matches the number (singular or plural) of the “finite” verb.  Remember to use the accompanying articles to help you.  
2. If you can find no recognizable subject, it might be carried over from a previous sentence or clause, or  
3. it may be an “impersonal construction” \[§3.10.1.3, §11.1\]  
4. Finally, use context – there may be multiple nominitive nouns to choose from, so choose the one that most closely fits with the verb.
