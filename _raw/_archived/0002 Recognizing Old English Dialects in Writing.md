This lesson gives you a practical reading skill: when a written Old English form looks "off," you should be able to ask whether it is really a dialect clue rather than a mistake.

**Win for today:** by the end, you should be able to use a few high-value spelling cues to tell West Saxon from Anglian and Kentish in common cases, while knowing where Mercian and Northumbrian are harder to separate.

## 1. Where the Dialects Sit

Old English dialects are a **late internal layer** inside Old English, not separate branches from PIE or Proto-Germanic. Wright says early West Saxon is taken as the standard in his grammar, while Northumbrian, Mercian, and Kentish show clearly defined dialectal peculiarities in the oldest records.

|Dialect|Region|Quick reminder|
|---|---|---|
|West Saxon|South of the Thames, except Kent and Surrey|The usual grammar baseline because so much surviving prose is copied in West Saxon.|
|Mercian|Between the Humber and the Thames|Often grouped with Northumbrian as Anglian.|
|Northumbrian|Between the Firth of Forth and the Humber|Also Anglian; often preserves non-WS spellings.|
|Kentish|Kent and Surrey|Often shows fronted or unrounded spellings where WS keeps something else.|

**Important caution:** Wright also warns that copied texts often mix dialects. So dialect spotting is a matter of _probability from clusters of cues_, not one magic letter in isolation.

## 2. The Fastest Recognition Strategy

**Start with a West Saxon expectation:** many grammars quietly assume WS forms.

**Look for repeated non-WS vowel spellings:** especially `ea` versus `æ/e`, and `y/ie` versus `e`.

**Ask whether the form looks Anglian or Kentish:** Mercian and Northumbrian are often easier to identify first as "Anglian-like."

**Only then try to separate Mercian from Northumbrian:** that is the harder finer-grained step.

## 3. High-Value Cues with Attested Forms

**Cue A: West Saxon `ea` often appears as Anglian `æ` or `e`**

This is one of the most useful reading cues. Wright repeatedly notes Anglian spellings with `æ` where West Saxon has `ea`.

**Attested comparisons cited by Wright:** WS `ceaster` beside Anglian `cæster`; WS `geat` beside Anglian `gæt`; WS `sceal` beside Anglian `scæl`; WS `meaht` beside Anglian `mæht`; WS `weax` beside Anglian `wæx`.

**Working rule:** if you expected a familiar WS `ea`-form and instead see `æ`, think **Anglian** first.

**Cue B: Kentish often pushes further toward `e`**

Where WS has forms with `æ` or `ea`, Kentish often shows `e`. This is a good reason Kentish can look visually "flatter" or more fronted on the page.

**Attested comparisons cited by Wright:** WS `dæg` beside Kentish `deg`; WS `fæder` beside Kentish `feder`; WS `wæter` beside Kentish `weter`; WS `ceaster` beside Kentish `cester`; WS `geat` beside Kentish `get`; WS `sceal` beside Kentish `scel`.

**Working rule:** if a form looks like a known WS word but with Kentish `e` in place of WS `æ` or `ea`, Kentish is a strong candidate.

**Cue C: Anglian and Kentish often avoid West Saxon `ie/y`-type spellings**

Wright regularly notes that forms with WS `ie` or `y`-type outcomes correspond to simpler `e`-spellings in non-WS dialects.

**Attested comparisons cited by Wright:** WS `dierne` beside non-WS `derne`; WS `ierfe` beside non-WS `erfe`; WS `giest` beside non-WS `gest`; WS `ciele` beside non-WS `cele`; WS `mæht` later `mieht`-type developments beside Anglian/Kentish forms like `mæht` or later `meht`.

**Working rule:** if a textbook WS form with `ie` or `y` shows up in plainer-looking `e`-form, suspect a non-WS dialect.

**Cue D: Kentish sometimes keeps `wio-` where WS and Anglian have `wu-`**

This is a narrower cue, but when you see it, it is memorable.

**Attested comparison cited by Wright:** WS and Anglian `wudu` beside Kentish `wiodu`.

**Working rule:** if you see `wio-` where you expected `wu-`, Kentish should jump to mind.

**Cue E: Mercian has some special umlaut spellings**

Mercian is not always easy to isolate, but Wright does give forms where Mercian shows umlauted vowels absent from the other dialects.

**Attested comparisons cited by Wright:** Mercian `featu` beside other dialects `fatu`; Mercian `heafuc` beside other dialects `hafoc`; Mercian `fearan` beside other dialects `faran`.

**Working rule:** if a form shows a rounded or diphthongized vowel where plain WS would not, Mercian is worth considering.

**Cue F: Northumbrian can show distinctive spellings inside inherited `ea`-words**

Northumbrian is often easier to recognize from individually cited spellings than from one giant rule.

**Attested comparisons cited by Wright:** Nth. `eorm` beside WS `earm`; Nth. `heord` beside WS `heard`; Nth. `æhher` beside WS `ēar`; Nth. `tæhher` beside WS `tēar`.

**Working rule:** when a word that you know from WS turns up with unusual Northumbrian vowel spelling and doubled `hh`/`hher`-type writing, Northumbrian is a good possibility.

## 4. The Most Important Big Picture

You do not always need to identify the exact dialect on first sight. Often the real skill is just this: **"This is not ordinary West Saxon, so I should stop treating it as a spelling error and start treating it as dialect evidence."**

|If you see...|Think first...|
|---|---|
|WS-looking `ea` replaced by `æ`|Anglian|
|WS `æ` or `ea` flattened to `e`|Kentish|
|WS `ie/y`-type form replaced by `e`|non-WS, often Anglian or Kentish|
|`wiodu`-type spelling|Kentish|
|`featu`, `heafuc`-type spellings|Mercian|
|`eorm`, `æhher`-type spellings|Northumbrian|

## 4a. Why One Text May Show Mixed Dialect Spellings

Your instinct here is a good one. Mixed spelling inside one Old English text can absolutely reflect **multiple scribes**, but it can also reflect **multiple copying stages** even when only one surviving hand is visible on the page.

Wright explicitly warns that so-called phonological irregularities often arise because copyists introduce forms from their own dialect, or because in transcribing a manuscript from one dialect into another they leave some older dialect forms behind. He also notes that much Old English poetry was originally Anglian but survives mainly in later West Saxon copies containing many Anglian forms.

|Possible cause|What it can look like in practice|
|---|---|
|More than one scribe|Spelling habits shift partway through a text, or different sections show different preferred forms.|
|One scribe copying a mixed exemplar|The same hand preserves forms from different older dialect layers because the source was already mixed.|
|Partial normalization|A scribe updates some forms into West Saxon but leaves others in Anglian or Kentish-looking shapes.|
|Archaism plus updating|Older forms survive in formulaic or copied passages while surrounding spellings look later or more normalized.|

**Best practical rule:** mixed spellings are real evidence, but not automatic proof of multiple hands. A single scribe can be inconsistent, especially when copying older material. So the right question is usually not "Did more than one scribe work on this?" but "What combination of **scribal habit, exemplar history, and dialect mixing** best explains what I am seeing?"
