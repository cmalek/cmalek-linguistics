*The Lord of the Rings* is medieval fantasy because Tolkien was a medievalist, and that really shows if you know what to look for. Tolkien was a professor of Old English at Oxford, and he uses a lot of Old English names and conventions in his writing.

The Rohirrim are basically the Anglo-Saxons with cavalry, and they straight-up speak Old English. (Or really, they speak Rohirric, which our good Professor Tolkien “renders” as Old English in his “translation,” because it’s an archaic form of Westron.) So, all the Rohirrim have Old English names. “Theoden” *(þeoden)* is one of many words meaning “lord” or “ruler,” so “Theoden King” means “king king.” (It’s also “Theoden King” and not “King Theoden” because that’s the convention in Old English.) His hall is called Meduseld, which just means “mead hall.”

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1204/format:webp/0*I7mD0LhuYxSewtIT)

Éomer is a real Old English name meaning “horse-famous,” and it appears briefly in *Beowulf*. Éowyn likewise means “horse-joy.”

The *“Where now the horse and the rider?”* lament of the Rohirrim is almost a direct quote from the Old English poem known as “The Wanderer”:

> *Where has the horse gone? where is the rider? where is the giver of gold?  
> Where are the seats of the feast? where are the joys of the hall?  
> O the bright cup! O the brave warrior!  
> O the glory of princes! How the time passed away,  
> slipped into nightfall as if it had never been!*

It follows a poetic motif called *“ubi sunt?”* which is Latin for “where are they?”

People usually think that “Saruman” is a play on “Sauron” or something, because they sound similar, but they’re actually unrelated. “Sauron” is in Tolkien’s invented Sindarin, and he already existed as a character pre-LotR. “Saruman” is actually Old English and means, “skilled person.” “Orthanc” *(orþanc)* is also Old English, and means “cunning,” “skill,” or “creativity.” (But it somehow sounds like the name of an evil tower, right??) “Isengard” means, roughly, “steel place.”

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1204/format:webp/0*SZ2e6Ae3gmdBDGMz)

It makes sense that Isengard and Orthanc (and Saruman himself) would have Old English names, since they’re so close to Rohan.

Other medieval Germanic languages have much less presence in *LotR*, but it’s worth pointing out that almost all the Dwarf names from *The Hobbit* are Old Norse. They come straight from the *Poetic Edda,* one of the main sources for Norse mythology:

> *There was Motsognir the mightiest made  
> Of all the dwarfs, and Durin next;  
> Many a likeness of men they made,  
> The dwarfs in the earth, as Durin said.*
> 
> *Nyi and Nithi, Northri and Suthri,  
> Austri and Vestri, Althjof, Dvalin,  
> Nar and Nain, Niping, Dain,  
> Bifur, Bofur, Bombur, Nori,  
> An and Onar, Ai, Mjothvitnir.*
> 
> *Vigg and Gandalf, Vindalf, Thrain,  
> Thekk and Thorin, Thror, Vit and Lit,  
> Nyr and Nyrath, — now have I told —  
> Regin and Rathsvith — the list aright.*
> 
> *Fili, Kili, Fundin, Nali,  
> Heptifili, Hannar, Sviur,  
> Frar, Hornbori, Fræg and Loni,  
> Aurvang, Jari, Eikinskjaldi.*
> 
> *The race of the dwarfs in Dvalin’s throng  
> Down to Lofar the list must I tell;  
> The rocks they left, and through wet lands  
> They sought a home in the fields of sand.*
> 
> *There were Draupnir and Dolgthrasir,  
> Hor, Haugspori, Hlevang, Gloin,  
> Dori, Ori, Duf, Andvari,  
> Skirfir, Virfir, Skafith, Ai.*
> 
> *— Volupsa 10–15*

(And here I thought that names like Nori and Ori were just meant to sound silly in a kids’ book.)

Finally, the name “Middle-earth” itself comes from Old English *middangeard.* That’s the Anglo-Saxon variant of Midgard. It’s called “middle earth” because it’s between the sky and the underworld.
