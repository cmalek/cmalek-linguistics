
A short working glossary for the sound labels that appear all the time in historical grammar, even when your main goal is reading Old English rather than studying phonetics for its own sake.

## Core Types

|Term|Plain meaning|Why you keep seeing it|
|---|---|---|
|Vowel|A sound with relatively open airflow.|Many sound changes target stressed versus unstressed vowels, front versus back vowels, or long versus short vowels.|
|Consonant|A sound with greater obstruction in the mouth.|Most named laws describe changes in consonant classes.|
|Stop|A consonant with full closure and release.|Grimm's Law is often described in terms of stops shifting.|
|Fricative|A consonant with friction from narrow airflow.|Very common in Germanic sound laws: `f, þ, h, s`.|
|Sibilant|A hissy or hushing fricative.|Useful when reading discussions of `s`-like sounds and their behavior.|
|Nasal|A sound with airflow through the nose.|Often relevant in assimilation and cluster changes.|
|Liquid|Traditional class including `l` and `r`.|Liquids often trigger or block nearby vowel changes.|
|Glide|A very vowel-like consonant, especially `j` and `w`.|Important for i-mutation and other palatal or labiovelar developments. In Proto-Germanic and Gothic, `j` is usually the `y`-sound [j], like the start of English `yes` or German `ja`.|

## Voicing and Breath

|Term|Plain meaning|Rough cue|
|---|---|---|
|Voiced|The vocal folds vibrate.|Put a hand on your throat for `z` or `d`.|
|Voiceless|The vocal folds do not vibrate.|Compare `s` or `t`.|
|Aspirated|Released with a noticeable puff of breath.|English `p` in `pin` is more aspirated than `p` in `spin`.|
|Unaspirated|Released without that strong puff.|Common contrast in phonetic descriptions of older stop systems.|

## Place Words

|Term|Plain meaning|Example area|
|---|---|---|
|Labial|Made with the lips.|`p, b, m, w`|
|Dental|Made at or near the teeth.|`þ, ð`|
|Alveolar|Made at the ridge behind the teeth.|`t, d, n, s, l, r`|
|Velar|Made with the back of the tongue against the soft palate.|`k, g`|
|Palatal|Made with the tongue toward the hard palate.|`j`, or palatalized consonants|
|Palatalized|Still basically another consonant, but pronounced with a secondary `y`-like coloring or movement toward the palate.|Useful for understanding OE `ċ`, `ġ`, and many historical front-vowel effects.|

## Change Words

|Term|Plain meaning|
|---|---|
|Ablaut|An inherited vowel alternation inside the same word-family or paradigm, not caused by a nearby sound in the current form. Strong-verb patterns and pairs like `fōt` beside the older background of `fēt` are where you keep meeting it.|
|Assimilation|One sound becomes more like a nearby sound.|
|Dissimilation|One sound becomes less like a nearby sound.|
|Syncope|Loss of an unstressed vowel inside a word.|
|Apocope|Loss of a final sound or syllable.|
|Gemination|Consonant lengthening.|
|Lenite / lenition|To weaken a sound, or the process of weakening. A stop may become a fricative, a fricative may become weaker still, or a consonant may disappear altogether.|
|Lenition|A sound becomes weaker or less obstructed.|
|Fortition|A sound becomes stronger or more obstructed.|
|Mutation / umlaut|A vowel changes because of a vowel or glide in the next syllable. Often that trigger belonged to an older ending that later disappeared, which is why attested pairs like `mann ~ menn` and `fōt ~ fēt` can look puzzling at first. In this context `j` means the glide [j], while `i` is the high front vowel [i].|
|Palatalization|A consonant shifts toward a palatal or palatalized pronunciation, often because of a nearby front vowel or `j`.|

## Two Reading Tips

1. If a historical explanation feels opaque, ask first whether the hard word is naming a _sound class_, a _place of articulation_, or a _type of change_.
2. In older philological writing, terms may be a little traditional or broad. The goal is fast recognition, not perfect modern phonetics on day one.
